## Lecture 01 — Introduction to Logistics: Design and Management

### 1.1 Logistics vs. Supply Chain (the bread example)
- The professor explains the whole idea using the **bread** example (bread and butter as breakfast/short meal).
- Ingredients of bread: **wheat (flour), salt, sugar, yeast, etc.**
- Trace back the chain:
  - **Wheat/Flour** comes from **flour mills**.
  - Flour mills get raw material (wheat grain) from **farmers**.
  - Farmers use **seeds, fertilizers, machines, pesticides** to grow crops.
  - The farmer uses a **tractor** (e.g., from **Mahindra and Mahindra**).
  - Mahindra & Mahindra's tractor has many suppliers; one raw material is a **tire**.
  - Tire is made of **rubber and steel wires**; steel wires come from the **steel industry**.
  - Steel industry takes raw material from **mining**, and the chain keeps growing.
- **Conclusion:** Supply chain management is a **systematic approach** — the whole chain is considered as **one system**, and losses or profits are **shared through all supply chain stakeholders**.
- **Where logistics comes in:** at every point where material is **transported** from one stakeholder to another, and wherever grain is **stored** in warehouses/storage houses. It also includes managing **information**.
- **Key statement:** *Logistics function is a subset of supply chain.*

### 1.2 Why Logistics is Important (cost share)
- In total supply chain cost, **50% comes from transportation** (part of logistics).
- Adding **inventory carrying cost**, roughly **70%** comes from these two components.
- Roughly **60–70% of supply chain cost is because of logistics.**
- Breakdown of logistics cost:
  - **Transportation — 58%** (maximum share).
  - **Inventory carrying cost** — next.
  - **Warehousing** — next (strategic storage locations before distribution to end customer).
  - **Administrative cost** — related components.
- **Road transport** carries the maximum share of material — a limitation, because of delays (road condition, accidents, weather), documentation, and rigid tax structure at state borders (long queues of trucks at borders).

### 1.3 Evolution of Logistics
- Started as **local view** — barter within a community (no common currency).
- Moved to **regional view** — access to products/services nearby (bull cart, horse cart).
- Then **national** — e.g., apples produced in the hills distributed to every part of the country (road, trains, ships).
- Then **global** — one-click purchase from anywhere (e.g., Alibaba in China, US market); sourcing raw material across borders for better price, quality, quantity.
- The professor plays a video ("Keep Up with the Clicks") showing e-tailers evolving to keep up with online clicks — anywhere, any zone, 24 hours, doorstep delivery, returns.

### 1.4 What is Logistics?
- **Logistics** = the **management of flow of goods** (raw material, sub-assembled parts, or final product) from one end to another.
- Also includes flow of:
  - **Information** — demand pattern, customer expectations, preferences, quantity, quality.
  - **Resources** — e.g., moving machines and **manpower** from one project site to another (man, money, machine, material, technology).
- Not only **forward flow** but also **reverse flow** (e.g., with one more click, product returns).
- Range of activities: **planning, implementing, controlling** the **efficient movement and storage of goods**.

### 1.5 Seven R's of Logistics
1. **Right product**
2. **Right quantity** — ice cream parlour example: on a normal campus day 1,000–1,500 ice creams; on placements/birthday occasions demand may be 3k, 5k or 6k, so the network must be **flexible** and should **not say no** to the customer.
3. **Right time** — packet must arrive before the promised/occasion time.
4. **Right condition** — Zomato/Swiggy: dinner should feel like it came straight from the frying pan to the dining table.
5. **Right place** — channels of placing the product.
6. **Right customer** — identified through right information.
7. **Right price/cost** — must remain **efficient**; cannot raise price claiming better services. Example: **Swiggy's USP** — you can order even a **single piece of sweet**, with **no minimum order**, 30-minute delivery (even 10 km away), so one order cannot be combined for economies of scale.

### 1.6 Key Elements of Logistics (five elements)
1. **Transportation** — all modes, **intermodal transportation** for fastest and efficient delivery.
2. **Warehousing** — storing product before delivering final product (to meet unexpected rise or fall in demand).
3. **Inventory control** — excess inventory is dangerous, and being below safety stock is dangerous; **JIT cannot be implemented for all components**.
4. **Order fulfillment** — customer places order on Zomato → routed to restaurant → preparing/packaging → delivery partner picks packet → shipped.
5. **Distribution** — designing the network; one extreme is doorstep delivery via click, the other is visiting the retail showroom to see/feel the product. Must be open to **all channels**.

### 1.7 Ultimate Aim
- Deliver the **correct products to the right location at the appropriate time** while **minimizing expenses** and **maximizing effectiveness**.
- Competent logistics management is vital for meeting customer needs, enhancing operational efficiency, and sustaining as a competitor.

## Lecture 02 — Logistics Stakeholders and Modern Logistics Concepts

### 2.1 Stakeholders in Logistics
- **Suppliers** — multiple layers ("suppliers of suppliers"), as in the bread example ending at mining.
  - Negotiate on **product, quantity, price, delivery**.
  - **Single vs. multi-supplier debate:** too many suppliers is a problem; only one supplier is also a problem (disaster natural/manmade, equipment failure, strike).
  - Professor's advice: keep **2–3 or 4 vendors** depending on criticality; for frequently troubled or scarce raw material up to **5–7**; **too many vendors** is hard to retain (small order quantities weaken price negotiation and economies of scale).
- **Manufacturers** — final assembly/production line converting raw material into **finished goods (FG)**. Multi-layer too (co-manufacturers, sub-assembly makers).
  - Example: manufacturing an **AC** but not good at handling electronic components (motherboard, programming control array) → outsource to a **co-manufacturer** who supplies the sub-assembly motherboard to fix in the AC casing.
- **Retail industry** — organized retail and **fragmented retail** (small shops). In India, fragmented retail shops carry the **maximum share**; must meet demand of both.
- **Transportation** — contributes ~50–55% of cost; modes: air, road, ship, rail.
  - Mode choice depends on: **availability of infrastructure, cost, sustainability, reliability, speed of delivery, customer requirements**.
  - **Road** takes the maximum share but is inefficient (fuel cost, delays, road condition, accidents, weather, documentation, tax structure at state borders).
- **Distribution network** — customer visits retail shop vs. doorstep delivery; whichever model the industry offers, must be competitive.
- **Order placement** — customer places order via website/portal/interactive platform.
- **Inventory management** — avoid stock-out and excess inventory. Inventory depletes as demand is met; at the **safety stock** level, place a replenishment order; cycle continues.
- **Warehousing** — strategic location decision; how many warehouses; locating near final customer aids speed but increases cost (5 warehouses vs. 1 → more manpower, instruments, infrastructure, inventory). It is a **long-term decision** (fixed-asset investment), cannot be withdrawn overnight.
- **Picking and packing** — order picked from inventory; distribution partner delivers.
- **Transportation** of packed orders from warehouses to destination.
- **Last-mile delivery** — availability check by **pin code** (Amazon/Flipkart). E-commerce players first collaborated with players like the **Indian post office** (present in almost all pin codes) then built their **own networks** for control over speed/quality.
- **Order tracking** — customers want to know: accepted, processed, dispatched, where it is now. Traditional post/speed-post tracking cannot match e-commerce tracking.
- **Customer service / returns** — three reasons:
  1. Customer simply **did not like the product** (one click return — a big challenge).
  2. Product not functioning / under **warranty or guarantee** — spare parts, pick-up, repair, engineer visit.
  3. **Sustainability / end of life** — recycle, reuse, remanufacture; identify the few age-limited components (e.g., AC/refrigerator/fan: motor, capacitor) using the **80-20 rule** and replace those.
- **Feedback and improvement** — after every delivery the customer is asked whether delivery/food quality was okay; some parameters are beyond the delivery partner's control but as a complete system the feedback matters.

### 2.2 Logistics Management
- Planning, implementation, and control of the **movement and storage** of goods, services, and related information.
- Movement must be **efficient and effective**; then go for **optimization** of movement/flow of information.

### 2.3 Advantages of Effective Logistics Management
1. **Efficiency** — plans A, B, C reduce the impact of interruptions (natural disasters like floods/earthquakes, manmade disasters, shutdowns, COVID-19).
2. **Cost reduction** — optimize transportation route (best as per traffic, distance, road condition); warehouse operations and inventory levels.
3. **Automation** — future warehouses man-free, robots maintaining/updating inventory, maximum space utilization, automatic lifts, reduced manpower cost, 24×7 working.
4. **Customer satisfaction** — manual handling can damage products (electronic/fragile), damage cost, damaged products shipped to customer; efficient logistics → timely delivery, quality → loyal customers → **competitive edge**.
5. **Risk management** — safety stock avoids stock-outs and production-line stoppage (e.g., missing a small screw/glass/steering wheel/gearbox stops the whole line; shortfall at stage 15 interrupts all later stages).
6. **Resource optimization** — vehicles (proper routing), warehouse space (automation), manpower.
7. **Market expansion** — only possible if the **logistics distribution network** reaches that market. Example: foreign companies came to India; **Hero Honda** collaboration (Honda had two-wheeler technology, Hero had a vast distribution network covering almost all regions of India), and **Maruti Suzuki**.

### 2.4 Logistics Design and Management
- Strategic planning, coordination, and execution of processes to ensure efficient flow of goods, information, and resources from **point of origin to point of consumption**.
- Covers **inventory management, warehousing, transportation, order processing, distribution**.
- Design example: two manufacturing plants making the same model M1; raw material from supplier 1 goes to both plants (design the distribution network). Finished goods ship to warehouses (strategic location and capacity). Customer potential in each market decides which warehouse serves which location.
- Effective logistics design = **minimize cost, maximize customer satisfaction, take care of sustainability, mitigate risks**.
- Must analyze **demand patterns** to decide transportation mode and inventory levels at warehouses; continuous tracking/monitoring gives feedback for improvement.

### 2.5 Core Elements of Logistics Design and Management
- **Strategic planning** — long-term; facilities, warehouses, mode of transportation; align with business strategy (expansion).
- **Inventory management** — balance supply/demand through forecasting.
- **Warehouse management** — manage capacity efficiently, maximum utilization.
- **Transportation** — depends on infrastructure, mode, speed required, cost bearing capacity, quality level.
- **Order processing** — from order entry to last-mile delivery, minimizing cost.
- **Technology** — **RFID** (scan barcode, locate item, track depletion), **UAVs/Unmanned Aerial Vehicles** (airspace delivery within 30 minutes), **GPS** (monitor movement of goods).
- **Continuous improvement** — develop **KPIs**, work on them.

### 2.6 Modern Logistics Concepts (introduction)
1. **Lean logistics** — reduce waste: extra transportation, inventory, movement of material, over-processing, over-production, waiting time.
2. **Just-in-Time (JIT)** — decide for which items JIT is feasible. Small/cheap components can be stored; costly components (the 20% that add 80% of cost — 80/20 rule) may go for JIT. Requires aligning suppliers near the manufacturing unit and very accurate demand prediction.
3. **Agile logistics** — flexibility and responsiveness; **dynamic supply chains** fed by continuous information sharing; always have a **plan B** to grab opportunities.
4. **Green logistics** — minimize **carbon footprint**; regulatory authorities push green products/supply chain. Example: in **European/US markets, 12 nations' customers prefer** last-mile delivery partners using **electronic vehicles or cycles**.
5. **Collaborative logistics** — collaboration/cooperation via strong relationships and quick information sharing among all stakeholders (as in the bread supply chain).
6. **Omni-channel logistics** — sell through multiple platforms: **Indigo** sells via MakeMyTrip, Goibibo, ixigo, IRCTC, its own website, etc.; with **130 seats** per plane, meeting segregated demand from many channels requires a very strong information system and flexible fulfillment options.
7. **Reverse logistics** — returns (not liking the product, damaged/not working, or end-of-life recycling/remanufacturing/reuse), supporting a **circular economy**.
8. **Supply chain visibility** — latest technological interventions to give customers end-to-end tracking.

### 2.7 Conclusion
- Logistics design and management is a **dynamic field** requiring meticulous planning and execution for smooth flow of goods, information, and resources among stakeholders.

## Lecture 03 — Manufacturing Logistics: National and International Scenario

### 3.1 Manufacturing Logistics — Definition and Scope
- **Definition:** manufacturing logistics is **overseeing the materials, resources, and procedures integral to producing goods within a manufacturing setting**.
- **Scope:** it brings together **procurement, inventory control, scheduling, production, transportation management, and distribution of finished products** — everything that moves material into, through, and out of the plant.
- Applies to **tangible, physical products**; the **customer is not part of the manufacturing process** (unlike services, where the customer is an input to and part of the process).
- **Core aims:**
  - Ensure a **smooth and uninterrupted flow** of material through the chain.
  - Hold the **minimum inventory** while never falling below **safety stock** (avoid stock-out).
  - Produce the **right product in the right order and quantity**.
  - Keep **material handling to a minimum**.
- **Why manufacturing logistics specifically?** Manufacturing creates **tangible, storable products**, so inventory can buffer demand; services are **intangible and non-storable**, so the two need fundamentally different logistics.

### 3.2 Manufacturing vs. Services Logistics
- **Nature of output:** services are **intangible — not visualised, no physical shape**; manufactured products are **tangible — visualised and in a physical state**.
- **Storability:** services **cannot be stored**; products **can be produced and stored as inventory** for later demand.
  - Example: a **flight from Calcutta to Delhi with 10 vacant seats** — once it takes off, those 10 unutilized seats are lost forever.
  - Manufacturing: produce when demand is low, **store in inventory**, and **push the inventory** when demand rises.
- **What is managed:** in manufacturing we manage **inventory**; in services we manage the **queue**.
  - Bank/post-office with a long single-channel queue, or a barber shop with no customers in the first hour — that idle time is **unutilized capacity that can never be recovered**.
- **Customer's role:** in services the **customer is an integral part of the process** and continuously provides inputs (services are highly **customized**); in products the **customer is not part of the manufacturing process**.
- **Buffer:** manufacturing uses **inventory** as the buffer against demand swings; services have **no inventory management** and instead rely on **queue management**.

### 3.3 Architecture (Elements) of Manufacturing Logistics
The professor lists **eight elements** that make up the architecture of manufacturing logistics:

1. **Supply chain management** — ensures the **raw-material supply and other components**, including **sub-assembly parts**.
   - Chain: **supplier 1 → supplier 2 (processes the material) → manufacturing unit**; other components and raw materials arrive from **many different vendors**.
   - From the manufacturing unit, components are converted into **finished goods** and moved to the final customer through **distributors, wholesalers, carry-and-forward agents, and retailers** — a **long forward chain connecting the customer with the manufacturer**.
   - The **logistics component** ensures the **smooth flow** of raw material, sub-assemblies, and finished goods through this chain to the end customer.

2. **Inventory management** — manufacturing usually maintains **three types**:
   - **Raw material**, **semi-finished goods** (WIP on the production line, not fully complete), and **finished goods**; plus items from **rejections and repairs** coming back.
   - **Holding cost** example: a big **departmental store carrying ₹10 crore inventory at all times** (its assessed "safe inventory") → that money stays **unutilized forever**.
   - Yet inventory **cannot be reduced below the safety stock**, otherwise the firm may **stock out**. Inventory management balances holding cost against demand fulfilment.

3. **Production planning and scheduling** — answers the basic operations questions: **what** to produce, **where** to produce, **how much quantity**, **when**, and **in what order (sequence)**.
   - **Where:** **in-house vs. outsource**. Outsource when the firm lacks **expertise and economies of scale** or wants to **reduce initial investment**; produce in-house when there is enough **demand, technology, and expertise**.
   - **Quantity:** driven by the **demand pattern**.
   - **When and in what order:** defines **scheduling and sequencing**.
   - **Sequencing example:** models **A, B, C on the same line**; if a box packs **four items of each**, the final carton is not ready until four of each are produced — so they must be produced in **equal quantity** to match the **basket** designed by the marketing/production team.

4. **Quality control** — three segments:
   - **Incoming quality control (IQC)** — addresses **raw material** and even the **final packaging**; packaging is **equally important** because it carries the **logo/brand image** and must be **tough enough to bear wear and tear** through the supply chain.
   - **Process quality control** — ensures quality as raw material **moves along the production line**; e.g., down a line of **500 stages**, identify the ~**10 very critical, error-prone stages** and **define their parameters**.
   - **Outgoing quality control** — once the product is ready to ship, the **outgoing lot is inspected as per the sampling plan**; only if it is okay is it **distributed to the end market**.

5. **Material handling** — shifting raw material/sub-assembly **between stages** within the plant (including temporary storage).
   - **Objective: it should be minimum** — production lines should be designed so that handling is minimized.
   - Rationale: handling can **damage products** — e.g., **electronic components handled without anti-static wristbands**, and **fragile items** that may break across many stages.

6. **Transportation** — provides the **physical distribution** of products, connecting the **first supplier, the layers of suppliers in between, the manufacturer, and the end customer**.

7. **Warehousing** — done **before distributing the final product** to the end customer; it is **not only storage and handling** — **value-adding activities** are also performed in the warehouse (discussed later as a separate chapter).

8. **Reverse logistics** — arises when the **customer is unhappy, wants to return the product, or the item is defective**, or with **excess material** and **end-of-life** products.
   - The item is **recycled, reused, or remanufactured**, forming a **closed-loop supply chain** — a very important part of logistics.
   - After delivery, some products also need **installation**; the professor notes how goods are stored in between, grouped by **pin-code**, and finally **distributed as per customer requirement** — a quick review of how logistics flows through the supply chain.

### 3.4 Factors Contributing to the Development of Logistics
1. **Globalization** — to compete you need world-class facilities/services; efficient transportation, inventory, distribution network; minimum inventory while meeting customer needs; best **intermodal** combination. Foreign players (Amazon entering India) push local players (Flipkart) to compete.
2. **E-commerce growth** — reshaped customer expectations: short delivery times, adaptable fulfillment options (product, flavor, color), multiple payment options (COD, internet banking, credit/debit card, digital wallet).
3. **Supply chain collaboration** — collaboration among suppliers, manufacturers, distributors, logistics service providers; digital information sharing helps quick decision-making (information flows down the chain from distribution to supplier 2).
4. **Advanced technology** — **GPS, RFID, cloud computing, data analytics**. Two-way benefit:
   - Manufacturer analyzes buying patterns (e.g., checking a ticket price Calcutta–Delhi once → next moment related ads appear on social media) for customized services.
   - Customer is empowered (checks flights on MakeMyTrip, Goibibo, airline site, or aggregator like **ixigo** to pick best option).
5. **Infrastructure development** — ports, airports, railways, highways; governments work on these to boost the economy.
6. **Emerging markets** — need distribution network to reach the market; understand market dynamics (regional/cultural factors; same advertisement won't work in India and US); climate, cultural values, local regulatory obligations; SEZ (Special Economic Zones) vs. outside, interstate documentation and customs clearance.
7. **Sustainability initiatives** — green packaging, energy-efficient transportation (ships, electronic vehicles), renewable energy, fuel alternatives to petrol/diesel.
8. **Customer expectations** — never fully delightable (5% → 7% → 10%); minimum expectation: **fast delivery, accurate tracking, hassle-free returns**.
9. **Regulatory changes** — e.g., **GST**, state-to-state differences; environmental standards — **banning trucks older than 15 years** in Delhi/NCR during high pollution; **Supreme Court/government banning BS4 engines**.

### 3.5 Global Logistics Industry Scenario
- Logistics contributes ~**15–20% increase**; expected **>$14 trillion** economy contributing to world GDP.
- Country logistics contribution to GDP: **Indonesia 24%**, **Republic of Korea 7.8%** — India somewhere in between.
- Developed economies have **lower logistics cost** due to streamlined operations, no customs-clearance waiting, automated systems.
- **FASTag** in India — only ~**45–50%** paying through FASTag; a new technology takes at least a **decade** to fully commercialize.
- Industry size: worth over **8.4 trillion euros in 2021**, expected to exceed **13.7 trillion by 2027**.
- Total logistics cost = **10.7% of global GDP**.
- **Asia Pacific** accounts for the largest share of logistics cost (population, many deliveries).
- **Third-party logistics** — United States holds the position.
- Emerging markets include **India**; supply chain pressure index increasing due to e-commerce demand.

### 3.6 Indian Logistics Industry
- India is the **second fastest growing nation after China**; **CAGR 8.4%** (behind China).
- COVID-19 hit: **23.9% down**; recovering faster than expectations.
- Market size **2022: ~$274 billion**; projected by **2030: $563 billion**; annual growth rate **9.4%**.
- **Major challenge: logistics cost** — India ~**14%** vs. BRICS average of **11%**.
- **Efficiency challenge:** India uses ~**70–75% road** transportation vs. global average of ~**60%** (road contributes ~60% of total logistics cost globally).

### 3.7 Logistics Sector in India — Challenges
1. **High order intensity ratio** — huge population gives many orders (advantage), but also pain for the logistics manager (prioritizing; e.g., perishable medicines/vaccines requiring same-day or 30-minute delivery).
2. **Payment cycle** — how quickly payment flows from customer end back to the first supplier.
3. **Transportation road blocks** — 70% share by road; unpredictable accidents/landslides/weather; checkpoints, toll stations (streamline with single payment/document).
4. **Rail tariffs** — cost per km: **road ₹2.5**, **train ₹1.5**, **ship ₹0.25** (ship most efficient but infrastructure-limited). Air is **<1% globally** (costly; used for emergency/high priority).
5. **Port and shipping problems** — longest time but sustainable and low cost; infrastructure/depth limitations prevent large vessels; end-to-end connectivity issues.
6. **Lack of skilled and specialist personnel** — packaging is the "first face"; training cost, labor turnover.
7. **Slow transition into newer technology** — initial setup cost high; fear of not recovering investment; importing expertise to train workforce is time-consuming and costly. Japanese network structure is fast/strong (competitive edge).
8. **Warehousing and taxation discrepancies** — poor warehousing → damages/leakages; cold storage lacking for perishables; multiple tax layers.
9. **Competition with global giants** — short supply cycles, latest technology. **Flipkart** carries ~48–50% share (backed by **Walmart**, claiming 60–70%); Amazon tough competitor.
10. **Customer mindset** — high expectations, variety, quality, short lead time.
11. **Growing customer demands** — Amazon internalized/streamlined its entire logistics process.
12. **Fuel economics** — base price ~₹30, after layer-over-layer taxes ~₹87, now reaching ~₹100; beyond control; pushes down margins. Alternatives: last-mile via electronic vehicles/cycles.
13. **Government policies and bottlenecks** — FASTag, one document, red-carpet treatment for investment.
14. **Shortage of drivers and delivery staff** — low motivation/compensation; empower them (e.g., same driver also does delivery/value-adding) to retain them.

### 3.8 Conclusion
- Manufacturing logistics deals with the product: controlling inventory cost, deciding production order/quantity, managing distribution and transportation; storage is important.
- In services, there is **queue management** (no inventory management).
- Major forces: **globalization, e-commerce innovations (30-minute delivery promises), technological investment, infrastructure, sustainability** (e.g., **Myntra** writing "100% recyclable" paper packaging).

## Lecture 04 — Storage and Warehousing

### 4.1 Storage — Definition
- The process of **storing goods, materials, and products at various points along the supply chain** from raw material procurement to finished goods distribution.
- Effective storage management ensures smooth flow of material, optimizes inventory levels, and meets customer demand.
- Can store raw material, sub-assemblies, or finished goods; even after dispatch down the supply chain before final customer distribution.

### 4.2 Dark Stores / Ghost Stores
- Players like **Blinkit, Zepto** promise **10-minute delivery**.
- A **dark store** in Delhi, Mumbai, Bengaluru:
  - Delhi store — routine household items.
  - Bengaluru/Mumbai — small store dealing with **perishable items** (fruits/vegetables).
- Features: **need not brand** the store; take a facility **outside the city** for lower price/strategic location; record orders via **omni-channel**; process orders in shifts (morning orders delivered by evening, evening orders processed in night/second shift, delivered early morning).
- Advantages: save time interacting with customers; minimize costs (no AC/cooling facility required, less lighting); more space between racks for specialized trained workers to optimally use space.

### 4.3 Key Elements of Storage
1. **Customer requirement (7 R's link)** — interact, analyze behavior pattern, right offering: local taste, quantity, flavors, shape, size, weight, frequency → manage product category.
2. **Manage suppliers** — suppliers also store goods; for a supplier, our raw material is their finished product; storage houses in between.
3. **Right place** — place product to customer using logistics and warehouse management (distribution centers/fulfillment centers); replenishment cycle based on forecasting demand/behavior/buying patterns.
4. **Managing within the store** — channelizing inventory, keeping products on shelves, ensuring product doesn't go obsolete, moving the right product first, handling expiry and returns.
   - **Milk supplier example:** predicts ~**1,000 liters/day**; stores packets (1L, 2L, half-litre) in refrigerator. If he runs out and says no to a customer, the customer carries the perception of non-availability. Next day he increases inventory. If he only consumed **900 liters**, the leftover **100 liters** is returned through the **reverse supply chain**, processed and delivered the next day. **Product availability is the major focus**; never say no to the customer.

### 4.4 Components of Storage
1. **Warehousing** — storage hubs where goods are housed prior to distribution. Usually outsourced to **third-party logistics**. Equipped with shelving, racks, pallets (auto pallets). Now replacing manpower with **robots** for efficient handling; move inventory first so nothing goes outdated; information stored digitally, electronic records shared.
2. **Inventory management** — store finished goods, plus raw materials/sub-assemblies. Ensure sufficient stock availability while minimizing holding cost; avoid obsolete inventory; design replenishment cycle.
3. **Storage facilities** — depend on size, shape, nature (liquid, gas, hard product); **cold storage** for perishables; **leak-proof** for hazardous items; specifications tailored to requirements.
4. **Storage equipment** — pallet racks, shelving units, mezzanine floors, forklifts (automatic), conveyors, tugs, **Automated Storage and Retrieval System (AS/RS)**.
   - Softwares: **Transportation Management System (TMS)**, **Warehouse Management System (WMS)**.
5. **Safety and security** — hazardous material access limited; protection from damage/load issues; fragile items; surveillance cameras, access control, alarms, physical barriers. Military/defense warehouses need specific infrastructure.
6. **Material handling** — movement of material/goods from one location to another; term usually used **within an organization/manufacturing unit**.
   - On production lines (assembly lines, workstations); PPC (Production Planning and Control) issues a **production kit** to the shop floor.
   - Before production: incoming material → **IQC** checks per sampling plan → reject (return to vendor) or accept → store for 5/10 days (safety stock).
   - **Damage example:** if 5 tyres are required per car and we have 50 tyres → should make **10 cars**; shouldn't lose tyres in handling. **Glass example:** storing 50 units, but ending with **45 units** because 5 were damaged in material handling.
   - Because manual handling damages products, big houses automate storage centers with robots.

### 4.5 Warehouse — Definition & Strategic Location
- A **commercial facility** designed for storage of goods/materials/products; functions as a **centralized hub**.
- Strategic decision: **location and capacity**.
  - If you can fully utilize the capacity and demand is there, one facility enjoys **economies of scale**.
  - If you move **near the customer**, you are more responsive (e.g., a location only **10 km** away vs. routing to a centralized location **50 km** away; responsiveness decreases at 50 km).
  - Trade-off: **responsiveness** (many facilities near customers, higher inventory carrying cost, more material handling damages) vs. **efficiency** (one centralized facility).
- Warehouses integrate logistics and inventory management systems to monitor inventory, expedite order fulfillment, optimize flow of goods in/out.

### 4.6 Warehouse Layout Design — Four Factors
1. **Flow** — product flow (inward journey, outbound logistics); depends on **frequency** of shifting/transporting, **size, shape, weight, nature (fragile)**. Store heavy/hard-to-move/fragile products at easily accessible locations.
2. **Accessibility** — connected with automation: if manual, need space for people/trolleys; with robots/automatic forklifts/automated tugs, less space is needed.
3. **Space** — with automation, space can be managed well; with human involvement, efficiency is lower.
4. **Throughput rate** — units processed per interval; ties to frequency. Example: a **4-wheeler car** with petrol version (base, middle, top, automatic), each in 4–5 colors (5×5=25), different engine capacities → multiply models (e.g., 50 petrol + 50 diesel + 50 CNG).

### 4.7 Seven Steps to Plan Warehouse Layout
1. Define the **purpose** of the warehouse (shape, size, weight, nature of product).
2. Define the **warehouse diagram** / available space.
3. Define **storage requirements** (liquid/solid/gaseous phase).
4. Define **material handling equipment** needed (big equipment or small robots).
5. **Map it out and optimize**.
6. **Test traffic flow** (check for traffic jams).
7. Arrive at the final **layout**.

### 4.8 Layout Types
- **U-shape** — enter one end, exit the other end; most commonly used; space uniformly designed.
- **I-shape** — maximum space; good for **high-volume products**.
- **L-shape** — frequently needed items kept near entry/exit; rarely needed items at the back.
- Choice depends on needs and available land. Warehouse elements: unloading cargo, loading cargo, equipment placement, cargo arrangement.

### 4.9 Utility of Warehouses
1. **Product consolidation** — combine small orders going to one location into one order → one transportation mean → reduces transportation cost, enhances distribution efficiency.
2. **Cross docking** — a facility where trucks come from different directions; shipments going to the same market are combined.
   - Example: factory 1, factory 2, factory 3 serve market 1, market 2, market 3. All shipments to market 1 are combined and sent as one; same for market 3. It is a small stoppage/redistribution center with **no inventory**.
3. **Value-adding services** — promotional activities/free products kitting; **labeling** in warehouses (e.g., **Ekart/Amazon/Flipkart** label products in their centers, or the seller uses the Amazon package directly).
4. **Seasonal storage** — manufacture and store in off-season; e.g., **woolen clothes** manufactured in summer for demand in September/October/November in hilly areas; **AC/coolers/fans** demand in summer, prepared in December/January. Prevents stock-outs and handles over-stocks.
5. **Inventory management** — balance supply and demand; three types (raw material, sub-assembly, finished goods).
6. **Strategic location** — near customer = responsive but higher cost (managing 10 facilities vs. 1).
7. **Risk mitigation** — buffer stock for disruptions (natural/mannned disasters, COVID-19, strikes, **farmer agitation** in the northern part causing longer alternate routes).

### 4.10 Cross Docking — Details
- Example recap: three manufacturers; market 1 requires all three products → combine at cross-dock and make **one delivery** instead of three.
- **Advantages:**
  - JIT inventory (available when required quickly).
  - Optimized transportation (routes, vehicle utilization).
  - Reduced inventory handling cost (no storage houses; just reshuffling/reordering).
  - Improved efficiency (no inventory cost, maximum vehicle capacity, minimum handling).
  - Faster transit time (one delivery).
  - Easy to integrate and track.
- **Challenges:**
  1. **Customer-supplier relationship** — aggregate demand from many customers to combine; requires a digital network sharing electronic records with no gap between requirement and supply.
  2. **Quality control** — no storage means no quality check at the dock; direct shipping.
  3. **Coordination and synchronization** — top-level integration of customer and first supplier; needs technological infrastructure.
- **Impact:** enhanced customer service (one delivery instead of many, e.g., Amazon/Flipkart delivery person visiting 2/3/5 times a day is irritating), transportation cost saving, improved efficiency, reduced transit time, overall supply chain performance, cost saving, competitive edge (share extra margin with stakeholders/customer or keep as profit).

### 4.11 Cross Docking — Case Studies
1. **Amazon** — one centralized facility in **Germany** covering whole of **Europe within one day** using cross docking; massive investment in information channels; high-tech cross docking centers compiling code-wise/pin-code-wise; done by **robots** (not manual).
2. **Roche Diagnostics** — a **Switzerland** pharmaceutical company, world-famous in the **vaccine industry**; perishable product, same-day delivery; uses cross docking to maintain the exact temperature and avoid cold-storage temperature fluctuation affecting vaccine quality.
3. **Mainfreight** — a well-known firm in **New Zealand**; offers cross docking services only (no product of its own); consolidates demand from segregated markets and makes efficient deliveries. Has a **service basket** for perishables, retail/routine products, and constant-demand products.

### 4.12 Conclusion
- Effective storage management ensures continuous flow of material; temporary storage is required before distribution to end customer; can do value-adding activities (packaging, labeling); for constant demand from one market, **cross docking** minimizes cost and increases efficiency.

## Lecture 05 — Logistics Services Providers

### 5.1 Logistics Service Provider (LSP)
- A company/organization offering a range of logistics solutions: transportation, warehousing, storage, distribution, management of goods/materials, reverse supply chain.
- Reasons companies outsource: not good at distribution setup, lack resources/expertise/access to market.

### 5.2 Generic Structure & Restaurant Example
- Supply chain: suppliers (of suppliers) → manufacturers → long distribution chain (wholesalers, distributors, retailers, carry-forward agents) → end customer.
- Need transportation between points, storage, packaging/labeling, fulfillment/distribution centers; returns handling also needs warehouses (repair returns, replace faulty components, repackage and re-shelf).
- **Restaurant example** (near/within IIT Kharagpur campus, 18–20 thousand people):
  - 15–20 people work in the restaurant; only capacity utilized is whoever visits.
  - For doorstep delivery of 100–120 orders/day, need **5 bikes and 5 riders** → fixed investment of **₹7–8 lakhs** — huge for a small restaurant.
  - Solution: collaborate with **Swiggy/Zomato** to avoid fixed cost.
  - To expand beyond 3–5 km to 15–20 km: procure more raw material; distribution handled by delivery partner (Swiggy/Zomato/local player) if not deploying own dedicated network.

### 5.3 Key Components of Services Offered by LSPs
1. **Transportation** — LSP decides mode (road/air/rail/ship).
2. **Freight forwarding** — moving stock from one point to another.
3. **Customs brokerage** — customs duties, documentation for overseas/interstate movement.
4. **Inventory management** — forecasting, demand patterns, arranging raw material inventory, order fulfillment (customized/tailored).
5. **Packaging and labeling**.
6. **Technological solutions** — tracking goods, final delivery, payment cycle; software platforms.
7. **Customization** — solutions depending on location, volume, special handling (fragile items, electronic components, food hygiene).
8. **Expertise and experience** — dedicated international players; combining 2–4 orders to improve efficiency (same cost whether carrying 1 or 10 packets).
9. **Global reach** — access to international markets; access to overseas suppliers/vendors for better quality/cheaper/on-time material.
10. **Technological integration** — WMS, GPS, RFID packaging, cloud-based platforms → transparency in the supply chain; track inventory on shelf even.
11. **Risk management** — LSP carries freight; insurance; diversion of vehicles; absorb disruption (if stuck on road due to weather/natural disaster, outsourced warehousing maintains inventory).
12. **Cost efficiency** — avoid fixed cost; share some profit with third party; maximize reach/market share.
13. **Continuous improvement** — LSPs already track KPIs/performance matrix.

### 5.4 Reasons for Outsourcing Logistics
1. **Avoid fixed capital expenditure** — e.g., bikes, 10 more manpower, order platform, bigger raw material storage; risk of assets if expansion fails.
2. **Cut cost** — uncertain demand; economies of scale; whether resources are fully utilized (3 hours/day use of 10 bikes/manpower).
3. **Increase flexibility** — e.g., IIT campus population drops from 18–20k to **2,000–3,000** during summer break; outsourced partner charges for actual orders (100 → 1000 orders).
4. **Focus on core business** — restaurant is good at cooking; don't go into transportation.
5. **Improve services** — specialist players' service level cannot be matched.
6. **Lack of scale** — sudden population reduction problem.
7. **Acquired talent** — specialists have the expertise/right talent.
8. **Labor-related problems** — everyday strikes/union issues in the transportation industry.
- **Survey (Inbound Logistics):** most outsourced functions are non-core/non-USP/routine activities, not **strategic or customer-facing** (which the company wants to control directly).
- **Share of outsourcing:** domestic transportation **83%**, warehousing **66%**, internal transportation **63%**, customs brokerage **46%**, inventory management **17%**.

### 5.5 Types of Logistics Providers (Party Logistics)
- **First Party Logistics (1PL):** only two parties — producer and receiver. Example: a **farmer delivering eggs to a grocery store**. The supplier manages its own inventory, storage, and delivery.
  - Components: **ownership and control** (full integration, quality control, but outsourcing to another unit means less than 100% control); **in-house operation** (own fleets, warehouses → fixed investment); **vertical integration** (acquiring suppliers; Dell-style direct customer interaction and configuration).
  - Benefits: greater control over quality, security, customer service.
  - Challenges: limited resources (transportation, warehousing facilities), technology system (needs IT manager/engineer), risk management (solely responsible for delays/shortage/disasters).
- **Second Party Logistics (2PL):** an intermediary collecting and shipping material to market; **asset-based carrier** with a transportation mode. Example: a **courier partner delivering the farmer's eggs** to the grocery store.
  - Components: **service scope in specific areas** (transportation, warehousing, order fulfillment, freight forwarding); customized solutions; **better integration** and quick information sharing; **scalability and flexibility** (charges for actual orders, scale up/down — e.g., 1,000 orders to 2,000–3,000 students scenario); **technological integration** (TMS for best routes/vehicle capacity; WMS for inventory/tracking/visibility).
- **Third Party Logistics (3PL):** complete services beyond transportation — brokerage, customs, warehousing, distribution, value-adding activities (packaging, labeling, kitting).
  - Share: **transportation management 22%**, warehousing **21%**, value-adding **19%**.
  - Provides intermodal services, own fleets, lead logistics solutions.
  - **90% of Fortune 500 companies use 3PL.**
  - **Hitachi** example: procurement logistics, production logistics (material handling from floor to dispatch to outgoing QC), reverse logistics, sales-related logistics, collaborative logistics (consolidating with 2–3 vendors to fill a container).
  - Benefit: leverage expertise/resources/infrastructure, scalability/flexibility, global reach, technological integration (data analytics), cost saving, risk management (once dispatched, not the company's responsibility).
- **Fourth Party Logistics (4PL):** complete solution from one end to the customer end, and **manages the 3PLs**. Best advantage: **single-stop solution, single invoice**; one player from suppliers of suppliers to end customer → quick, accurate information sharing; uses ERP systems, IoT devices.
  - **Philips vs. Havells example:** Philips outsources manufacturing (co-manufacturers) as they want to control quality/branding/innovation; **Havells'** USP is efficient/quality manufacturing of **MCBs, RCCBs**, but they may not be good with the distribution network.
  - 4PL carries **no assets**; integrates all players.
  - Benefits: continuous improvement (fixed KPIs), global reach, market insights for expansion/diversification, risk management, resilient distribution network.
- **Fifth Party Logistics (5PL):** all players integrated, including all 3PLs; aggregates all customer requirements — you outsource even the outsourcing. Manufacturer and market are part of 5PL.
  - **Philips example:** co-manufacturers produce at different locations, take care of raw material suppliers and distribution network; Philips pays for the product, packaging, labeling; final product comes to a warehouse from where Philips creates demand and distributes. As brand owner, you just brand and bring new ideas.
- **Sixth Party Logistics (6PL):** next-generation player using **AI, machine learning** — AI/ML prepares the best possible **3D design**, you finalize changes, physical prototype developed/tested, market feedback taken automatically via **IoT**.

### 5.6 Quick Summary of Party Logistics
- **1PL** — direct delivery.
- **2PL** — transportation companies.
- **3PL** — logistics service provider.
- **4PL** — complete supply chain solution plus logistics services.
- **5PL** — full services expert logistics service provider.

### 5.7 Case Study — Elixia
- Provides end-to-end logistics solutions: transportation, distribution, warehouse, in-factory logistics, value-adding services.
- **>300 clients**; worth **₹35 billion INR**; operating in **60 million sq ft** warehouse area; **>10,000 vehicles**; serving **>17,000 PIN codes**; **>500 operational locations**.
- Problem identified: **improper vehicle allocation** (truck carrying 10 tons but carrying only 7/5/8 tons — full capacity not utilized) and **route planning not done properly** (not using GPS/RFID for routing).
- Solution: defined actual cost, optimal container capacity utilization, routes, mode of transportation via a software solution.
- **Results:** saved **₹6.7 lakhs in one month**; cost saving **8.4%**; **650 tons** carbon footprint reduced; vehicle space utilization increased by **25%**.

### 5.8 Conclusion
- For expansion, you need not think about the distribution network — collaborate with players (e.g., a single hotel can collaborate with **MakeMyTrip, Goibibo** to be visible to tourists). 3PL vendors provide tailored solutions and help modify/deliver products so the end customer feels satisfied/delighted.

## Lecture 06 — E-Logistics

### 6.1 E-Logistics vs. Traditional Retail
- E-logistics requires a **very high service level** (doorstep delivery, payment options, returns, variety, quality).
- **Returns comparison:** traditional brick-and-mortar return is rare (maybe a known face/shopkeeper and a major fault); online e-commerce returns are very high — almost **30–35% of products** returned.
- Doorstep delivery with a customer-specified time window (e.g., 6–7 pm) adds complexity.
- Traditional store covers one city/town/village with a **known population** → demand fairly known → challenges lower; e-logistics evolves daily (unknown future delivery speeds/expectations).

### 6.2 E-Logistics — Definition
- All the **physical flows of an organization that sells goods through an online platform** (selling a hotel room via own website, MakeMyTrip, Goibibo, etc.).
- Also called **electronic logistics / digital logistics** — use of digital technologies and electronic systems to manage and optimize various activities.

### 6.3 Major Challenges in E-Logistics
1. **Operations** — doorstep delivery vs. customer pickup locations.
2. **Visibility throughout the supply chain** — where inventory is, how much is left at retail store/distribution center/fulfillment center; as a customer, tracking dispatch, invoice, nearest hub, out-for-delivery.
3. **Efficiency**.
4. **Withdrawal period** — much longer in e-commerce. Example: **Meesho/eBay/Myntra** gave ~**20–26 days** to return apparel; customers used products then returned → loss; companies reduced the period and added **permanent tags** (removing the tag voids return).
5. **Negative experience** — **35% of customers** with a negative delivery experience will no longer order on the same e-commerce website.
6. **Fraud/payment issues** — money deducted but no acknowledgement; refund takes 4–5 working days; customer feels unsafe sharing banking details/passwords/CVV.

### 6.4 E-Logistics Market (International)
- **Taobao + Tmall** (both from **Alibaba**, China) carry more than **40%** share globally; Tmall ~**30%** globally and contribute ~**5–6% to Chinese GDP**.
  - **Tmall** — branded products (Nike, Adidas, Zara).
  - **Taobao** — small retailers/entrepreneurs; **C2C** model; local brands; ~**15%** share.
- **Amazon** — big US player. Other players: **JD.com, eBay, Walmart**.

### 6.5 E-Logistics Market (India)
- Major players: **Flipkart** and **Amazon**.
  - Flipkart carries ~**60%** share in electronics/appliances/mobile phones; earlier reducing due to Amazon's strong entry, now backed by **Walmart** (claims ~**80% equity** stake).
  - Together **~63%** of the Indian market by these two.
- Flipkart started ~**2007–08**; Amazon entered India ~**2013**; still growing.
- **Industry share in Indian e-commerce:** **40% electronics**, followed by **40% apparel**, then grocery/food, jewelry, furniture (lower penetration because customers want to physically see/touch). For phones, specifications are well-known (e.g., iPhone 14/15), quality promise comes from **Apple** as authorized distributor.
- **Festive season (GMV):** Flipkart carries **62%** share (thanks to **Big Billion Days**; sometimes 80–90% offers on home appliances/mobiles/electronics). Order quantity (units sold): Flipkart **49%**, Meesho **21%**.
- Sales increase during festive: **mobile sales 7×**, **electronics/appliances 5×**, **fashion 2–3×**.
- **Challenge:** not sure about demand → stock-outs during Big Billion Days / Amazon Prime Days; delivery promised within 10 days may take **15–20 days**.
- **Walmart's strategy** — **everyday low pricing** (same price any time) → customer feels safe with stabilized price; Walmart smoothly channels inventory because demand is **90–95% fixed** (small variation, managed with safety stock).
- **Fashion e-commerce India:** Flipkart **27%**, then **Myntra**, then Amazon and Meesho at **15–15%**. Myntra's **sustainable paper packaging** labeled "**100% recyclable**" — liked by the professor as customers become aware of carbon footprint.

### 6.6 E-Logistics Solutions to Customers
1. **Delivery methods** — home delivery; **pickup point** (for office people; popular in **France**).
2. **D+ days promises** — e.g., within three days, one extra day is unbearable; yet Flipkart/Amazon delay during festive seasons.
3. **Greener delivery method** — bicycles/electric vehicles; **87% of French people** interested in sustainable last-mile delivery.
4. **Free deliveries** — **43% of French online buyers** left shopping baskets vacant wanting free deliveries. Strategies: minimum purchase value for free delivery, next delivery free, loyalty-based free deliveries.
5. **Delivery tracking** — order accepted/processed/shipped/reached/out-for-delivery messages; some customers find many messages annoying → single clickable link for updated order.

### 6.7 Key Elements of E-Logistics
1. **Supply chain visibility** — track movement of goods, monitor inventory, find potential bottlenecks.
2. **Transportation Management System** — optimize route, pick carrier/mode, load planning, track shipments.
3. **Warehouse Management System** — manage inventory, picking/packing/shipping, maximize capacity, prevent items going outdated.
4. **Inventory management** — demands from different platforms consolidated with real-time tracking; storage houses near customer.
5. **Electronic Data Interchange (EDI)** — avoids manual entry mistakes and information inflation (**bullwhip example**: actual demand 10 → retailer says 12 → wholesaler 14 → manufacturer plans 16 → imports raw material for 16).
6. **Global Trade Management** — software for regulations, tariff management, customs duties, international trade norms.
7. **Last-mile delivery** — optimization software, delivery tracking apps, automated delivery vehicles (robots); cost-benefit analysis of covering 100 m more to reach 10 more customers.
8. **Reverse logistics** — for a delivery, the company bears the cost; speed post costs ₹70–100, but a return earns nothing. Four deliveries in the cycle: manufacturer → distribution center (1st), distribution center → customer (2nd), return → distribution center, return → seller/manufacturer.
9. **Analytics and predictive insights** — ML/data analytics predict demand, optimize processes, anticipate disruptions (except natural ones).
10. **Blockchain technology** — transparency, security, traceability (transactions and the product flowing).

### 6.8 Major Challenges — Returns
- **30%** of products returned online vs. **9%** at brick-and-mortar stores.
- Free delivery: **49%** want it.
- Return reasons: received damaged, product looks different from website, wrong item received, other reasons (size/comfort). **23% wrong item** needs checking to ensure the item reaches the right customer; **22%** can be targeted with fair information.
- **67% of shoppers** check the returns page before purchase; **62% more likely to shop online** if they can return an item in the store.
- Amazon survey (February): of returned products, **59% sellable**, but customers **damage 32%** (shirt example: pins/packaging not returned, collar-binding packaging damaged, needs washing/ironing again).

### 6.9 Same-Day Delivery — The Next Normal
- **Amazon Prime** — pay once, get same-day delivery on selected items, free delivery for all items, plus Amazon Prime Video (bundled advantage) to compete with Walmart.
- Achieved by deploying warehouses near customers; order by noon → get by **9 pm**, seven days a week; free delivery over **$35** (fully free with Prime).
- **Walmart** partnered with **UPS** (United Parcel Service); cost **$10 per purchase**; flat $10 delivery and 10% delivery fee; no minimum order.
- **eBay** — three steps: shop from your nearby retail store; partnership with local stores and local courier partners → order in morning, get by noon.
- **Same-day delivery market** expected **13.8% by 2027**.
  - **49%** of customers more likely to shop online if same-day delivery.
  - **51%** of online customers aged **18–34** lack patience to wait two days.
  - Almost **99% of retailers** plan to offer same-day delivery by **2024**; gives an **85% competitive edge**.
- **Quick commerce in India** — **Blinkit, Zepto, Swiggy Instamart**: **10-minute delivery** (grocery; e.g., realizing sugar is missing while making tea; can wait max ~30 minutes). No minimum order concept (Swiggy's USP — even one piece of sweet).
- Quick-delivery items: **milk, egg, bread, curd, fruit, vegetables** (don't want to wait); indulgence items — **soft drink, dessert, chocolate, cigarette** (want instantaneously). **23%** of grocery items are last-minute — cannot plan unless the kitchen is **Industry 4.0**/AI-supported (alarm before reaching safety stock).

### 6.10 Conclusion
- The e-commerce platform faces huge challenges but keeps stretching further (same-day, 10-minute delivery, perishables); the challenge is to be responsive while remaining efficient.

## Lecture 07 — International Logistics (Part 1: Cross-Border Transactions)

### 7.1 Why International Logistics
- Increasingly important discipline for the global economy; companies leverage it to **reduce cost** and take advantage of **highly specialized capabilities** in different regions/countries.
- Three major stakeholders viewed internationally: **suppliers, manufacturers, customers** (minimum two or more countries).
- **Suppliers (international):** reasons to go overseas — raw material availability, cheaper raw material, easy/cheap resources (manpower), technology, market.
  - **Pros/cons:** local supplier → can implement JIT, no safety stock, smooth supply even if network fails. Overseas supplier → plan inventory in advance; replenishment lead time (e.g., **10 days**); if anything goes wrong, whole production stops.
  - Overseas plus: e.g., **Chinese market** well-known for **lighting solutions** — fair quality at very reasonable price vs. high local cost; better raw material quality.
- **Manufacturers (international):** co-manufacturers; why US apparel sets up hubs in **Pakistan, Bhutan, Bangladesh, Nepal** — raw material availability, skills, cheap resources. **Walmart** sells a **$5 t-shirt** in the US by processing overseas and selling back.
  - Technology example: **Japanese** known for technology/quality; **Toyota Production System (TPS)**; reason for the **Hero Honda** venture (Honda had technology, Hero had a vast distribution network in India).
- **Customers (international):** access to foreign expertise/technology/resources and foreign customers; brand visibility. **Ola/Uber** example — one app anywhere in the world, pay in your own currency, automatically converted and credited to the partner; familiarity/safety for travelers → why international logistics is required.
- International logistics: **sourcing, hiring, and delighting customers around the world** — not just physical movement but also **money and information movement**.

### 7.2 Why Go International (factors)
1. **Cost efficiency** — despite multiple tax structures, tariffs, duties, long-distance cost, it can still be cheaper. Example: **sunflower oil/raw material produced in India sent to some African countries, and the finished product imported back** cheaper than processing it in India and selling to the Indian market.
2. **Reliability (and misconception)** — if a shipment goes wrong, the lot is gone and the next shipment may be **20 days** away. Strategy: if demand is continuous → start own distribution network; if demand is fluctuating → outsource.
3. **Relationships** — with many vendors (vendor 1…vendor n), if anything goes wrong in one part of the world, you survive through the distribution network.
4. **Competitive advantage** — international logistics can attract international customers, especially with low differentiation/USPs.

### 7.3 Components of International Logistics
1. **Customs clearance** — taxes, customs, duties; e.g., in India **GST** (goods in first/second/third/fourth category); tax structure not fixed (revised by countries).
2. **Supply chain visibility** — **RFID, IoT sensors** (not fully utilized yet); RFID example: in one-stop stores like **Big Bazaar, Vishal Mega Mart, Spencer's**, the tracking gun reads the barcode → inventory number, level left, pricing; information shared digitally with manufacturer and raw material supplier to prepare the next consignment; **GPS tracking** for real-time visibility of goods on the road.
3. **Inventory management** — fluctuation in international demand; inventory at ports costlier; maintain service level and **inventory turnover**.
   - **Inventory turnover** = (total daily sales) / (average cost of inventory).
   - **Average inventory** = (beginning inventory + ending inventory) / 2.
4. **Risk management** — geopolitical instability (elections, shutdowns), **currency fluctuation**:
   - Import: €1 = ₹100 today → import charges ₹100; if rupee depreciates to ₹105, you pay ₹5 extra for the same €1 product.
   - Export: exporting €1 gives ₹100; if depreciated, you get ₹5 extra; if appreciating, reverse. Rates affect profit margins.
   - Also natural disasters, supply chain disruptions, local supply chain disruptions.
5. **Incoterms (International Commercial Terms)** — rights and responsibilities of buyers/sellers; risk with buyer or supplier if quantity/quality/transport incident goes wrong; dispute resolution location (buyer India, supplier China — cross-border regulations).
6. **Local culture and language consideration** — e.g., latest **Ola/Rapido** ad: foreign tourists can book a taxi/auto and speak in their language with AI-enabled translation.
7. **Sustainability** — French survey awareness; in India, no single-use plastic, using **jute bags**; eco-friendly practices and energy-efficient operations (else product can be rejected in the market).

### 7.4 Cross-Border Transactions
- Can be as easy as customer-to-customer or as difficult as B2B.
- Value of cross-border transactions forecast to reach **$250 trillion by 2027** — a rise of more than **$100 trillion in one decade**.
- Factors: international trade and e-commerce (whole world is one market), manufacturers expanding supply chains across borders, global investment and asset management, international workforce (people in villages accepting salaries from international players), one global market.

### 7.5 Steps in Cross-Border Transaction
1. **Purchase and initiation** — start the purchase cycle/transaction.
2. **Routing and processing** — payment info sent via an **encrypted gateway**; bank connected to the gateway.
3. **Payment verification** — verified/approved or declined by the receiving bank (insufficient funds; also transaction charges, e.g., €500 = ~₹45,000 may become ~₹48,000–₹50,000).
4. **Transaction confirmation** — approved or declined message.
5. **Transaction fulfillment** — between the two parties (digital transfer happens via **global automated clearing house**, taking **2–5 business days**; once approved, order fulfillment can start).
6. **Tracking** — status of transaction reconciliation and settlement.

### 7.6 Challenges with Cross-Border Transactions
1. **Seven steps/players** — each stage small holes, time-consuming and charges (account verification, fund transfer).
2. **High funding cost** — maintain funds in advance (reserve ₹50k for a ₹45k requirement due to transaction costs).
3. **High transaction fees** — bank fees, merchant bank fees.
4. **Currency exchange rate** — appreciation/depreciation impact.
5. **Long transaction chains** — many players.
6. **Multi-currency cross-border transactions** — if more than two currencies, multiply the steps; transaction only between first and end party, but many in between, each paid extra.
7. **Sale tax** — local structure; product "sold" at each of ~15 stages, adding ₹0.5–₹1 each, initial ₹10 ends at ₹18–20.
8. **Limited banking hours** — 24-hour digital transfer but settlement/reconciliation only during bank working hours (foreign market time lag of **10–12 hours**).
9. **Fragmented and truncated data formats** — different formats, address spelling/format errors → declined.
10. **Legacy technology platforms** — e.g., Indian banks counting notes by machine twice then manually; slow adoption.
11. **Complex compliance checks** — fraud, sanctions, financial crime; if a name matches a wrongdoer's name, it is declined without further verification.
- **One-stop solution:** **Amazon Global Market Logistics / Amazon Wallet**.

### 7.7 Major Stakeholders in International Logistics
1. **Exporter** — sells the product.
2. **Importer** — imports the product.
3. **Freight forwarder** — coordinates/monitors operations for transport and international freight logistics; may do value-adding (packaging, communication).
4. **Customs agent** — documents, formalities, inspections, clearance.
5. **Shipping company** — legal owner of the vessels.
6. **Shipping agent** — legal representative of the shipping company at a given port; organizes administrative tasks.
7. **Consolidator** — sells vessel space (if not fully occupied by one player, sells to multiple).
8. **Carrier** — actually transports the product.
9. **Insurance** — premium per transaction; ensures safe delivery.
10. **Banks** — ensure the transaction.

### 7.8 Conclusion
- Many stakeholders/stages → compromise between cost and time; consider replenishment time and cost. Example: an **Amazon global logistics** partner handles everything from Kharagpur to the US market with a **single invoice**, minimizing transactions.

## Lecture 08 — International Logistics (Part 2: Market Penetration Strategies)

### 8.1 Six Options for Going International (Forward Channel)
Focus is on the forward channel (serving customers internationally), not backward integration (international suppliers). Options: **exporting, licensing, joint ventures, ownerships, importing, counter trade.** Each has pros/cons (cost efficiency, on-time delivery, loss of quality control, dilution of ownership).

### 8.2 Exporting
- Selling goods/services from one country to another; **no intermediary** → **most simple and least risky method**.
- Types:
  - **Direct exporting** — sell directly to customers in foreign markets without intermediaries; own fulfillment centers, retail shops, doorstep delivery.
  - **Indirect exporting** — use trading companies, agents, distributors, or local courier partners. Example: selling in the **UK market** with a distribution/order fulfillment center in the UK, using a local partner for cross docking, avoiding investment in the distribution network.
- **Benefits:** market diversification, competitive advantage (e.g., a **credit card** usable anywhere in the world, no need to care about exchange rates/NOC documentation), economies of scale (when the Indian market is saturated), revenue growth.
- **Challenges:**
  1. **Market entry barriers** — local restrictions, regulatory/trade restrictions, different tax/legal structures.
  2. **Logistics challenges** — India is geographically widespread (hilly north to south); different infrastructure (train/road/ship), infrastructure constraints. This was a basic reason for the **Hero–Honda** joint venture (Honda technology + Hero's distribution network for cycles); after the breakup both compete separately because each got market/industry knowledge.
  3. **Financial risk** — e.g., opening a warehouse of **500-ton** capacity using only 350, then 400, and demand falls → what to do with the capacity/network?
  4. **Currency exchange** — selling €1 for ₹100, if rupee appreciates you get only ₹95.
  5. **Legal and regulatory compliance / intellectual property rights.**
  6. **Cultural and language barriers** — can be avoided via indirect exporting (local courier partners).
- **Examples:** **Tata Motors** (vehicles to South Africa, Europe, South East Asia; bus network), **Bajaj Auto** (motorcycles, three-wheelers, commercial vehicles to **70+ countries**), **Infosys** (IT software solutions across North America, Europe, Asia Pacific).

### 8.3 Licensing
- The **licensor** provides the **licensee** rights to use their strategy/recipe to produce/sell products/services; intellectual property — **trademarks, patents, copyrights, proprietary technology**.
- **KFC/McDonald's** example: rather than setting up own distribution centers all over the world (long time, unfamiliar markets), share resources/trademark/recipe/raw material/technology with local vendors to produce similar quality → grow brand image (same store look anywhere → customer feels connected).
- **Types:**
  - **Trademark licensing** — use brand name/logo (e.g., McDonald's).
  - **Patent licensing** — use patented technology/invention (secret recipe).
  - **Copyright licensing** — literary/music/art/software (e.g., dubbing a Hollywood movie in Hindi or making a sequel).
  - **Franchising** — right to operate the same business with same brand name, model name, operating procedures.
- **Benefits:** market expansion, risk mitigation (a burger with the McDonald's name assures minimum quality), low capital investment, brand exposure/visibility.
- **Challenges:** no direct control over quality (on behalf of McDonald's someone else uses the same recipe), intellectual property/competitive risk (today's partner becomes tomorrow's competitor — Honda now knows the Indian market/supply chain/strategic locations), dependency on licenses (cannot make changes; cannot move to another market without permission), legal and contractual risks (negotiation/drafting/enforcement; e.g., **Hero Honda** struggled while splitting).
- **Examples:** **Amul** (Amul-branded products in 20 countries, maintaining quality/standards, license agreements, training partners); **ITC Limited** (brands **Aashirvaad, Sunfeast, Bingo** — partnering for manufacturing and distribution); **FabIndia** (licenses its brand for retail operations in several countries with local partners).

### 8.4 Joint Venture
- Coming to a foreign market, exploring a third market (or leveraging updated technology/resources). **Hero Honda, Maruti Suzuki**.
- **Types:**
  - **Equity joint venture** — partners contribute capital/resources to establish a new legal entity; hold ownership stake; fixed share in profits, risks, control (e.g., an LLC).
  - **Contractual joint venture** — for a specific period; define termination provisions. **Hero Honda** was contractual; **Maruti Suzuki** is not contractual.
- **Benefits:** risk sharing, access to resources (Honda technology ↔ Hero manufacturing/distribution), easy market entry, cost efficiency (no huge investment), risk diversification (local market knowledge).
- **Challenges:**
  1. **Cultural challenges** — e.g., **Walmart acquired 80% of Flipkart**; Walmart notorious for worker-related policies (e.g., the **Rana Plaza** tragedy in Pakistan where many workers died and Walmart refused compensation), strict anti-union guidelines; Flipkart started by Indians → policies/strategies merging, cultural differences.
  2. **Strategic alignment** — e.g., **Tata Nano plant in Singur, West Bengal** — farmer agitation; Tata invested ₹1,000 crore, then withdrew ("our values are stronger than our steels" — no profit at the stake of farmers' interest). If two companies, easy decision-making is not there.
  3. **Control and governance** — clearly mention control (Walmart's 80% stake has authority); equal share → equal decision-making powers; dispute resolution.
  4. **Intellectual property protection** — confidentiality/ownership (Honda's secret recipe no longer secret for Hero).
  5. **Exit strategy** — dissolution, divestment, or buyout.
- **Examples:** **Mahindra & Mahindra** with **Mahindra Susten** (renewable energy), **Hindustan Unilever Limited** with **Unilever** (personal care/home care), **Reliance Industries** with global energy companies (exploration, production, refining in the US and other countries).

### 8.5 Ownership (Acquisition)
- **Ownership is the reverse of JV** — one takes ownership of another (**acquisition**). Example: **Ranbaxy**, and **Flipkart** (Walmart's 80% stake → decision-making with Walmart).
- Encompasses the right to **use, manage, transfer, and dispose of assets**.
- **Forms:**
  - **Wholly owned subsidiaries** — 100% share, full control of decision-making/management (Walmart can control Flipkart operations in India).
  - **Equity investment** — part of the investment → access to markets, customers, technology, distribution channels; minority/majority ownership position; opportunities for collaboration, integration, value creation.
  - **Franchising** — third party operates under your brand name using intellectual property.
  - **Acquisition** — purchasing existing business assets/subsidiaries; forward or backward integration (acquire suppliers or distributors); 100% → full control, policies, market presence/excess.
- **Benefits:** knowledge transfer (Flipkart ↔ Walmart, world's largest retailer / third largest employer), better risk management (broader picture).
- **Challenges:** capital requirements, regulatory compliance, **cultural differences (Walmart vs. Flipkart; "vocal for local" campaigns)**, integration challenges, political and economic risk (Walmart earlier failed venturing with **Bharti Airtel**; then acquired Flipkart with 60%+ in some markets), inflation/recession/exchange-rate fluctuations.
- **Examples:** **Aditya Birla** acquired **Novelis** (aluminum rolling/recycling giant), **Tata Steel** acquired **Corus Group** (largest player globally), **Dr. Reddy's Laboratories** acquired select business divisions and manufacturing facilities of multinational companies.

### 8.6 Importing
- Bringing goods/services into your country from a foreign distributor (just purchasing).
- **Benefits:** more product options, cost competitiveness, **time to market** (no manufacturing needed), market expansion, risk mitigation (import 100 today, 1000 tomorrow, 10 day after — no built capacity to worry about).
- **Challenges:** trade barriers, regulatory frameworks/trade restrictions/import duties, supply chain complexity (many players, trade relations), **no direct control over quality**, currency and financial risk, financial volatility/credit risk, competitive pressure.
- **Examples:** **Walmart** imports worldwide (feeds suppliers large orders → negotiating), **Amazon India** partners with global players for diverse products, **Apple India** imports iPhones/iPads/MacBooks and sells via authorized distributors.

### 8.7 Counter Trade
- Avoids the financial transaction — a **barter system**: "I produce ₹1,000 goods, you produce ₹1,000 goods; I give you my products, you give me yours." Removes intermediaries, documentation, exchange rates, banks/agents → preferred.
- Involves **reciprocal trade agreements**; used by private firms and government organizations.
- **Advantages:** cost cutting, no real currency involved → financial risk (exchange rate) managed better; win-win if transaction and logistics costs are fairly compared.
- **Challenges:**
  1. **Fixing the equivalence** — evaluating that ₹1,000 goods (yours) = ₹1,000 goods (mine).
  2. **Mismatch in product handling** — one product easily packed/stored (no special cold storage) vs. another needing special attention/cold storage/handling, difficult packaging, expiry dates → hard to negotiate.
  3. **Transaction/legal/trade framework differences** between the two industries.
  4. **Operational risk** — delivery delays, inventory management challenges; if delivery is delayed at one end, the other suffers.
- **Examples:** **Indian Oil Corporation** — importing crude oil from **Saudi Arabia** and selling agriculture products and infrastructure development; **ONGC** (Oil and Natural Gas Corporation) with **Bidesh Limited** scheme — offering technical expertise and investment in exchange for energy resources.

### 8.8 Major International Logistics Players
- Global: **FedEx, Ryder, Expeditors (Expo Logistics), UPS (United Parcel Service), DHL**.
- India-related: **Blue Dart, GATI, FedEx, Mahindra Logistics, GATI KWE**. **Delhivery** — courier partner earlier with Amazon/Flipkart, now with eBay, Meesho, etc.

### 8.9 Case Study — Peru Syringe Shipment (COVID-19)
- During COVID-19, Peru faced a sudden rise in demand for medical devices. To run the vaccination program, a continuous supply of **syringes from China to Peru** was needed, but logistics congestion (transportation restrictions, shutdown) was there.
- **Solution:** used **charter flights**; worked out the passenger area required in each flight and **tapped the unused area** to transport syringes. A total of **8 air charters and part charters** from southern China (where the factory was) to Peru.
- Route: **Shanghai → Alaska → Los Angeles → Mexico → Lima** (difficult route because slots were not available).
- **Result:** complied with the client's timing and maintained reasonable transport cost; syringes arrived on time every month to complete the vaccination campaign phases in Peru.

### 8.10 Conclusion
- No business is purely local today; customers feel comfortable with brands that exist overseas (familiarity everywhere). Challenges are huge due to many stakeholders. **Amazon Global Logistics** solves distribution problems and **Amazon Wallet** reduces transaction cost among multiple vendors.

## Lecture 09 — Logistics Key Performance Indicators (KPIs)

### 9.1 Purpose of KPIs
- Measuring and managing logistics performance = evaluating aspects of logistics operations to assess **efficiency** (cost), **effectiveness** (meeting customer demands), and **compliance** (with vision/mission/goals).
- Customer-side KPIs: on-time delivery, variety/inventory, accurate order processing, cost, doorstep delivery, convenience, and whether expectations are met on opening the packet.

### 9.2 The Two Extremes — Trade-off
- **Service provider implications** vs. **service level provided to customer**; trade-off between:
  1. **Responsiveness to customer** — e.g., **Swiggy** delivering even a single piece of sweet (not bothering about economies of scale; not combining orders).
  2. **Cost efficiency** — e.g., **Walmart** (world's largest retailer), selling a t-shirt at **$5**; needs economies of scale; minimizes cost of providing services.
- Cannot be extreme in both; find a proper blend.

### 9.3 Types of KPIs (by functional area)
Divided into: **transportation, warehouse/inventory/order fulfillment, distribution, and overall supply chain/logistics performance**.
- Purpose of KPIs is two-fold: (1) find your **current position** (rate 1–10); (2) **benchmark** against the best (e.g., Amazon for delivery time).
- Sample KPIs introduced: **shipping time, order accuracy, picking accuracy, order cycle time, equipment utilization rate, trailer utilization rate, transportation cost, dwell time, inventory carrying cost, warehousing cost, pick and pack cost, operating ratio, use of packaging material, number of shipments, inventory accuracy, inventory turnover, inventory-to-sales ratio.**

### 9.4 Transportation Management KPIs
1. **Average cost per order** — add up costs (marketing campaign $500, COGS/operation cost $1,200, packaging, shipping, storage) and divide by total number of orders. Compare with benchmark for similar products; identify unusual products adding more cost.
2. **Average transportation cost** — cost per km × total distance; e.g., monthly gross income ₹10,000 → transportation is **8%** of gross income.
3. **Freight cost per unit shipped** — freight cost / units shipped (e.g., **$0.70 per unit**); reflects use of the cargo mix (unutilized capacity adds cost).
   - **Concept:** cost per unit decreases with increasing quantity up to the container's maximum capacity (e.g., 10 tons), then increases sharply when a second truck is needed (2–10 tons decreasing cost; 11–13 tons cost shoots up).
4. **Transit time to distance** — e.g., 5 days for a distance → **0.0025 days per mile**; compare road vs. train vs. ship vs. air to select the mode.
5. **Stock rotation** — total sales / average inventory (e.g., **20** → company sells through its stock 20 times in the period); helps avoid expired/outdated stock; depends on product perishability.
6. **Out-of-network shipment** — unplanned shipments; e.g., **0.4%**; happens due to poor forecasting; expensive.

### 9.5 Distribution Management KPIs
1. **On-time final delivery** — e.g., 15 of 18 products delivered on time → **83%**. Must also consider quality: broken product/damaged packaging is not a complete order; incomplete package (3 of 5 items) is not complete.
   - On-time delivery requires the complete cycle: customer finalizes order → seller ready → courier picks up → nearest hub → other state/nearest hub → fulfillment center → delivered.
2. **On-time pickup (carrier performance)** — e.g., 43 of 46 shipments picked on time → **93.5%**.
3. **On-time shipping** — e.g., Delhi/Gurgaon hub → Kolkata hub → nearest fulfillment center → out for delivery. If 18 of 23 fully on time → **78.3%**. Goal: 100% — any bottleneck delays other activities.

### 9.6 Warehouse and Order Management KPIs
1. **Unplanned shipment** — e.g., **0.1%** (4 of 4000 shipments); reason: couldn't figure out demand; products not available or semi-finished; courier already picked → gap of 4–5 hours or next day (**12-hour gap**).
2. **Order accuracy / order picking accuracy** — starts with recording the order (customer info, payment info, requirements/special instructions, **address** — wrong address/pin code causes inaccuracy). Example: **96%** correct orders packed. Airport luggage bar code example: if routed to the wrong destination, high cost, customer dissonance.
3. **Inventory accuracy** — physical inventory vs. books; e.g., physical **3,458** vs. books **3,506** → **98.6%**. Example: 50 tyres, 5 per car → should make 10 cars; a missing ₹10 component can stall a ₹10-lakh final product.
4. **Dock to stock** — time to place stock on shelf ready for sale; e.g., **12 hours / 18 shipments = 0.6 hours per shipment**.
5. **Unit processed per square feet** — e.g., **3.7 units per sq ft**; depends on product (steel rods vs. water tanks require different space; in a truck, steel rods can take **20 tons** but water tanks only **2 tons** due to size).
6. **Space use in warehouse** — e.g., **3,300 of 16,500 sq ft = 20%** utilized; target 85–90%.

### 9.7 Overall Logistics Performance KPIs
1. **Cost per pound** — water tanks (low weight, big size) → e.g., **$0.38 per pound**; relative to product/industry.
2. **Cost per mile** — (total fixed cost of assets/vehicle carrier + variable cost) / miles; e.g., **$1.34 per mile**; varies by mode (road vs. ship vs. air — air higher).
3. **Number of shipments** — identify regions with maximum orders → combine orders, minimize delivery time (metro city same-day delivery due to population density; village: 10 km for 20 products vs. city: 2 km for 500 orders); analyze seasonal fluctuations (fruits/vegetables); monthly shipment by reason, by product, and buying frequency.

### 9.8 Conclusion
- KPIs identified across transportation, supply, distribution, warehousing, order management, and overall logistics performance; next session targets how to improve them.

## Lecture 10 — Tools for Improving Logistics Efficiency

### 10.1 Quality — Two Perspectives
- **Quality** has two definitions: one for the **customer** and one for the **product supplier/manufacturer**.
- **Quality control** in logistics ensures the product reaches customers with specified standards and quality.
  - Example: receiving a damaged package at the doorstep (even unopened) gives a bad impression and hampers brand image; customers may then prefer physical stores for delicate products.
- Focus areas of quality: **transportation, warehousing, inventory management, material handling, packaging**.

### 10.2 Quality Assurance and Definitions
- **Quality assurance** — a procedure to ensure quality by **preventing mistakes and defects**.
- Definitions of quality:
  - **Fit for purpose** — readily usable for its intended purpose; distribution network should deliver so the product is "plug and play" at home.
  - **Right first time** — no chance of mistake; example: must not ring someone else's door and apologize for delivering the wrong order.
- **Producer perspective** — main focus on **conformance to specifications** (e.g., packaging strength to bear wear and tear during transportation; define parameters/range within control). Cost of meeting quality affects price, profit, margins.
- **Consumer perspective** — **fitness for use**; not worried about specifications or behind-the-scenes parameters. Example: for a **5-star AC**, the customer only cares that it reduces the electricity bill and provides proper cooling with inverter technology — not the aluminum/copper binding or capacity of electronic components.

### 10.3 Cost of Quality
- **Cost of conformance** (direct cost) — doing quality checks: a team, equipment, facilities to sort/pack/label/write addresses so the right product reaches the right customer.
  - Indirect cost: **quality training** (skilled people for packaging, warehouse handling, routing/delivery), **studies/surveys** to check if quality is met.
- **Cost of non-conformance** — not meeting standards:
  - Wrong delivery, wrong product shipped; order comes back rejected; product becomes **scrap**; fragile items may break on return; damaged during shipment → **refund**, **rework**, warranty-period engineer visits replacing components free of cost.
  - **Damaged product share ~25–30%** when delivering (direct cost); indirect cost: **brand image** hampered. Survey: **80–85% of customers** with a prior bad experience avoid those e-commerce websites and prefer going physically.
- **"Quality is free"** — if quality is ensured, none of the non-conformance costs occur, so maintaining brand image makes quality effectively free.

### 10.4 Identifying Faults — Packaging Example
- Packaging happens in **three shifts (A, B, C)**, three different people.
- Common packaging errors: damaged packaging not checked, wrong product packed, missing accessories (e.g., mobile charger missing), missing instruction manual.
- Fault analysis by frequency: problem 1 = 15 times, **problem 2 = 20 times (highest)**, problem 3 = 10 times, problem 4 = 5 times.
  - Highest frequency: **wrong product packed** → prioritize and take quick action.
  - Next: **packaging damaged** → operator and packaging material vendor responsible.
- Identify **which shift operator** has maximum complaints.
- **Root cause analysis (5 Whys style):** operator not happy with job/company/compensation/medical; skills not aligned; disturbed (personal/professional life). Root cause traced to **placement**: the person was capable of something else but deployed on that workstation; **HR** should identify placement during the interview/selection.

### 10.5 Automation Example
- **DHL** example: **40,000 items per hour** handled through automated machines; **11,000+ facilities** injected with packages — showing how automation improves performance indicators.

### 10.6 Plan-Do-Check-Act (PDCA) Cycle
- Also known as the **Deming wheel / PDCA wheel**; approach given by **Dr. William Edwards Deming**; **Walter Shewhart** known as quality guru (Deming Prize in Japan).
- This is the first tool discussed.

**PLAN:**
- Define the **objective** — identify the problem (e.g., packaging-related).
- Three sub-steps:
  1. **Identify the improvement opportunity** — pick the highest-occurring problem first (e.g., wrong product shipped 20 times) to reduce the most complaints.
  2. **Set measurable targets** — e.g., reduce from 20 to 12/10 in 1 month, to 5 in 2 months, then check consistency at 6 months.
  3. **Prepare the action plan** — sustain the plan, address other workers' grievances, come up with a new policy if needed.

**DO:**
- Test the plan by actually performing the action.
- Compare actual output against standards; if no deviation → continue the process (positive feedback). If deviation → take action: **corrective action** (change process/raw material/technology) and **preventive action** (policy so it does not happen in future).

**CHECK:**
- Check whether the goal has been met.

**ACT:**
- If met → standardize/sustain the procedure (for other employees too); if not met → take feedback, take corrective action, and repeat the cycle.
- The cycle keeps going until the specific problem is resolved, then move to the next problem.

### 10.7 How PDCA Enhances Logistics Quality
- **Quality costs** are associated with obtaining the required quality (e.g., deployment of **industrial preventive maintenance**).
- **Appraisal costs** are quality costs — testing, manpower training, equipment, facilities.
- **Non-quality costs** — if standards are not met and faulty delivery happens again and again, customers lose interest and will think twice before ordering (especially for financial transactions).

### 10.8 Preview of Upcoming Tools
- **7 QC tools**, **quality circle**, **5S approach**, and **benchmarking** — to improve logistics quality in subsequent sessions.
