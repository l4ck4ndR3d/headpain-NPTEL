## Lecture 41 — Supply Chain Performance: Balanced Scorecard Approach (Contd.)

### 41.1 Recap — Four Perspectives
- The **balanced scorecard** was introduced previously; this session applies it to the supply chain and measures/improves performance across all **four perspectives**.
- The four perspectives: **financial results**, **internal business processes**, **learning and growth**, and **customer/client satisfaction**.
- **Financial** — profit, market share, return on investment: how much is invested in warehousing, extra inventory, R&D/innovation and whether returns materialize; **machine productivity** (adopt new technology or use old technology efficiently).
- **Internal business processes** — improve and innovate internally, empower resources to satisfy the customer with quality, delivery, and cost.
- **Client/customer** — all clients/stakeholders in the value chain must be made satisfied.

### 41.2 Financial Perspective KPIs
- **Cost reduction** — negotiate on **quantity** with the supplier; **automate the warehouse** to cut handling/storage cost; implement a **point-of-sale system** (directly linked with the sales team) to minimize inventory.
- **Spending and cost of goods sold** — do a **spending analysis** (how much transportation, logistics, manufacturing, raw-material suppliers contribute). Example: **logistics contributes 14% versus an industry average of 10%** → a **4% gap** to close.
- **Resource optimization** — routing, vehicle routing, warehouse optimization, manufacturing-facility optimization.
- **Payment terms** — negotiate **30, 60, or 90 days**; the longer the period, the higher the negotiation power; use **leveraged agreements** so the financial cash can be used elsewhere during that period.
- **Supplier consolidation** — if one supplier can supply multiple products, consolidate orders into large quantity for negotiation.
- Find a **cheaper location** and use **open competitive bidding** (pick the cost-efficient, quality-efficient player).

### 41.3 Internal Business Processes
- **Sourcing methodology** — **vendor rating** picks the best stakeholder; strategic sourcing decides purchasing frequency, **lot size**, lead time, and whether **JIT** cuts inventory cost; **risk protection** (phase-out of a product, raw-material inventory); **supplier alliances, contract management, performance management**.
- **Decision-making skills** — **predictive analytics, big-data analytics** to predict demand, supply, and fluctuations in internal and external environments.
- **Total predictive maintenance** — maintain a machine **before breakdown** so there is no breakdown and no rejection.

### 41.4 Learning and Growth
- Develop resources/skills across the team; manage the **turnover/attrition rate**.
- **Infosys example:** during the **2008 recession** most IIT stakeholders fired employees, but Infosys **retained all employees and developed their capabilities**, treating it as time to "pull up your socks" so that when market demand returned it could run faster.
- To retain employees, show a proper **career path/development path** so development does not saturate.

### 41.5 Customer Perspective
- Satisfy the customer with **features, variety, customer services, quality, price** (whatever quality can be offered within the price range), **after-sales services**, and **branding** — e.g., an expensive **Rolex** or other high-status product carries an image attached to customer performance.
- **Customer KPI — order fulfillment rate**, customer satisfaction score/feedback; prioritize areas with lower feedback.
- **Flipkart/AC example:** Flipkart gave the best pricing, cash back, and free installation, but the **local technician** (from **Hitachi**, **Whirlpool**) does the installation; if the technician is late or misbehaves, the service provider is rated lower even if everything else was right — so think about the player who can control installation services.
- **Lead time** — time taken to fulfill the order.

### 41.6 Internal Business Process KPIs (Supply Chain)
- **Cycle time** — order completion speed; how quickly a model changeover happens.
- **Inventory turnover** — how much inventory is kept and how quickly it is sold.
- **Supply-chain flexibility** — how quickly the chain adapts to new product variety, supply disruption, disaster, or strike; **resilience** to absorb disruptions while maintaining smooth supply.
- **Innovation and improvement** — continuous innovation (**Philips** tagline "**innovation and you**"), training and development, technology utilization, innovation rate, **technology penetration rate**.
- Whether a **WMS (Warehouse Management System)** is implemented; whether **Industry 4.0**-supported systems are used.

### 41.7 Balanced Scorecard Framework for Procurement
- **Financial measures:** save cost by negotiating (more purchasing power when buying in bulk); **total cost of ownership** (not only raw-material cost — include delivery and storage cost); cost per unit/per transaction; **procurement budget** vs planned.
- **Customer measures:** complaints directly related to vendors. Example: bought a TV and the **sound system is not working** → do the **why-why analysis** → problem in the component of the sound system; use **cross-functional teams** to select/evaluate suppliers; internal customer satisfaction.
- **Internal business processes:** raising purchase orders when inventory depletes; **kanban production system**; keep quantity in mind to negotiate price while also considering inventory.
- **Learning and growth:** whether vendors are **ISO certified** (if you claim ISO, your vendor must be); employee satisfaction and turnover; **average value of purchase order**.

### 41.8 Steps to Implement the Balanced Scorecard in Supply Chain
1. **Define the strategic objective** — cost efficient, responsive, variety, or maximum pin-code coverage; stick to it.
2. **Develop specific matrices** — e.g., if serving all pin codes in India, matrices for lead time by pin code (rural, urban, semi-urban, metro); you cannot ensure one-day delivery in rural areas with fragmented demand.
3. **Collect the data** — record lead time, inventory cost, transportation cost per unit per kilometre, manufacturing cost per unit; record total cost produced.
4. **Review and adjust** — target the areas contributing maximum cost; use the **80-20 rule/Pareto analysis** (address the areas contributing 60–70%).
5. **Foster collaboration** — implementation happens through stakeholders.
- **Benefits of using the balanced scorecard:** holistic approach (all functional units and stakeholders), **strategic alignment**, enhanced decision making (facts and figures), continuous improvement, and sustainability.

### 41.9 Quality Performance Indices
- **Percentage of rejection** = defects received / total items received (e.g., **10% rejection** of raw material; from the market **5% of products come back** because they were damaged during shipment).
- **Defects per million opportunities** — used to implement **6 sigma**, where **3.4 defects per million opportunities** are allowed; calculate as number of defects found / total items tested.
- **Supplier rejection percentage** = number of rejections / total number of suppliers (e.g., **3%**).
- **Percentage known compliance** = number of non-compliance / total observations. Example: vendors must send **QC reports** with each lot; of **10 lots only 8 reports** received → non-compliance; measure **non-compliance rectified**.

### 41.10 Delivery Performance Indices
- **Supplier on-time and in-full delivery** — order 50 grocery items; 40 delivered in time, 10 not available = not in full; calculate percentage in full and in time.
- **Percentage delivery in full on time** = delivery in full percentage − rejected percentage (delivered full on time but 5 of 50 returned due to defects).
- **Supplier lead time variability** — e.g., stated 3 times delivery time **±1 day**.
- **Overall lead time** — actual delivery time / required lead time.
- **Percentage of shipment arriving in good condition** — good shipments vs defective shipments.
- **Average delivery time** = sum over periods from dispatch to receipt / total number of shipments.

### 41.11 Cost Performance Indices
- **Cost performance index** = **earned value / actual value**. If equal to 1, the project is exactly on budget; if **greater than 1, under budget** (cost efficient); if spending extra, the factor is more than one.
- **Earned value = planned value × percentage complete**; **planned value** = authorized budget assigned; **actual cost** = total cost incurred for actual work performed.
- **Cost avoidance** = **actual purchase price − lowest quoted purchase price** (actual price 100, lowest quoted 80, but vendor with 90 picked → cost reduction = 100 − 90).
- **Procurement ROI** = (**cost reduction + cost avoidance**) / cost of procurement operations.
- **Procurement cost as percentage of sales** — if procurement adds **30%** cost and other industries maintain **28%**, you are **2% less cost efficient** → negotiate better.

### 41.12 Techniques to Measure Supply Chain Performance
- **Inventory investment** as a percentage of gross/net revenue (e.g., **10% inventory** added to gross); optimize via **sales and operation planning**.
- **Value stream mapping** (delete non-value-adding activities), **quick change over / SMED (Single-Minute Exchange of Die)**, and **kanban** (red-green-yellow bins, visual replenishment).
- **Inventory efficiency/inventory turnover** = annualized cost of goods sold / average inventory investment; **days of supply** = inventory investment / daily cost of goods sold.
- Exclude excessive and obsolete inventory (do not hold inventory required only after 60/90/100 days).
- The **7 wastes** (extra transportation, extra inventory, extra motion/handling, waiting time, over-production, over-processing, defects) are addressed; **kanban** enhances JIT.
- **On-time delivery performance** — how many orders are on time vs late; implement **Lean Six Sigma**.
- **5S housekeeping**, **TPM** (total productive maintenance so equipment does not go down), **poka-yoke (mistake-proofing)**, **jidoka**.
- **Unplanned orders** carry more cost (not forecast) — minimize by accurate demand prediction, SOP, sales and operation planning, and customer collaboration; **schedule change** from raw-material shortage/equipment failure reduces efficiency.
- **Overdue backlog, material availability, excess and obsolete inventory, customer service target, perfect order** (complete quantity, quality, accurate recording).
- **Gross profit margin = revenue − cost of goods sold**; **return on assets (ROA)**; **gross margin return on investment** (return on technology investment).

### 41.13 Forecasting Accuracy
- Measure deviation between actual demand and forecast demand. If a **1-time +5 and another-time −5 error**, adding gives **0**, so use **average absolute deviation** (consider magnitude only; total deviation 10).
- **Root mean square error (RMSE)** = square the deviations, sum, divide by n, take the root.
- Improve with **sales and operation planning**, **time-series analysis**, and **regression analysis**.

### 41.14 Case Study — ShipBob
- **ShipBob** is a leading **technology-enabled third-party logistics provider** that implemented the balanced scorecard approach.
- **KPIs selected:** **perfect order rate** (complete quantity and quality, no returns), **supply chain cost** (warehousing, transportation, last-mile delivery), **order fulfill rate** (do not say no to a customer), **lead time** (minimize when suppliers are near), **supplier relationship**, and **cash-to-cash cycle** (deliver within one day → generate cash in one day).
- They updated warehouses with a **warehouse management system** and optimized **labor, storage, and transportation cost**.
- **Conclusion:** the balanced scorecard is a comprehensive framework that tracks not only financial but also non-financial measures, includes all departments (value-adding and non-value-adding) working towards one strategy that hits the vision; tap KPIs, set achievable targets, and take initiatives.

## Lecture 42 — Importance of Distribution in Supply Chain

### 42.1 Why the Distribution Network Matters
- The distribution network ensures the **final end-mile delivery** of products and services; it is the **point of contact** directly in touch with the end customer/consumer, so customer information, feedback, and preferences are recorded there.
- Designing the distribution network starts by fixing two broad parameters: the **level of services** provided and the **cost** (trade-off between them).
- **Supply chain structure:** raw-material suppliers → manufacturers → customer; in between we manage movement of raw material (supplier→manufacturer) and finished goods (manufacturer→end customer).
- **Distribution cost is the logistics cost** = transportation cost + warehousing cost + storage cost + facility-setup cost. (Total supply chain cost also includes material acquisition, manufacturing, and distribution cost.)

### 42.2 Different Players, Different Strategies
- **Walmart** — low cost, huge variety, fair quality; **Dell** — responsive, customized PCs; **Zara** — quickly replenishing design patterns, not competing on cost efficiency; **7-Eleven** Japanese store — changes shelves quickly, morning and evening merchandise differ, includes local merchandise.
- Players with different distribution strategies: **Amazon, ShopClues, Walmart, Flipkart, eBay, Myntra, Meesho, ASIO** — one distribution network is not sufficient even for one industry; align the chosen network with the strategic goal (cost efficient → hit cost parameters).

### 42.3 Level of Services (Service Factors)
- **Response time** — time taken to respond to a customer query (customized products let the customer wait; standardized products need very less response time).
- **Product variety** — e.g., **Maruti Suzuki**: petrol **VXi, LXi, ZXi** (minimum 6 models per fuel type) × diesel/hybrid/electric (24 models) × colours (2 → 48, 10 → 240) × engine capacities (1200 cc/1600 cc → double); such variety is difficult to maintain offline, so can be maintained online (minimum **2–3 months** waiting), while a famous mid-model like **Grand Vitara VXi** is delivered quickly.
- **Product availability** — always in stock; online can show availability via **virtual inventory**; high-demand products (e.g., **Apple iPhone**) or seasonal offers on Flipkart/Amazon cause out-of-stock when people consume more than actual demand.
- **Customer experience** — doorstep delivery enhances experience; e.g., at **Spencer** you pick items and ask them to deliver boxes to your doorstep.
- **Time to market** — how soon after launch the product reaches the market; online is available everywhere immediately, whereas offline may take **10–20 days** to reach end level.
- **Order visibility** — tracking (reached Gurgaon hub → Calcutta hub → Kharagpur → out for delivery), especially online.
- **Returnability** — the distribution network must easily process returns and credit the customer; online return rate is **25–30%**, and **60–70% of customers check the return option** before buying.

### 42.4 Two Broad Phases of Design
1. **Visualize the broad structure** — e.g., furniture market: you must feel wood quality, finish, colour before finalizing (furniture stays **15–20 years**); routine grocery like **Tata salt** — offline or online does not matter as long as it reaches safely.
2. **Convert the broad structure into specific equations** — offline requires a store in Kharagpur; online accepts orders and delivers anywhere (Calcutta, Delhi, Bangalore, Ahmedabad, overseas). Choice of distribution network has a significant impact on profitability and becomes a **USP** (Dell's customized product via website + local retailer).

### 42.5 Key Relationships — Number of Facilities
- **Number of facilities ↑ → response time ↓**: one centralized facility in **Kolkata** covering 700–1000 km takes **3–5 days**; facilities near each market (covering ≤50 km) deliver quickly.
- **Number of facilities ↑ → inventory cost ↑**: inventory maintained at many locations instead of one.
- **Number of facilities vs transportation cost:** with a two-leg structure (100 km and 10 km), **inbound logistics cost** (coming in) versus **outbound logistics cost** (shipping out); **outbound is always higher than inbound**, so minimize outbound distance by placing facilities near the customer. Transportation cost **first decreases** as facilities increase, then **increases after a point** because full economies of scale are lost — a **10-ton truck** versus small **2-ton trucks** when a customer orders only **1–1.5 ton**.
- **Furniture table example:** a finished table lets a truck carry only **10–20 tables**, but if disassembled, more than **1000 tables'** material can be shipped and reassembled at the customer end (as done by Amazon/Flipkart).
- **Amazon warehouse example:** receives full truckload shipments of books inbound, sets up small centers near customers, consolidates books inbound, maintains inventory near the customer, then delivers.
- **Number of facilities ↑ → facility cost ↑** (fixed investment).

### 42.6 Total Logistics Cost
- **Total logistics cost = facility cost + transportation cost + inventory cost** (information cost discussed with network designs).
- As long as economies of scale are enjoyed, cost lowers with the number of facilities; increasing further raises cost (truck capacity unutilized).
- At one point the **total cost and the response time are both minimized** — that number of facilities is the right decision.

### 42.7 Distribution Network Design Options
- **Direct shipping** — manufacturer stores the product, you order online, and they ship directly (e.g., **Dell**); all intermediaries (retailer, wholesaler, distributor, carry-forward agent) removed → cut cost, control quality.
- **Centralized distribution** — **Walmart** maintains inventory at the center and distributes to every market → minimizes inventory cost, cost efficient.
- **Decentralized distribution** — very near the customer, immediate shipping → minimizes response time but increases inventory cost.
- **Cross docking** — no storage, only temporary consolidation (full truck from point A→B, then consolidate orders to a location like Kharagpur in smaller trucks).
- **Drop shipping** — order placed online, product dropped at your doorstep; best when inventory is at the manufacturing location; not suitable for customized/variety products.
- **Hybrid distribution** — mix centralized and decentralized (e.g., set up a distribution center in **Midnapore** where population is huge to enjoy economies of scale, serve **Kharagpur** from Midnapore or Calcutta); hybrid can be offline + online (Dell).
- **Third-party logistics provider** — 1PL to 6PL; outsource when unsure about order scalability, market, logistics expertise, or courier partners.

### 42.8 Impact of Online Sales
- **Opportunities:** huge access to the market via fulfillment centers/warehouses (e.g., Flipkart's fulfillment center near Kharagpur or Calcutta). **Challenges:** covering the geographical reach, rural distribution where minimum orders come.
- **Inventory management models (online):**
  1. **Inventory-led model** — Amazon fulfillment center in Calcutta covers ~**500 km** and maintains inventory.
  2. **Zero-inventory model** — no inventory; on receiving the order, process from the manufacturer and supply via cross docking.
  3. **Retailer storage model** — tie up with local vendors (e.g., **Hitachi** retailer stores the AC inventory).
- **Last-mile delivery, order processing and packaging, return management** (returns are also inventory that must be updated), **technology and automation** (WMS, distribution routing system), **customer experience, scalability and flexibility**.
- **IIT Kharagpur campus example:** population ~**5,000** rises to **25,000–30,000** when students return → orders on Zomato/Swiggy scale up.
- **Integration with physical stores** — Zomato maintains no inventory, just ties up with local restaurants; Ola is another example.
- **Data analytics** — online players collect customer footfall/viewing data to target advertisements and highlight brands.

### 42.9 Impact on Customer Services and Cost
- **Customer services:** physical product response time **2–3 days** (digital product instant, e.g., antivirus key); huge product variety (only upload images); high product availability; **24×7** convenience (e.g., Amazon Fresh bread and butter by 8 a.m.); geographical reach; personalization; faster time to market; order visibility; easy returns; direct sales-to-customer feedback; flexible pricing/promotions (Big Billion Days, Prime Day → see **50–60% rise** in demand); efficient fund transfer (COD liquidates assets immediately vs offline credit sales).
- **Cost — inventory:** aggregate inventory in centralized locations; high-demand items (Amazon books) hold huge inventory near the customer, low-demand books printed on order (**postponement**).
- **Cost — facility:** centralization reduces cost; **Netflix operates ~50 warehouses versus Blockbuster's more than 1000 retail stores**; grocery stores (Spencer, Big Bazaar, Kirana) let the customer pick, reducing labor cost.
- **Cost — transportation:** digital goods cost nothing; non-digital aggregated inventory reduces outbound logistics cost while individual-order processing increases it.
- **Cost — information and infrastructure:** a strong information system for visibility/tracking; **Amazon acquired Zappos → access to more than 1 million products**, requiring more than **2 million photographs** on the website.

## Lecture 43 — Designing Distribution Networks in Supply Chain

### 43.1 Six Distribution Network Design Options
- Based on firm/industry/product nature and the required services; one end is **cost**, the other is **responsiveness** — for cost, consolidate inventories/orders and use economies of scale; for responsiveness, provide customized services and deliver individual products.
- The **six designs:** (1) manufacturer storage with direct shipping; (2) manufacturer storage with direct shipping and in-transit merge; (3) distributor storage with carrier delivery; (4) distributor storage with last-mile delivery; (5) manufacturer/distributor storage with customer pickup; (6) retail storage with customer pickup.
- Each design is evaluated on **cost parameters** (inventory, transportation, facilities & handling, information) and **service parameters** (response time, product variety, availability, customer experience, time to market, order visibility, returnability). Dotted lines = information flow; solid lines = product flow.

### 43.2 Design 1 — Manufacturer Storage with Direct Shipping (Drop Shipping)
- Manufacturer maintains its own storage facility/inventory, so the **manufacturing location is central** → inventory cost minimized; customer orders directly with the manufacturer → more control/visibility and direct control over quality, delivery, packaging.
- You must **arrange the shipment** and set up your own distribution network (requires logistics expertise); benefits: save cost, **higher margin** (not shared with a delivery partner), control over inventory, quality, delivery, and customer experience.
- Heavy-item example (**home appliances, furniture, tiles, marble**): visit the retail shop, choose design/colour/quality, retailer records the order, and the manufacturer ships directly to avoid handling damage — the same as **marble** pieces breaking during loading/unloading. Order visibility IT platform needed.
- **Cost:** inventory lower (centralized); **transportation very high** (outbound increased; individual orders over 1000 km); facilities and handling lower; **information huge investment** (exact order information must pass through the retailer).
- **Services:** response time very long (**4–5 days**); product variety very high; product availability very high (all models at one location); customer experience good (home delivery) but not for products the customer wants to interact with (online furniture penetration only **1–2%**); time to market very fast; returnability expensive/difficult (product returns to manufacturer, but nearby return inventory can be kept as a small deviation from the pure strategy).

### 43.3 Design 2 — Manufacturer Storage with Direct Shipping and In-Transit Merge
- Similar to drop shipping, but orders from **multiple sellers/vendors are merged in transit** at the fulfillment center and **one delivery** is made (e.g., **Amazon Fresh** order of 50 grocery items from four vendors arrives at different times; the center waits and merges them).
- Requires a very strong **communication and collaboration network** and **real-time tracking** (if the fourth vendor is not tracked, items are delayed and customer experience suffers).
- **Cost:** inventory similar to drop shipping (manufacturer holds); **transportation somewhat lower** (multiple orders consolidated into one delivery); **facilities and handling higher** (a consolidation storage house and tracking of incoming orders); **information strong investment**.
- **Services:** response time similar to drop shipping; product variety and availability same (high); **customer experience enhanced** (single delivery instead of multiple); time to market and order visibility same; returnability similar to drop shipping.

### 43.4 Design 3 — Distributor Storage with Carrier Delivery
- Instead of the manufacturer, the **distributor stores the inventory** and manages receiving, storing, and shipping; products transported to customers via **package carriers or 3PL providers**. The manufacturer loses some control over inventory, delivery, and quality.
- **Cost:** inventory cost higher (multiple distributor locations near customers); **transportation lower than manufacturer storage** (outbound distance reduced); facilities and handling higher; **information structure simpler** (one or two more players introduced, less investment).
- **Services:** response time less (faster delivery); product variety reduced and availability reduced (variety maintained at distributor locations, sometimes all models not available); customer experience better than the first two (can visit the distributor and touch/feel the product); time to market higher (**10–15 days**); order visibility easier (more intermediaries); returnability easier (returned to the local distributor, not 1000 km away).
- **Benefits:** distributor may add value-adding services (consolidation, kitting, packaging) and enables efficient distribution across geographies and higher product variety.

### 43.5 Design 4 — Distributor Storage with Last-Mile Delivery
- Distributor stores the product at different locations and delivers to the doorstep (e.g., **Amazon**); distributing responsibilities include picking, packing, recording, and processing the order; last leg may be the distributor's own network or a 3PL.
- **Cost:** inventory higher (multiple locations); transportation high for individual orders but **outbound logistics cost reduced** (local inventory); facilities and handling higher (consolidation at a local facility hub); a strong information network not essential but many players require a transparent information channel.
- **Services:** response time very quick (local inventory); product variety lesser than options 1–3; **customer experience very good** (direct shipping); time to market more; order visibility implementable (Amazon/Flipkart/Walmart kind of system); returnability easy (click return on the platform, returned to the distributor/retailer).
- **Decisions:** efficient **route planning** (maximize order delivery on a route), managing delivery personnel, maintaining delivery infrastructure, ensuring customer communication through end-mile delivery. Distributor may offer **installation, assembly, product customization**.

### 43.6 Design 5 — Manufacturer/Distributor Storage with Customer Pickup
- Distributor/retailer stores inventory and there are **customer pickup sites** (e.g., one center in Kharagpur); customers not available at their address (at office or elsewhere) can consolidate orders and pick them up at a convenient store within 2–3 days.
- **Cost:** inventory maintained; **transportation lower** (no home delivery); facilities and handling high (nearby pickup locations holding inventory); information investment required (consolidate orders and update the customer that orders reached the pickup location).
- **Services:** response time higher; product variety little lesser; availability lesser; **customer experience lower** (no home delivery); time to market more; order visibility difficult (but the virtual network tracks the product); returnability easier if the pickup location handles returns.

### 43.7 Design 6 — Retail Storage with Customer Pickup Sites
- The local routine item: go to a shop and pick the product (retailer maintains inventory; sometimes out of stock, sometimes excess). The best feature is the **cross-selling opportunity** — e.g., "shop for **3500** get a coupon for **2000**" or "order up to **5000** get a bag/free item".
- Parameters are similar to Design 5 (retailer/distributor storing); the difference: at a retail shop you may face an out-of-stock item, but at a customer pickup site you are sure the order reached the site and you will receive it.

### 43.8 Evaluating Design Options and Dell Case Study
- The **survey/rating (Chopra and Meindl)** rates designs 1 (highest preferred) to 6 (poor). **Response time highest performance** = retail storage with customer pickup (**within 10 seconds** at the shop); online orders take minimum 2–4 days. **Product variety/availability** minimum at a retail shop; online always has availability/variety.
- For a **high-demand product**, prefer **retail shop with customer pickup** (store high inventory, known consumption); low customer effort, quality, variety, size, and configuration each favor different networks.
- **Dell case study (online sales of computer hardware):**
  - **Product customization** — Dell leverages the internet to record the order/specification; the customer waits **2–3 days** for a customized PC, but standardized products are expected immediately.
  - **Time to market** — launch anytime (even midnight); **cost efficiency** via centralized location; **24×7** service; **cash flow** quickly (COD handed over on receipt of cash).
  - **Cost:** inventory aggregated at the Dell store/manufacturing location (centralized inventory cost minimum) with **postponement** (reactive process — assemble only on receiving the order); facility cost low; **information cost heavy**; **transportation very high** (direct from manufacturer).
  - **Performance:** high-valued customized hardware; online sales significantly improve responsiveness and reduce cost (centralized inventory + postponement). **Challenge:** online sales are less pronounced, the inventory-reduction benefit is not as impactful, and **responsiveness is poor** (2–3 days).
- **Conclusion:** Dell's online model offers substantial advantage for **high-value customized hardware**; standardized products suit offline retail where inventory can be consumed any day. No pure strategy — blend design options according to industry/product, ensuring quality and timely delivery.

## Lecture 44 — Facility Location Planning

### 44.1 Importance and Strategic Nature
- Decide **where to locate** production facilities, warehouses, vendors, and fulfillment centers; a major factor is **minimizing total transportation distance** (→ minimizes transportation cost) and being **near the end market** for quick response — but you cannot set up a facility for every market.
- Facility location planning is a combination of **operational research** (find an initial basic feasible solution, then an optimal solution that minimizes distance/cost) and **computational geometry**.
- For **hazardous material**, avoid being near society (spreading infection).
- Location decisions are **strategic** with long-term impact (**20–30 years or more**; e.g., the IIT KGP campus is not relocated); they affect profitability/productivity and are **capital intensive** — once invested, decisions cannot easily be reversed. **Tata Nano plant in Singur, West Bengal** example: huge initial investment, political issues and farmer agitation meant the location was not fixed properly.

### 44.2 Consequences of Poor Location Decisions
- **Sell the facility** to a competitor/another industry → the same infrastructure may not be feasible (machinery won't work, manpower to be transported) → damage to brand image.
- **Close down and liquidate assets** → immediate cash inflow but assets sold at a lower cost (book value); book value of property may stay or rise, other assets lose value.
- **Managing shutdown** — lay off employees, comply with regulatory requirements, difficult transition.
- **Continue operations** — others make **5% profit** on the same product while you make **1–2%** or struggle even for that.
- **Relocate** — liquidating/selling some assets or moving machines; unnecessary transition cost, defects/damages, and uncertainty. Reasons for considering a new location: new service center, new production facility, new vendor/warehouse location (e.g., to ensure same-day delivery within a 100 km region), or even an educational institution.

### 44.3 Three-Tier Model of Assessing Location Competitiveness
1. **Country competitiveness** — political factor (support with customs-duty concessions, welcoming tax structure as India is doing), technological aspect (technology available or imported), operational (open to international trade, policies).
2. **Sector competitiveness** — other players in the sector (real estate, hotel, manufacturing, automobile); starting an automobile unit where no player exists raises the question of raw material (a car needs more than **500 vendors**).
3. **Company competitiveness** — skill labor, infrastructure, technology; if not available, collaborate (e.g., **Hero with Honda, Maruti with Suzuki**).

### 44.4 Factors Affecting Facility Location Decisions
- **Availability of minimum primary infrastructure** — road connectivity, electricity, IT infrastructure, air connectivity, water facility (thousands of people associated with the location need these).
- **Disaster scenario** — natural disasters, cloud-bursting, floods, earthquakes; **Japanese organizations** come up with such infrastructure since they are earthquake-prone. **Nissan case study:** resumed operations within **44–45 days** after an earthquake.
- **Tax structure** — property tax, corporate income tax, sales tax; a **single sales tax structure** vs multiple tax-collection points.
- **Labor availability and quality** — human skills required to operate the plant.
- **Construction cost and quality** — educational institutions set up slightly away from the city because city construction/land cost and initial investment are high (compromise on city infrastructure).
- **IT industry** — needs quality of life.
- **Economic development incentives** — states promote particular industries. **Pharmaceutical industry:** India has almost **20% share of the world market**; produces more than **60% of the world's vaccines**; Himachal's **Baddi** area provides incentives and contributes **30–35%** of the country's production to generate employment.
- **Quality of life** — education, healthcare, sports, cultural facilities for families.
- **International factors:** transportation (overseas demand/manufacturing), economic stability, taxation policies, exposure to a new market (**Apple** planning manufacturing in India to serve Indian demand), environmental regulations/sustainability, political and legal structure, **IPR** enforcement, ease of doing business, social factors (local culture aligning with organization culture; demographic trends — India is the **youngest population**), technological factors (R&D/innovation infrastructure), supply chain/proximity to suppliers (e.g., China), proximity to customers (Apple), operational flexibility (scale up/down at minimum cost), avoiding language/communication barriers, healthcare and education facilities.
- **PESTEL analysis** (political, economic, social, legal, technological) plus factors like raw-material availability and rail infrastructure (heavy industry).

### 44.5 Options for Locating Facilities
- **Separate facilities for different products/services** — e.g., **ICICI Bank** services, mutual funds, and loans in separate facilities in the same city; **P&G** has one facility for household cleaning products and another for personal care items; specialized facilities provide more quality focus.
- **Separate facilities for different geographic regions** — Calcutta, Delhi; automobile industry; **Amazon** fulfillment centers near big markets to reduce distance and transportation cost.
- **Separate facilities for different processes** — molding, assembly, packaging units (molding may supply plastic components to the assembly line and also to competitors); **Tesla** — Giga factory in **Nevada** focuses on batteries/energy storage while the **Fremont factory in California** is dedicated to vehicle assembly; dedicated operations are streamlined and more efficient.

### 44.6 Qualitative Models
- **Comparison chart analysis model** — locations **A, B, C** against factors **1…n**; assign a scale (5 to 1, 7 to 1, 9 to 1, or 10 to 1) where 5 means very good/highest efficiency; critical subjective factors: quality of life, availability of raw material, education, healthcare, infrastructure (electricity, water, road).
- **Point rating method** — rate locations 1 to 10 against each factor (e.g., factor related to raw-material supplier availability: **A = 9, B = 7, C = 8**); the highest total score is the best facility (a qualitative judgment).
- **Factor rating method** — assign **weights** to factors (e.g., 3, 2, 1) and multiply each score by its weight (9×4, 6×2, 7×1, 5×3, 6×1); the highest weighted total score wins.

### 44.7 Quantitative Models
- **Weighted point-rating model** — assign weights (total should be **100% or 1**); example weights: **land cost 20%, construction cost 20%, proximity to customers 15%, proximity to highways 15%**; multiply each location's rating by the weight and pick the highest.
- **Simple median model** — find coordinates of the new location having the **minimum rectilinear distance** from old locations. Example: locations with x-y coordinates (e.g., **Kanpur at (50,10)**), cost of moving one unit one unit distance **₹10**, annual load for each plant. Find the **median of the total load** (e.g., **1432**); move along the x-axis, adding loads — Bareilly **452**, +Shahjahanpur **453 = 1130** (not covered), +Kanpur covers the median → x = **50**; along the y-axis (Gonda) → y = **50**; the new facility is at **(50,50)**. Total transportation cost = cost per unit distance × annual load × rectilinear distance (e.g., 10 × 452 × distance).
- **Center of gravity method** — new x coordinate = **Σ xi·li / Σ li**; y coordinate = **Σ yi·li / Σ li** (weightage assigned to all serving locations/suppliers). Examples: **sugar mills** near sugarcane farms, **tea factories** near Assam tea-leaf growing areas, **crude-oil companies** near maximum crude-oil raw material.
- **Varignon's framework** — nodes (raw-material suppliers/markets) tied with a **common thread** to a common node; high demand → high weight; the common node moves toward the higher weight and comes to equilibrium on a **frictionless surface** (the new location). This model uses **Euclidean distance** = √[(x1−x2)² + (y1−y2)²], whereas the median model uses rectilinear distance.
- **Conclusion:** facility location is a critical strategic, long-term investment; once chosen you cannot withdraw without huge loss to brand/product/services. Evaluate all micro and macro factors (PESTEL) and industry competitiveness; **Amazon and Toyota** have successfully implemented these techniques.

## Lecture 45 — Demand Forecasting

### 45.1 Introduction and Context (World Bank Data on India)
- After deciding the location, the next step is **demand forecasting** — only after predicting demand can capacity be designed (how large/big the location should be).
- India's growth rate in **FY 2022 and 2023 was 7.2%** — the **second highest among G20 countries** and almost **twice the average for emerging-market economies**, underpinned by robust domestic demand, strong public and private investment; bank credit growth increased to **15.8%** from **13.3%**; GDP predicted **more than 6%**.
- **Expenditure approach:** private investment spending grew **10.6% year over year**; private consumption improved to **3.5%**.
- **Production/supply-chain perspective:** **gross value added (GVA) growing 6.5%** YoY (manufacturing), construction **9.5%**, services **7–8%**, agriculture contracting **0.8%**.
- The **gap between GDP and GVA** (GDP higher) indicates **demand is high but supply is not enough** → imports; hence the need to predict demand so facilities are ready for future challenges.
- **Demand forecasting** is the process of **understanding and predicting customer demand**; it drives supplier replenishment, storage, manufacturing capacity, and distribution-network design.

### 45.2 The Demand Function
- Define the demand function clearly: demand depends on economic condition, price of the product, price of the competitive product, inflation rate, price of complementary goods, and many other factors.
- Configure the demand function carefully for your industry/product so that neither over-production nor stock-out occurs.

### 45.3 Factors Affecting Demand
- **Seasonality** — summer demand for ACs, water coolers, fans; winter demand for woollens (peak demand cannot be met by unlimited capacity expansion; perishable items cannot be piled up).
- **Competition** — **Nokia** was once the Indian market leader (relied on for after-sales service) but battery issues destroyed it; **Jio** offers pulled down other cellular networks (Vodafone collaborated but could not compete).
- **Geography** — a low-floor car may not work in a hilly area; cosmetics/creams differ by weather (Kharagpur/Calcutta vs hilly areas).
- **Economy** — during the **2008 recession** and the **COVID pandemic**, demand for automobiles and luxury products fell while demand for low-price/basic goods (grocery) rose and prices shot up.
- **Types of goods** — perishable goods need accurate prediction; matured products (automobiles, data services/network) have relatively fixed demand and planned phase-out/transition.

### 45.4 Advantages of Demand Forecasting
- **Plan inventory** — avoid extra inventory, carrying cost, holding cost, and extra maintenance effort; avoid stock-out.
- **Cost reduction** — reduce inventory, production, transportation, and distribution cost.
- **Customer satisfaction** — be available in the market and retain loyal customers.
- **Production planning and resource allocation** — prepare extra resources for a peak season or build inventory for later.
- **Marketing and promotion** — know which product to campaign more and which to restrict; plan marketing budget.
- **Financial planning** — how much to invest in raw material, capacity, production, warehouses, distribution.
- **Risk mitigation** — regularly forecast, spot fluctuations early and mitigate.
- **Strategic decision making** — where to locate a warehouse/distribution center (e.g., set up one in Kharagpur if demand is high there); new product development, market and capacity expansion; **supply-chain optimization**; **seasonal planning** (scale up/down); competitive edge.

### 45.5 Challenges of Demand Forecasting
- **Lack of data/resources** — insufficient data or resources to collect enough data → inaccurate prediction.
- **Limited visibility into future** — forecasting extrapolates historical trends; some risk always remains.
- **Technological change** — a new technology may make current inventory/production outdated and unsellable.
- **Inaccurate demand models** — choosing the wrong qualitative/quantitative model.
- **Poor forecasting process** — collecting only **500 samples vs 5000 or 50000** gives different results.
- **Insufficient resources** — collecting 50,000 data points needs technology and investment.
- **PESTLE/external factors** — political, economic, social, legal, ethical, technological factors cannot be predicted.

### 45.6 Types of Demand Forecasting
- **Passive demand forecasting** — does not consider the dynamic environment; assumes last year's demand continues (for steady demand).
- **Active demand forecasting** — for startups/new companies with new products/ventures or R&D on an old product; requires active consideration of the dynamic environment and quick updates.
- **Short-term** (less than 1 year), **medium-term** (1–2 years), **long-term** (more than 2 years); depends on industry nature — entertainment (movie/web series critical for the initial 1–2 days/weeks), fashion (short-term), capacity expansion (5 years), overall blueprint (10–20 years targeting the vision).
- **Micro-level** (within the industry — competing products, complementary products, raw-material prices) and **macro-level** (PESTEL).

### 45.7 Qualitative Techniques
1. **Delphi method** — a group of **10–15 (selection up to 10–50) experts** sits around a round table; pick experts in the relevant industry (automobile, 2-wheeler/4-wheeler). Steps: selection of experts → **questionnaire design** (close-ended/open-ended) → **first round independent inputs** (unbiased) → **feedback analysis/summary** → circulate inputs of all experts → repeat until **consensus** → final report/forecast.
2. **Sales-force composite forecast (pooled sales-force composite method)** — used for retail-consumed products (FMCG, food, construction — cement, steel rods, bricks). Each salesperson covers ~10 markets and 20–25 retailers, recording daily inventory depletion, competitor depletion, and market demand; data compiled region → district → state → country → continent (for an MNC).
3. **Expert knowledge** — interviews with experts, listed questions (close/open-ended), discussion points; includes **focused group discussion** or **brainstorming**; experts predict technological development for the next 5 years.
4. **Survey method** — define the objective (predict demand), design the questionnaire (multiple-choice, **Likert scale**, ranking, open-ended), select samples (different markets/consumers), collect the survey (online — **Monkey Surveys, Google** — telephonic, face-to-face, email), then analyze, interpret, tabulate, and draw diagrams.

### 45.8 Quantitative Techniques
- **Simple moving average** — use the latest data (e.g., 3-month moving average: 150 + 160 + 170 divided by 3 for March; for April take February–April, for May take March–May); gives equal weightage to the last three months.
- **Weighted moving average** — give the highest priority to the latest month (e.g., 0.5 to March, 0.3 to February, 0.2 to January); demand for March = 150×0.2 + 160×0.3 + 170×0.5; a 1,2,3,4 scale can also be used (4 = latest month highest weightage).
- **Exponential moving average** — gives weightage and captures fluctuations; unique feature: it incorporates **all past data points** but needs only **one month's data**; **smoothing factor α = 2 / (n + 1)** (for n = 3, α = **0.5**); forecast for month t = α × actual + (1 − α) × last month's predicted demand (e.g., 0.5 × 170 + 0.5 × 155).
- **Trend projection** — plot historical data and find the best-fit line (**linear/simple linear regression, multiple linear regression, or polynomial regression**); extrapolate for the next month but only up to the maximum scale considered.
- **Simple linear regression** — equation **y = b0 + b1·x**; **b1 is the slope** (per-unit change in independent variable affects the dependent variable b1 times; e.g., **0.55**); **b0 (y-intercept) = ȳ − b1·x̄**; example: number of **TV ads** (independent) vs **cars sold** (dependent). Exponential trend model uses **a·e^(b·x)** (b = growth rate, a = initial value); polynomial equations are used when a straight line does not fit.

## Lecture 46 — Capacity Planning

### 46.1 Concept and Need
- **Capacity planning** determines how much production capacity is required to meet **changing demand** for products/services; the demand is not continuous (small fluctuations, saturation, increase, or decline).
- It balances available resources (space, raw material, manpower, machines) to optimally satisfy customer demand and ensure maximum utilization.
- **Project example:** constructing a building takes **3 years**; evaluate monthly against plan. Outcomes: (1) moving as per plan (rarely happens); (2) **ahead of plan** — deploying extra resources (using **7 manpower where 5 suffice**) is not productive/efficient; (3) **behind plan** — shortage of manpower, raw material, or machines.
- Production capacity, strategy planning, and project planning go hand in hand; limited resources must be optimally utilized.

### 46.2 Time Horizons of Capacity Planning
- **Long term** — strategic decisions: hiring workforce, setting up facilities within the supply chain. Example: **Walmart** entering the Indian market needs stores, storage houses, distribution centers, warehouses, and (if producing) production facilities; expand step by step (central India → north/east/west). A warehouse set up in Calcutta may continue for **50 years or more**; if demand rises, expand its capacity.
- **Short term** — day-to-day/reactive decisions: cover a manpower shortage on one line due to absenteeism by moving **2–3 extra people** from another line, or shift manpower from packaging product A to product B.
- **Medium term** — scheduling, forecasting, utilization tracking, strategic planning; for **seasonal demand** (4–6 months, summer/winter) build up ready inventory during the off-season.

### 46.3 Measuring Facility Capacity (Classroom Example)
- **Design capacity** — maximum capacity under ideal conditions, running **24×7** with no stoppage (if the machine permits).
- **System capacity** — lower than design capacity; accounts for **expected variations** (changeover time, initial defects, routine maintenance downtime, lunch/snacks/coffee/tea breaks).
- **Actual output** — lower than system capacity; accounts for **unexpected variations** (interrupted raw-material supply, machine breakdown, high absenteeism due to bad weather/weekend).
- **IIT Kharagpur classroom example:** design capacity **90 students** (exam hall); with a stage, board, and projector installed, the **effective capacity** drops to **~75** (space between chairs and stage); on a normal day only **50–60** turn up → 15 chairs unutilized.
- **Efficiency = actual output / effective capacity × 100**; **Utilization = actual output / design capacity**; **Effective capacity = design capacity − allowances**. Aim for efficiency around **95% or more** (100% impossible due to changeovers/breakdowns).

### 46.4 Numerical Example
- Design capacity **230 units per hour**; running **7 days × 3 shifts × 8 hours = 168 hours** → 168 × 230 = **38,640 units**; effective capacity **28,000**; **utilization 64.7%**, **efficiency 89.3%**.
- Utilization cannot be much improved when restricted by space, but efficiency should reach ~95%.

### 46.5 Types of Capacity Planning
- **Workforce capacity planning (manpower)** — resources are the **5 M's: manpower, money, machine, material, methods (technology)**; if 100 people are required, absenteeism/permanent exits mean 100 are not always present; capacity = number of workers × hours (e.g., 5 people × 8 hours = **40 hours**).
- **Product capacity planning** — number of products/resources needed to fulfill deliverables; a **pet store** must provide pet food, toys, equipment, accessories, medicines; an **automobile services provider** also offers cars, financial support, after-sales service, accessories, insurance, financing — a **one-stop solution**.
- **Tool capacity planning** — required machinery, vehicles, assembly line, parts, in the required sequence/timing (linked to sequencing and scheduling).
- **Resource capacity planning** — maximize the existing resources; a machine that can run **24×7** used only **8 hours/1 shift** leaves **16 hours idle** (produce **20,000 instead of 50,000**); with no demand, produce for competitors (molding plants supply plastic parts to **Philips, Havells, L&T, ABB, Usha**).
- **Project capacity planning** — how many resources/machines/manpower required; **Gantt chart** with activities **A1, A2, A3, B3** on a timeline over 3 years; check the fifth week against plan and identify shortfalls (limited resources or resources not used optimally).

### 46.6 Capacity Planning Process
1. **Estimate capacity requirement** — depends on forecasting; define the demand function (population, purchasing power, income, price of product, price of complementary goods, quality, availability).
2. **Evaluate existing capacity** — if 5 machines are required but only 3 exist, buy 2 more; if the fifth machine is utilized only **30%**, find an alternative.
3. **Identify alternatives** — e.g., keep 4 machines and outsource/purchase the fifth's requirement until its capacity can be fully utilized.
4. **Conduct financial analysis** — high initial cost with only 30% utilization is not worth it; buy the component from a vendor for 6 months/1 year.
5. **Assess qualitative issues of the alternative** — employees may feel insecure about outsourcing; organization culture/work hygiene may differ from the other company; mention in the contract where the raw material must be purchased.
6. **Find the best long-term alternative**, balance capacity with requirements, **implement the best selected alternative**, and **monitor results**.

### 46.7 Alternatives to Balance Capacity
- **Increase capacity** — new equipment, more machines/resources.
- **Optimize existing capacity** — e.g., a **movable/temporary stage** (not required during exams) so 15 extra chairs can be set up; avoid brand-image damage from part-time hiring if the organization is ethically strong.
- **Outsource production**, use a **flexible workforce** (temporary/part-time workers).
- **Demand management strategies** — increase price (**rationing**) so customers buy less.
- **Develop a contingency plan** — build up inventory or keep a backup supplier; adopt new fully automated technology to increase output/productivity.
- Also **public opinion/stakeholder reaction** to the alternative.
- **Growth patterns:** if declining, introduce a new product to use the capacity; with **cyclic demand** (e.g., educational institutions have a 2-month summer break) use the downtime for **MDPs, FDPs, and training programs** (no fixed investment, only logistics, met from registration fees).

### 46.8 Break-Even Analysis
- **Total cost = fixed cost + variable cost**; **total revenue**; the **break-even point** is where revenue exceeds cost → **break-even quantity**.
- Below the break-even quantity is the **loss region** (better to **outsource**); above it is the **profit region** (produce **in-house**).
- **Break-even quantity = fixed cost / (R − V)**, where **R = revenue per unit** and **V = variable cost per unit**.
- **Pizza example:** break-even quantity **800 pizzas**; the unit is expecting orders of **700** → not profitable → outsource to an existing restaurant that can achieve break-even via other orders.
- **Make-or-buy example:** fixed cost for making + variable cost per unit vs buying at **$150 per unit**; break-even quantity **3200**; producing **1000 units** in-house costs **₹2,60,000** vs buying **₹1,50,000** → buy (also below break-even quantity).
- **Capacity management with break-even:** plant A runs **12 hours** → break-even quantity; expand by a **double shift** to 24 hours (little higher cost, profit still there); then invest in fixed assets (hiring machines/manpower, expanding space, e.g., **90 → 130 capacity**); when all options are exhausted and demand still rises, set up a second plant (plant B), increasing total cost again.

## Lecture 47 — Sequencing & Scheduling

### 47.1 Concepts
- **Sequencing** — the order of processing a set of tasks/jobs (in what order, in what sequence).
- **Scheduling** — sequencing plus **time** (how much time on one machine, movement from one machine to another, and the time required for all jobs on all machines).
- Objective: **minimize total processing time (flow time)**, reduce delays/waiting time throughout the channel, and ensure resource utilization.
- Jobs are always higher in number than the machines available (2, 3, or more); optimally allocate jobs to machines to minimize total cost.

### 47.2 Sequencing Rules (Single Workstation)
- **First In First Out (FIFO)** — jobs A(3 days, due 5), B(4,6), C(2,7), D(6,9), E(1,2). Flow times: 3, 7, 9, 15, 16 (sum **50**); **average flow time = 10 days**; delays: 0, 1, 2, 6, 14 → **average delay 4.6 days**.
- **Last In First Out (LIFO)** — sequence E, D, C, B, A. Flow times: 1, 7, 9, 13, 16; **average flow time = 9.2 days**; delays: 0, 0, 2, 7, 11 → **average delay 4 days**.
- **Shortest Processing Time (SPT)** — the shortest job is processed first; **average flow time 7.2 days**, **average delay 2.4 days**. **Limitation:** a job with the longest time is delayed indefinitely and due dates are ignored (it processes a one-day job with a 15-day due date before a five-day job with a six-day due date).
- **Longest Processing Time (LPT)** — opposite of SPT (descending order); **average delay 6.8 days** (very high).
- **Earliest Due Date (EDD)** — process whichever must be delivered first; sequence E, C, A, B, D; **average delay 2.4 days**.
- **Least Slack** — **slack = due date − processing time**; minimum slack processed first; sequence **5, 2, 4, 1, 3**.
- **Critical Ratio (CR)** — **CR = time remaining until due date / total remaining production time**; minimum CR processed first; sequence **D, A, C, B**.

### 47.3 Advantages and Challenges of Sequencing
- **Advantages:** avoid **bottlenecks**; **adaptable planning** (critical ratio/EDD implement **dynamic programming** — ratios change daily); a set of **standard rules** so no conflicts; **lean production** with reduced waste in decision-making; the customer can adapt the process sequence; prepared with the program if something goes wrong.
- **Challenges:** the environment is dynamic (what was planned yesterday changes today); hard to standardize; limited availability of resources (machines, labour, materials) may force a sequence change and cause delays; the question of **optimality**; large-scale operations (thousands of jobs to shuffle back and forth); balancing prioritization criteria (due dates, job importance, customer requirements) can conflict — e.g., a loyal, frequent client generating revenue may be deprioritized.

### 47.4 Johnson's Rule (Two Machines)
- Used to sequence multiple jobs on multiple machines; allocate start and finish times based on processing time; assume all jobs follow the **same sequence** (first machine, then second, then third); all jobs are prioritized equally.
- **Steps:** list each job's time on Machine 1 and Machine 2 → find the **minimum time** among all jobs → if it is on **Machine 1, place the job first**; if on **Machine 2, place it last** in the sequence → strike it out and repeat until all jobs are placed.
- **Example (jobs A–E):** minimum **1.5** is job **B** on **Machine 2** → last; next minimum **2.2** is job **C** on **Machine 1** → first; then **2.8** (E on Machine 2) → last; then **3.2** (A on Machine 1) → first; finally **D**. **Sequence = C – A – D – E – B**.
- **Time computation:** Machine 1 finishes all jobs in **22 days**; Machine 2 starts after Machine 1's first job (idle initially), completes at **23.5 days** (total makespan); **Machine 1 idle time = 23.5 − 22 = 1.5 days**; **Machine 2 idle time = 6 days** (initial wait plus waiting for a job to finish on Machine 1).

### 47.5 Extension to Three Machines
- Convert the **three-column matrix into two columns** — one for **a + b** and another for **b + c** — and treat them as two machines; apply the same Johnson algorithm.
- **Example:** minimum times (e.g., **8** for jobs **1 and 4**) → place on Machine 1 first; next minimum **9** (Machine 1); next **11** (Machine 1) → **sequence 1, 4, 5, 3, 2**.
- Compute times for all three machines iteratively; example shows Machine 2 taking **35 days** with idle time, and Machine 3 with an initial **8-day** idle. Find idle time and waiting time for each machine.
- If there are more than three machines, use the **assignment problem** (matrix of jobs vs machines with costs) to find the optimal assignment minimizing total cost — covered in a later session.

## Lecture 48 — Transportation Management

### 48.1 Components of Transportation Decisions
- Transportation moves raw material from vendors to the factory, distributes finished goods, and performs doorstep delivery; transportation is a major part of logistics.
- **Four key components:** (1) **load** (what and how much is moving), (2) **mode of transportation**, (3) **flow** (resources moving through the network), and (4) **routing** (most efficient route to minimize total cost).

### 48.2 Key Aspects of Load
- Load requirements cannot be generalized by **weight** alone — **size**, **nature**, and **volume** of the product also matter.
- **Water-tank example:** a **1,000-litre** water tank weighs **~1 ton** but fills a truck rated at **10 tons** because nothing can be kept inside → capacity wasted.
- **Steel-rod example:** uses little space but reaches the **10-ton** maximum load.
- Packaging matters: box size vs product weight, delicate products cannot be handled carelessly, and constraints on stacking.
- Consider demand patterns from different markets, transportation costs, and service requirements; avoid **underutilization** and never exceed capacity — overloading a 10-ton truck increases accidents, failures, vehicle damage, and fuel cost.

### 48.3 Load Size, Weight, Volume, Composition, Packaging
- **Load size** — amount of cargo; determined by product characteristics; specialized equipment for **gases/hazardous liquids** (leak-proof pipeline-type system, no loading/unloading exposure).
- **Load weight** — total mass; both overloading and underloading hinder efficiency; **BS3/BS4 engine restrictions** in some areas (use BS5/BS7).
- **Load volume** — space occupied; standardize packet size, or use **palletization** and **stacking** for uniform single-pallet/uniform-stack size. Online e-commerce faces different packet sizes (a marker/slide pointer vs grocery vs mobile vs home appliance) — Amazon's A-to-Z items have different cubic sizes.
- **Packaging** is the first phase of shipping; damaged packaging gives the impression of a damaged product (people may return without opening); protect against damage, spoilage, and theft.
- Consolidate different sizes/vary packaging to fill space (e.g., put material inside an empty water tank to carry 2–3 tons); keep **heavy products on one side, lighter on the other**, and optimize the **centre of gravity** so load is distributed evenly.

### 48.4 Fleet Selection
- Factors: **geographical coverage** (local restrictions from weather, traffic, terrain), **local conditions** (weather, traffic, cargo characteristics), **cost** (total cost of ownership, not just transportation cost), **technology and optimization** (data-driven route selection, eliminate subjectivity), overall performance parameters configured in software, overloading checks, manpower qualifications and service hours (long driving hours increase accidents).

### 48.5 Modes of Transportation
- **Air** — quick transportation, speed, reliability, global connectivity; reduces lead time; preserves perishables; but very high cost (highest cost).
- **Road** — the only option for doorstep/last-mile delivery; big advantage of **door-to-door**; challenges include traffic, weather, regulatory restrictions (trucks may not operate during daytime in large cities), and higher cost per mile (**₹3–4 per km**); highest carbon emissions.
- **Rail** — suitable for long-distance shipments, bulk cargo, and heavy goods; high capacity, energy efficiency, and cost-effectiveness for large quantities; but not door-to-door (weather, port congestion need road support).
- **Ship/sea** — the **most economical** and most **sustainable** (minimal carbon footprint); best for long distance and bulk; reliable; but infrastructure-dependent and not for end-mile.
- **Challenges:** traffic, road conditions, weather, regulatory restrictions, and **geopolitical considerations**. Often **intermodal** transportation is used based on availability, infrastructure, and requirements.

### 48.6 Logistics Flows
- Three flows: **material**, **information**, and **financial**; plus a **reverse flow**.
- **Financial flow** differs by channel: online e-commerce receives cash only at delivery (COD), while retail credit sales take longer to liquidate assets and risk non-payment.
- **Reverse flow** — products not liked are sent back; returns, repairs, recycling, or disposal (if used) must be managed to maintain customer satisfaction and loyalty.

### 48.7 Route Planning
- Strategic selection of optimal transportation routes depending on distance, time, cost, capacity, and mode. Objective: **minimize total transportation cost and reduce transit time**, optimizing resource utilization.
- A supplier may supply several factories; manage all supplies to minimize total cost using an algorithm that lists sources, records demand, and finds the minimum cost (the transportation algorithm).

### 48.8 Consolidation Example
- Shipment from a plant location to three cities (**Topeka, Kansas, Wichita**); rate **3.42**; day-one shipment **70 pounds** → compute total cost for all three cities. Individual day transportation total cost = **$2,350**.
- Shipping **250 items** lowers per-unit cost, **300 items** lowers further (full truckload of **400**); consolidating orders across days reduces cost but the waiting increases **lead time**.
- **Plant inventory to warehouse inventory example:** product worth **$25 per pound**, plant and carrying costs **30% per year**; use **365 days** to calculate in-transit inventory cost and the average inventory carried.
- Cost comparison: **road transport is minimal, air transport maximal**, but truck transport is **not sustainable** (highest carbon emissions).

### 48.9 Case Studies
- **Polaroid Corporation** uses a **TM (Transportation Management) solution from HighJump Software** to achieve the lowest-cost carrier rates, choosing the best fleet option from the manufacturing plant to the retailer in the US and Canada.
- **Tree of Life** (food-packages distributor) improved **inbound freight efficiency from 55% to 75%** using **JDA Software**.
- **Orange Glow** improved its on-time delivery rate using **Lean Logistics TMS**.
- **Conclusion:** data-driven decisions are necessary to minimize total transportation costs — deploy software for carrier selection, routing, and scheduling.

## Lecture 49 — Transportation Problem Programming

### 49.1 Concept and Objective
- The **transportation problem** is a classical optimization problem: minimize **transportation cost** (or maximize profit) when transporting goods from **m sources** (factories/vendors) to **n destinations** (markets).
- **Transportation cost is the total cost of ownership (TCO)** — transportation cost + wear and tear + operational cost + depreciation of the asset (e.g., **₹10 per unit** to move one product from a factory to a market).
- The same algorithm can apply to vendors serving factories.
- **Decision variables (xij)** — how many units are shipped from factory i to market j; **objective function** — minimize total cost Σ cij·xij; **constraints** — supply (row sum ≤ available supply ai) and demand (column sum = market demand bj).
- The cost function can include distance, path followed, mode of transportation, number of units, and speed.

### 49.2 Assumptions (Many Are Not Practically True)
- **Fixed supply and demand** — practically demand/supply change (10,000 today → 12,000 tomorrow; capacity expanded from 10,000 to 25,000 via extra shifts); plan dynamically.
- **Single commodity/single homogeneous product** — transporting a mobile phone vs a car has different costs; hence the model may not apply as-is to e-commerce, but suits uniform products (e.g., a **Maruti Suzuki plant in Calcutta** transporting cars to markets).
- **Cost linearity** — assumes 1 unit = ₹10, 2 units = ₹20, 100 units = ₹1000; in reality **economies of scale** apply — a pizza delivery boy carrying **1 pizza = ₹100**, but **2 pizzas** cost less per unit (fuel same, maybe an extra 2 km); economies of scale does not hold in the model.
- **Capacity constant** — maximum capacity from each factory and maximum demand from each market are fixed.
- **Non-negative decision variables** — units of xij cannot be negative.
- **Full shipment** — goods are fully transported from source to destination; not e-commerce partial fulfillment (ordering 50 grocery items but receiving 40 the same day).

### 49.3 Mathematical Formulation
- Matrix organisation: rows = sources (factories **S1, S2, … Sm**) with supplies **a1, a2, … am**; columns = destinations (markets **1, 2, … n**) with demands **b1, b2, … bn**; **cij** = cost of shipping one unit from source i to destination j (e.g., C23 = cost from S2 to market 3).
- **xij** = number of units shipped from factory i to market j (e.g., x32 = units from factory 3 to market 2).
- **Objective:** minimize Σ cij·xij subject to supply and demand constraints (allocation in any row ≤ supply; allocation in any column = market demand).

### 49.4 Definitions
- **Feasible solution** — any solution satisfying all conditions. Example: **10 metres of cloth**; a shirt needs **2 m** (profit ₹5), a trouser needs **3 m** (profit ₹7); decide the mix to maximize profit within the limited resource.
- **Basic feasible solution** — in the transportation problem, **m + n − 1 allocations** at independent non-negative positions (order of the matrix minus 1).
- **Non-degenerate basic feasible solution** — exactly m + n − 1 non-negative allocations at independent positions.
- **Degenerate solution** — a basic variable in the feasible solution gets a **0 value**; degeneracy must be removed.
- **Optimal solution** — the feasible solution giving the minimum transportation cost meeting all requirements.

### 49.5 Types and Phases
- **Balanced problem** — total supply = total demand. **Unbalanced problem** — supply ≠ demand; the first step is always to make it balanced.
- **Phase 1 — initial basic feasible solution:** methods are **North-West Corner method, Least-Cost method, and Vogel's Approximation Method (VAM)** (any one; not optimal).
- **Phase 2 — optimal solution:** iterative process (e.g., **MODI method**) to minimize cost.
- **Maximization vs minimization:** to convert a maximization matrix into minimization, multiply by **−1** or subtract all values from the highest value in the matrix.

### 49.6 North-West Corner Method (Worked Example)
- Factories and supplies: **Jaipur 40, Udaipur 60, Mumbai 70** (total **170**); markets and demands: **Kanpur 70, Pune 40, Delhi 60** (total **170**) → balanced. Cost examples: Jaipur→Kanpur **₹4**, Udaipur→Kanpur **₹3**, Udaipur→Pune **₹4**, Mumbai→Pune **₹2**, Mumbai→Delhi **₹8**.
- Allocate from the north-west corner: **Jaipur→Kanpur 40** (Jaipur exhausted, Kanpur left 30); **Udaipur→Kanpur 30** (Kanpur met, Udaipur left 30); **Udaipur→Pune 30** (Udaipur exhausted, Pune left 10); **Mumbai→Pune 10**; **Mumbai→Delhi 60**.
- **Total cost:** 4×40 + 3×30 + 4×30 + 2×10 + 8×60 = **₹870**.
- Verify that each row sum equals supply and each column sum equals demand; then proceed to the optimal solution in the next session.

## Lecture 50 — Transportation Problem Programming (Contd.)

### 50.1 Least-Cost Method
- Allocate as many units as possible to the cell with the **minimum cost**; strike off the row (if supply exhausted) or column (if demand met), whichever is smaller.
- Same example: minimum cost cell is **Jaipur→Delhi at ₹1** → allocate **40** (Jaipur exhausted, Delhi left 20); next minimum **Mumbai→Pune at ₹2** → allocate **40** (Pune met, Mumbai left 30); handle a **tie** by choosing the cell where maximum can be allocated; then **Udaipur→Kanpur at ₹3** → allocate **60**; then **Mumbai→Kanpur at ₹6** → 10, **Mumbai→Delhi at ₹8** → 20.
- **Total cost = ₹480** (40×1 + 60×3 + 10×6 + 40×2 + 20×8). **Tie-breaking** may give **alternate optimal solutions** with the same minimum cost — multiple options all optimal.

### 50.2 Vogel's Approximation Method (VAM) — Penalty Method
- Also called the **penalty method**: the penalty is the **opportunity cost** of not allocating to a cell.
- **Steps:** for every row and column, subtract the minimum cost from the next minimum cost → **row difference** and **column difference**; pick the **highest penalty**; allocate the maximum units in the minimum-cost cell of that row/column.
- **Tie-breaking (three steps):** (1) choose the minimum-cost cell; if tied, (2) choose where maximum can be allocated; if still tied, (3) choose arbitrarily.
- Same example: highest penalty **4** (row); minimum cost **₹2**; allocate **40** (Pune depleted, 30 left); remove the column and **recompute** row/column differences (they change because a column is deleted); repeat (3×2 matrix → 2×2 matrix) until all supply is allocated.
- **Total cost = ₹480** — incidentally all three methods gave 480, but **VAM is usually more efficient** than the least-cost method (which is itself faster than the North-West Corner method).

### 50.3 MODI Method (Modified Distribution Method) — Optimal Solution
- The **MODI method** determines the **optimal solution** and is based on the **opportunity-cost method**; it may require **more than five iterations**.
- **Prerequisite checks:** the problem is balanced; there are **m + n − 1 allocations at independent positions** (no **closed loop** possible when turning only at occupied cells).
- Find the values **ui (rows)** and **vj (columns)** using the occupied cells; **set any one variable to zero** and solve the equations (e.g., if **v1 = 0**, then u2 = 3, u3 = 6, etc.). With **6 occupied cells and 5 equations** (3 rows + 3 columns − 1 = 5) the condition m + n − 1 is fulfilled.
- Compute the **opportunity cost dij = cij − (ui + vj)** for **unoccupied cells only**.
- **Optimality conditions:** if **all dij are positive**, the solution is optimal (stop); if **any dij is negative**, the solution is not optimal → iterate; if a **zero** appears, that cell gives an **alternative optimal solution** (same total cost).
- Example: a negative opportunity cost (**−1**) indicated further reduction was possible (allocate to that cell and redraw the **closed loop**, assigning alternating **+ and − signs**, then add/subtract the **minimum value = 10** among the negative cells); new allocation changed, total cost reduced.

### 50.4 Unbalanced Problem and Dummy Market
- If supply ≠ demand (e.g., supply **170**, demand **95**), add a **dummy market** (or dummy source) to balance; its demand equals the difference (e.g., **22**) and the cost is **0** in all cells.
- If the excess supply is too high it cannot be consumed, but must be included in the calculations; using the **least-cost method**, the dummy cells (cost 0) are allocated first, and the maximum allocatable equals the difference.
- Another balanced example (total **340**): least-cost method allocation gives **total cost ₹1,410**; check **m + n − 1** allocations (5 columns, 2 rows → 3 − 1 = ... giving **7** allocations); apply MODI.

### 50.5 Worked MODI Example (Optimal Solution)
- Occupied cells set **ui + vj = cij** (e.g., u1 + v2 = 1, u2 + v3 = 3); 7 equations for 7 allocations; set one variable to 0 and solve for all ui and vj.
- Compute opportunity costs **dij = cij − (ui + vj)** for unoccupied cells; initially a **negative value (−1)** appeared → not optimal → iteration via a closed loop (add/subtract the minimum among negatively signed cells, e.g., **10**).
- After the new allocation, again find ui, vj, and the opportunity costs; all became **positive** → optimal. **Total cost = ₹1,400** (minimum possible for that setup). Changing the allocation from there would not decrease the cost.

### 50.6 Advantages of the Transportation Algorithm
- Leads to a **competitive edge** by reducing costs and improving service levels.
- Reduces **transportation cost and lead time** (reducing distance minimizes per-unit delay); improves overall **efficiency**.
- Provides an additional **margin** and reduces **fuel expenses, vehicle maintenance, and labour costs**.
- Better service level (shorter lead time), better inventory planning (know exactly when the next delivery arrives), and **resource optimization** at minimal cost.
