## Lecture 31 — Supply Chain Impellers and Value Chain

### 31.1 From Drivers to Impellers
- The previous week covered the **supply chain drivers** (logistics: facility, inventory, transportation; cross-functional: information system, sourcing, pricing) and how to change them to manage overall **efficiency and responsiveness**.
- This session introduces the **impellers** — the forces/factors that **force us to change/manipulate the drivers** here and there in terms of coming up with a **basket of products and services**; these lead to the **competitive strategy** and the **supply chain strategy**.
- **Competitive strategy** example: an organization currently has a **10% share** and wants **25%**; targeting middle-class/lower-class families → **pricing** and **supply chain efficiency** become very important; the supply chain strategy must be in **synchronization** with the overall competitive strategy.
- **Transparency** is the trigger: the customer is "full of information"; if you keep even **1–2% higher price** than competitors, the customer finds out at the same moment and will never turn back. You must pick pricing, delivery, feature, quality, and variety strategies carefully (30 players offering the same product/services are all visible to the customer).

### 31.2 Impeller 1 — Empowered Customer
- The customer is empowered because of **access to information**. Air-ticket example in three levels:
  - **Level 1** — the airline's own website (**Indigo**): all Indigo flights, prices, number of stoppages, timings.
  - **Level 2** — aggregators (**MakeMyTrip, Goibibo, IRCTC**): search Calcutta → Delhi and get all possible combinations, mix of players, pricing, routes, stoppages; compare within no time.
  - **Level 3** — **ixigo**: puts all the platforms on one platform; shows the **best price in MMT, Goibibo, IRCTC** for a particular flight.
- An **offline retailer cannot say** he charges **₹3000** when the customer gets it online for **₹2500**; the offline retailer may negotiate **₹200–300**, arguing online takes time — but if the product is not urgent the customer can **wait 2 days and save ₹500**. So the customer is empowered thanks to IT tools.

### 31.3 Impeller 2 — Empowered Producer (IT Tools)
- From the producer/manufacturer/services-provider perspective, the tools are: **MRP (material resource planning)**, **ERP (enterprise resource planning)**, **vendor-managed inventory**, **total asset visibility**, **warehouse management software**, and **transportation management software** (best/optimum route by distance, load carried, infrastructure, road condition, weather).
- **MRP/ERP** gives: how many different raw materials are required, the **batch size** of each, **when to place orders** with vendors, and **how much order** vendor A, B, C each gets (of three vendors).
- The future is the **supply chain control tower** (discussed last-to-last session) where all parameters can be tapped. Vendor-rating example: of **5 listed vendors** for a component, the **third is most efficient on price**, the **first quickest on delivery**, the **fifth most reliable on quality** — information is obtained quickly and orders placed.

### 31.4 Impeller 3 — Globalization; Other Impellers
- **Globalization (third impeller):** one can no longer say "this works in the local market, not the foreign market." A credit card that works **only in India** is unattractive because travel frequency is increasing. Outsource wherever you get the **best quality, best quantity, best price**; with fast infrastructure, distribution networks deliver in no time. You can reduce raw-material cost, labor cost, and facility-location cost.
- **Sustainability:** news of **53°C in Delhi** (first time, summer) drives the sustainability debate. In supply chain terms: **green logistics, green supply, recycling, reuse, remanufacturing, refurbishment**, extending product life so waste is not generated, and adding value **after end use** of the product.
- **Collaboration and integration:** working with suppliers, distributors, and **also including the customer** in the manufacturing process (the old idea that customers are part of only services is gone). **Dell** records the customer's requirement/specification and configures the laptop/PC accordingly. Strong partnership with suppliers, storage houses, manufacturing houses, distribution network, warehouses/fulfillment centers, transportation agencies, and end consumers.
- Conclusion: these **5 major impellers** help design the supply chain.

### 31.5 COVID-19, Panic Buying, Bullwhip Effect and System/Trade-off Concepts
- Video links: the **bullwhip effect of the COVID-19 pandemic** and an **Amazon** case on giving customers what they want.
- **COVID-19 panic buying:** fear of no grocery/kitchen items → stocking; demand jumps from **500 units to 1500 units** though the actual demand is the same. Beans example: sudden shortage fears, mask prices going from **₹20–25 to ₹100**, surge in gloves/masks/sanitizers/PPE kits/face shields → disruption at one end → raw-material supply interruption → **ripple effect**.
- **Amazon:** it need not use one delivery speed for all — for one product 5 days is fine, for another **same-day** (higher price). This shows all the impellers.
- **System concept:** supply chain is a **holistic, interconnected, integrated system** — not one manufacturer's or one raw-material supplier's supply chain. Recognize changes/disruptions at one end because they have a ripple effect.
- **Total cost/profit concept:** if total supply chain cost is **$500** from start to end, it is borne by the supplier, supplier's supplier, manufacturer, and the distributor serving 500 retailers; if profit per unit is **$50**, it is shared throughout (supplier, co-manufacturer, warehouse, transportation agencies, retailer).
- **Trade-offs:** inventory vs responsiveness (more inventory → responsive but higher cost → efficiency compromised); cost vs quality (**Surf Excel for middle class, Nirma for lower class**); cost vs speed (**Amazon 1-day = $25, 5–6 days = $5, 10–15 days = free**); stock-out vs safety/seasonal stock (if you say no today, the customer won't return).
- **Bullwhip effect** factors: **information delay, forecast inaccuracies, order batching.** Order batching: retailer places **50 units** → manufacturer produces **75** (changeover takes time; larger batch lowers per-unit cost after standardization) → extra inventory cost (trade-off).
- Stock-market analogy: prices rise as everyone buys, then fall; the **4th June election results** sentiment; a **5% change** assessed by the next player as **7%**; a long/multi-stage structure amplifies the bullwhip, so a **flatter structure** with accurate information sharing is better.

### 31.6 Value Chain — Value-Adding vs Non-Value-Adding Activities
- The focus shifts to **value chain** activities: some are **value adding**, others are **non-value-adding/supporting** (not that they are not required).
- **Information technology:** value-adding when it helps **develop a new product/new feature**; non-value-adding when it is used only to keep inventory at the safe level in the market (no customer value).
- **Finance/accounting:** budgeting, financial planning, investment planning, working-capital management, liquidity ratio, audits, tracking inventory and assets, transaction records, payment terms (**90 days/60 days**) — internal management of the organization; the customer gets no value out of it.
- **New product development:** renovating the product and providing new features — the customer directly gets the benefit → value-adding.
- **Marketing and sales:** recording requirements and providing customized products/services, after-sales services, promotional offers, educating the customer, recording feedback → value-adding.
- **Operations:** the core value-adding activity (converting raw material into finished goods).
- **Distribution:** earlier not value-adding (customer went to the retailer and picked up inventory); **now value-adding** with **doorstep delivery** because the customer has no time to visit the shop or carry packets.
- **Supporting activities:** installation/usage help, customer care, routine maintenance, financing (financial planning, working capital), accounting, **HR** (training, development, recruitment, selection, placement, induction — just to run the organization; the customer doesn't care whether there are 500 or 1000 workers).

### 31.7 Sensing and Responding (Supply and Demand)
- The success of any supply chain = how quickly you **anticipate, understand and respond** effectively; you must **sense the supply market**.
- Your raw-material suppliers supply not only to you but also to competitors/other industries; they may **stock out** due to excess demand, or others may **hold excess demand** for peak season.
- **Responding to supply:** manage vendors on quantity, quality, variety, and grade of material.
- **Deciding and committing:** choose strategic options, set priorities, make informed decisions to ensure smooth supply and demand.
- **Sensing demand:** whether customers are shifting to your group or to a competitor (extra features, lower price, promotional offers); forecast the demand pattern; align supply chain activities.
- **Responding to demand:** acquire raw material, plan production scheduling/sequencing, decide the number of models/units, set inventory level, set up/polish distribution networks, and ensure on-time fulfillment and end delivery.
- Conclusion: sense **both ends** (backward integration with suppliers to meet customer demand); the customer is empowered but so is the **manufacturer** (targeted social-media advertisements — "5% down the price" — then prices rise when you actually book). The latest development is **sustainability** — ensure how the used product can be utilized again, making the customer more delighted/satisfied.

## Lecture 32 — Strategic Supply Chain Management

### 32.1 Supply Chain Strategy — Introduction
- Content adapted from strategic management books by **Ireland & Robert** (competitive strategy / strategic management process); diagrams modified for the supply chain perspective.
- **Supply chain strategy** = planning, design, execution, control and monitoring of supply chain activities — from raw-material production to end consumption — integrated and synchronized.
- Basic purpose: **align business goals / overall competitive strategy** with the supply chain strategy.
- Unique parameters: cost-efficient delivery, enhancing customer experience, minimizing inventory, minimizing transportation/warehousing/raw-material acquisition cost.
- **Push-pull supply chain strategy:**
  - **Make-to-stock** — a continuous optimum **pull** strategy: manufacture continuously, store inventory near the market/fulfillment center.
  - **Make-to-order** — manufacture only when the order is received.
  - **Engineer-to-order** — highly customized; start from product development after the customized order, test, then deliver (high-end/special equipment).

### 32.2 Strategic Management Process (Generic Steps)
1. **Review the current situation** — mission, vision, statements (want to be cost leader for 20 years or highly responsive); set objectives.
2. **Analyze the environment** — internal **and** external.
3. **Develop new strategy** — corporate, business, functional levels.
4. **Implement** at every level and every stakeholder.
5. **Control** — measure output against standards; take corrective and preventive action.
- **Internal analysis:** strengths, capabilities, where you lag. **External analysis:** the **PESTLE** tool (political, ethical, social, technical, environmental, legal); identify opportunities and threats (pandemic → raw-material shortage → build inventory or adopt an agile strategy).
- **Strategy levels:** corporate (**growth, stability, retrenchment**), business (competitive), functional (inventory, distribution, manufacturing, outsourcing).
- Functional example for cost efficiency: keep **minimum inventory**, set up a **centralized warehouse** (more lead time → responsiveness reduced, but inventory cost minimized).
- Video point: without a supply chain strategy, hitting randomly here and there will not work for long — a **common thread** must bind all components.

### 32.3 21st-Century Forces and Competitive Advantage
- Forces guiding the market: **global economy** (access to it, but also volatile to global disruptions — the **2008 recession** started with **Lehman Brothers bankruptcy**; the COVID-19 recession); **globalization** (global raw material quality, technology, manpower skill, distribution network; global customers buying from anywhere); **rapid technological changes** (shorter product life cycle; **iPhone 14 → 15 → 16 → 17** repetitive buying and e-waste); **increased importance of customer knowledge** (empowered customer).
- **Superior value-creating strategy** (relative to competitors) → **commit** resources → define what the firm **will and will not do** → competitive advantage.
- Examples: **Philips** — "**Innovation and you**" (innovative products, revising features, longevity, technology); **Walmart** — minimum-cost product; **Zara** — replenish latest trends in showrooms, changing patterns/styles **every week** vs traditional fashion stores taking **1–2 months**.

### 32.4 Competitive Strategies
- **Cost leadership vs differentiation:** cost leadership targets the broad market with lowest cost (low/middle customer group); differentiation adds unique value (**Rolex, BMW, Mercedes** hardly bother about cost).
- **Jio** example of cost leadership in the smartphone/internet era — technology diffusion accelerating (radio took decades; TV faster; smartphone faster; internet fastest).
- Narrow-market focused cost leadership (industry-only products, e.g., a carton-box strength-testing machine); narrow-market focused differentiation (high-end, limited-use products).
- **Determine and control cost drivers:** **automation** (remove manpower, higher productivity, fewer defects); new distribution channel (minimum lead time, maximum coverage, minimum cost); advertising media suited to the targeted audience (sports products during the World Cup/IPL); direct sale vs indirect sale; **reconfigure the value chain** (raw-material cost, grading, **forward integration** = Dell direct, **backward integration** = acquire suppliers); change of location.
- Cost-creating value activities: cost-effective **MIS**, simplified management structure, manufacturing technology, low-cost raw material, monitor supplier performance, link suppliers with the production process, economies of scale, efficient-scale facilities, effective delivery schedules, low-cost transportation (end-mile by **electric bikes/cycles** — sustainable and cost-efficient).
- **Differentiation strategy:** unique features/performance (**Sony** sound systems; **Hitachi** AC leader; **Dell**; **Walmart** cost-efficient); sustainability (**Myntra** paper packaging, 100% recyclable); technological differentiation; high-quality raw material; fast delivery (promise **90% of products within one day**; consolidate prime orders with normal orders; more fulfillment centers near big markets); customer-credit flexibility; specialist warehouses (**UPS** automated; **Amazon's biggest distribution hub near Delhi/Gurgaon**, automated).
- **Effective supply chain strategy:** synchronized; handles disruption; mitigates risk; collaborative approach; **agile** structure (scale down to 5 products, scale up to 500); **resilient** (minimum inventory/safety stock during shutdown/strike); **digital platform** connecting all players (ERP, IoT, **AI/ML** detecting a demand spike and sharing plans); **cyber-security** firewalls.
- **Optimizing supply chain strategy:** sales and operation planning (S&OP), workforce management/training, supply chain network design (minimum cost/time), demand planning (AI/ML accurate prediction), inventory balance between service level and cost.

### 32.5 Firm Strategic Choices and Value-Chain Strategies
- Firm strategic choices: **economies of scale**, barriers to market entry, **diversification** (higher entry barriers via new features/latest industry), product differentiation, **industry concentration** (high = a few players capture most, e.g., **Jio, Airtel, BSNL**), market friction (direct/indirect costs in financial transactions).
- **Value-chain component strategies:**
  - **Product development:** portfolio selection (range based on market analysis), in-house vs outsourcing.
  - **Marketing & sales:** segmentation (demographic, geographic, psychographic, behavioral), positioning, pricing, promotional activities.
  - **Supply chain strategy:** procurement (supplier selection, repetitive procurement — large quantity gives better negotiation power), manufacturing/services operations (location, technology, conversion, capacity planning), distribution (modes/channels — doorstep, customer pickup, retail, organized stores), outsourcing decisions (warehousing, transportation).
  - **Broad supply chain structure:** supplier strategy (backward integration), operation strategy (mainstream conversion), logistic strategy (forward integration — distribution network, fulfillment centers, end-mile delivery).
  - **Design strategy:** inventory management (trade-off service level vs carrying cost), transportation (modes, routes, carriers — **ship** most sustainable/min cost, road increases carbon footprint, air increases cost), operating facilities (fulfillment centers, inventory locations, manufacturing hubs, capacity by market), information flow.
  - **Services strategy:** service design, service quality, customer experience management, service differentiation, service recovery, technology integration, employee training.
- **Case — Unilever (CPFR, collaborative planning, forecasting and replenishment):** FMCG — packed food, drinks, personal care, cleaning products; five-year strategy focused on restructuring the supply chain (governance, global procurement, supply chain manager, supplier engagement, technology); **merged 30 warehouses into 5 big distribution hubs**; policy of **zero inventory**; hence **cross docking**; AI/ML/IoT forecasting; stronger supplier partnerships. Key takeaway: **forecasting accuracy up 10%, inventory down 10%, sales volume up 5%**.
- **Case — Eton (US; power management, electrical, hydraulic, mechanical systems):** developed a **resilient supply chain strategy** during the pandemic via **multi-sourcing**, increased supplier collaboration, and end-to-end connection.
- **Case — Intel:** **ethical sourcing** — tin, tungsten, gold, titanium made **conflict-free**; **RFID tags** on packaging/bags to identify the origin of the metals.

## Lecture 33 — Strategic Supply Chain Management (Contd.)

### 33.1 Developing Supply Chain Strategy — The Environment
- Strategy is for the future, which is **uncertain and complex**; you must understand dynamics from suppliers, manufacturing, warehouse, distribution channel, and the customer end.
- Factors forcing constant strategy change: **evolving customer demands, technological advancements** (obsolete technology → no one buys), **global market dynamics**, and **regulatory authorities** (e.g., banning **BS3 petrol engines and BS4 diesel engines** → next version of engines).
- Models help **tap, predict and track** internal and external environmental forces.

### 33.2 External Environment Factors
- **Economic:** consumers (low/medium/higher class), pricing, competition (monopoly market vs rivalry).
- **Physical:** supply chain distribution network — online, retail chain, organized/unorganized store.
- **Social/cultural:** lifestyle, behavior, attitude, personality, cultural/subcultural, demographic (Indian culture differs from the US market that **Walmart** targets).
- **Global forces:** global players entering the Indian market — **Walmart acquired an 80% stake in Flipkart**.
- **Technological:** shorter life cycles → minimize inventory before the new product arrives.
- **Political/legal:** change in political ruling and special policies (infrastructure development, employment generation).
- **Demographic:** characterize the target population for the distribution network.
- **Internal analysis:** R&D skills, manufacturing/assembly (can cut cost drastically), inventory management, distribution/end-mile delivery; outsource where you are not competent.

### 33.3 Competitive Advantage — Resources, Capabilities, Core Competence
- **Tangible resources:** infrastructure, machines, production facilities. **Intangible:** brand image, secret recipe, skills, connection with suppliers/end customer.
- Resources → capabilities → **core competencies** (**Walmart** = cost-efficient distribution; **Zara** = responsive/innovative supply chain).
- A core competency must be: **valuable** (customer ready to pay), **rare** (no other player promises/delivers it), **costly to imitate**, **non-substitutable**.
- **Value chain analysis** — outsource where you are not good (R&D, marketing & sales, warehousing). Restaurant example: source ideas/material/distribution (via **Zomato/Swiggy**) and focus only on the recipe/cooking.
- **Value** = something that ensures **above-average returns**; adding attributes/performance/features customers are ready to pay for.
- Competitive advantage comes from a unique set of skills → rare, non-substitutable, hard-to-copy recipe.

### 33.4 SWOT Analysis
- **Strengths & weaknesses = internal; opportunities & threats = external.**
- Robust foundation required; address weaknesses (improve or outsource), capitalize strengths (**Amazon** promising 1-day/2-day delivery), leverage opportunities while mitigating threats.
- Strength examples: good supplier relationships (**Walmart** feeding large orders → economies of scale + heavy price negotiation); tangible/intangible resources (**KFC, McDonald's** recipe consistency worldwide).
- Weakness examples: market complaints, unhappy customer feedback, poor R&D, high manufacturing cost, manual vs automated manufacturing, inaccurate purchasing, own distribution network.
- Two perspectives — **company (manufacturing)** and **vendor**. Company supply chain strengths: **purchasing power** (negotiate price and quality), high **inventory turnover** (queue for the new iPhone), **payment history** (90% within 60/90 days; 10% returned for quality), **multiple vendors** (negotiation power). Vendor strengths: **credit terms** (longer time to repay), quality (**Intel** processor inside Dell/Lenovo), willingness to **retain inventory**, setting up a manufacturing facility nearby for **JIT**.
- Vendor weaknesses: struggles with quality, delivery schedules, or scale up/down.
- Opportunities: e-commerce **one-day delivery** (100% one-day delivery → traffic to your website), consolidate **5–6 orders into one delivery**, expand capacity to nearby markets; vendor opportunities (improve services/quality/delivery, update manufacturing technology).
- Threats: government banning diesel engines (**BS4**), environmental agencies/**WHO** strict guidelines on **e-waste**, sustainability-aware customers, natural disaster/pandemic/flood (emergency/disaster-relief supply chains), customers shifting to **Jio** (Vodafone/Hutch divest/liquidate/merge).
- **Amazon case:** efficient supply chain as the key measure; three fundamental elements — **extensible warehouse and distribution network** (all pin-code coverage), **diversified fleet**, **advanced technology** (quick information sharing); **2,373 active facilities**; North America alone **>200 fulfillment centers**. The **"Fulfilled by Amazon" (FBA/FVA)** program: suppliers supply to Amazon's fulfillment center, Amazon does packaging and delivery; Amazon helped develop supplier technology; introduced the **6-wheeled automatic delivery robot "Scout"**; **inventory turnover increased by 8.39 times**.
- Amazon SWOT: strengths (well-known brand/pioneer, cost advantage low-cost products, continuous business improvement); weaknesses (low e-commerce margins, seasonality); opportunities (diversify into furniture, grocery, mobile; increase awareness; local partners/suppliers); threats (low margin, patent infringement, low entry barriers, security issues with digital networks).

### 33.5 Porter's Five Forces
- Given by **Professor Michael Porter** (Harvard Business School); article on how competitive forces shape strategy.
- **Five forces:**
  1. **Competitive rivalry** (center): number of competitors, quality/differences, extra features, cost, customer loyalty (fragile).
  2. **Supplier power:** limited number of suppliers → supplier negotiates; many suppliers → you negotiate (depends on quantity); size, uniqueness of services, cost of change (supplier is a strategic investment — sharing the secret recipe/designs).
  3. **Threat of new entry:** low threat → anyone can enter; barriers = high capital/technology investment, regulatory restrictions, cost advantage from recovered initial investment/R&D/stabilization.
  4. **Buyer power:** quantity, quality needs, negotiation power, price sensitivity, cost of change.
  5. **Threat of substitution:** close/replacement products → renovation/innovation strategies.
- Designing supply chain strategy: supplier concentration, switching cost, supplier relationships, buyer power, price sensitivity, product differentiation, barriers to entry, industry structure/competitive landscape, market concentration, technological advancement, customer preferences toward substitutes, regulatory environment, market growth.
- **Jio** example: free services → competitors stabilize existing share; **IPL free broadcasting**, **World Cup free on Disney+ Hotstar**; resources must support the competitive strategy.

### 33.6 Supply Chain Strategy Implementation
- There is **no one best strategy**; game theory says a **pure strategy is obsolete** — use a **mixed strategy** (e.g., 20% cost-efficient, 60% responsive, rest a blend).
- After configuring the external/internal environment: identify **demand type, customer location, supplier location**.
- **Demand:** seasonal/linear/consistent; consistent → efficient (weekly/monthly forecast); seasonal → strong distribution network in-season and manufacture/inventory off-season.
- **Product portfolio:** what competitors offer; why customers move (extra features) → include them.
- **Customer location:** far from a metro (**>500 km**) → cannot promise one-day delivery; consolidate orders or build a big hub nearby; within country vs global shipments.
- **Supplier location:** overseas → longer delivery time; set up nearby vendors for emergency/JIT.
- **Inventory:** manage type, size, nature, complexity.
- **Starbucks case:** **35,000 retail shops**, **>50 years** of operation, extensive network; efficient supply chain + quality coffee beans via **vertical integration/collaboration** with coffee producers under the **Coffee and Farmer Equity (C.A.F.E.) and ESG plans** (ethical, environmental, social, quality criteria); **>4 lakh producers** directly collaborating; "from the farmer's field to the cup of coffee" integrated. **Peter Gibbons** transformed the complex supply chain into a simple structure using a **four-stage model from SCOR (plan, source, manufacture, deliver; removed return)** and a **centralized logistics system**; four metric areas — **operational safety, on-time supply and order fulfillment rate, overall cost of the supply chain, financial performance**; digital technologies for transparency. Result: **ethically sourcing 98.6%**, worldwide **revenue up 5%**.

## Lecture 34 — Achieving Supply Chain Strategic Fit

### 34.1 Competitive Strategy and Supply Chain Strategy
- Two things to reconcile: the **competitive strategy** (baseline of how you compete in the market, developed with the customer and observing other players) and the **supply chain strategy** (how you deal with retailers, transportation, distribution, storage/fulfillment, and suppliers).
- **Functional strategies** within each stakeholder (operation, quality, R&D, marketing, accounting, HR, ISO, purchase, store) must attach to a **common thread**; then all stakeholders with all functional units must be aligned so the complete supply chain functions in synchronization with the competitive strategy.
- Three-step process for strategic fit; the scope must expand from one operation/functional unit to **complete functions throughout all stakeholders**.
- Examples: **Walmart** (low price, wide variety, fair quality/responsiveness); **McMaster** (MRO products; convenience + responsiveness); **Blue Nile** (diamonds online; low price + availability, centralized distribution center).

### 34.2 Blue Nile — Online vs Showroom
- **Blue Nile** maintains a **centralized distribution center** supplying all markets; if inventory were kept at 10 locations you would also need **security at 10 locations** — huge investment. Online gives complete variety (images, colors, storage capacities) and lets you consolidate orders for multiple products in the same category.
- A physical **jewelry showroom** carries limited variety because it interacts with customers and makes personalized/customized products — and needs 10 locations.

### 34.3 Supply Chain Strategy Types
- No pure strategy; it is a **mixture** (Walmart efficient, Zara responsive).
- **Efficient:** minimize total cost — raw-material acquisition (negotiate, feed large orders), minimum inventory, minimum wastage, transportation/fulfillment warehousing utilization, vehicle routing, container utilization, efficient end-mile; **acceptable (not highest) quality**; standard delivery.
- **Responsive:** agile to customer interruptions/order cancellations (stop shipment at nearby hub, reroute the same packet with changed billing/invoice); variety, quality, service level; flexible manufacturing (changeover to B model, dedicate 2–3 production lines; responsive suppliers).
- **Lean:** reduce waste (**TIMWOOD** — transportation, inventory, motion, waiting, over-processing, over-production, defects); **JIT**; continuous improvement; value stream mapping; inventory near the customer adds value (quick delivery) but excess inventory must be minimized.
- **Agile:** agility, adaptability, flexibility; ice-cream parlor example (2–3 ice creams for a family, 20 people, **500 ice creams** for a birthday); **Philips** "Innovation and you."
- **Collaborative:** strong collaboration; **Dell** earlier online-only, now uses retail partners to be more responsive, keep inventory, and reduce lead time; **70–80%** of orders are routine configurations → combine efficiency + responsiveness; modular design (change sound drivers, replace a hardware module).

### 34.4 Strategic Fit — Why It Is Important
- Competitive strategy and supply chain strategy must move **in line** (same direction); otherwise it is a disaster.
- Contradiction example: manufacturer promises high availability, but the retailer won't keep huge inventory (cost; demand uncertain for 2–4 months).
- **Swiggy** single piece of sweet → the local courier partner gets very less margin → contradiction; the local partner must be convinced.
- Importance of strategic fit: meet customer needs; improve overall supply chain performance (**Amul** — no stock-out anywhere, because once a customer shifts and develops a taste, it is a problem); enhance competitiveness via **USPs** (**Myntra** paper packaging, removing plastic — sustainable, minimizing carbon footprint); manage cost and risk (shift from efficient to responsive under risk/uncertainty).
- **Amul example:** milk quantity fixed, but cheese/ice cream variable; perishable → obsolete/expiry cost and cold-storage holding/carrying cost.

### 34.5 Three Steps for Strategic Fit
1. Understand the **customer and supply chain uncertainty** (customer preferences/needs/priorities; supply chain capabilities/uncertainties).
2. Understand the **supply chain capabilities**.
3. Achieve **alignment** between supply chain strategy and competitive strategy (continued next session).

### 34.6 Customer Needs and Implied Demand Uncertainty
- Customer needs: features (mobile camera megapixels, storage, games, apps, internet/social media), quantity (5 vs 500), response time (2 days vs **Flipkart 1 day**), variety, pricing, desired rate of innovation.
- **Maruti variety example:** petrol **LXI base → next LXI → VXI → next VXI → top ZXI** (5 variants); 5 colors → 25 products; 25 petrol + 25 diesel + 25 hybrid + 25 CNG ≈ 100 models; with 2 engine capacities (1000cc, 1600cc) → **200 models** (Vitara/Grand Vitara).
- Quick innovation → shorter product life cycle → obsolescence.
- **Implied demand uncertainty increases with:** increasing range of products; decreased lead time; increased variety; increased number of channels (Indigo's own website → MMT, Goibibo, ixigo, IRCTC); increased rate of innovation; increased service level.
- **Taxi vs ambulance:** taxi low margin, low implied uncertainty; ambulance high margin, **forecast error 5–10% vs up to 100%**, stock-out 2% is a disaster vs usual, markdown up to **25%**.
- **Supply uncertainty** rises with new products (uncertain supply; suppliers still standardizing raw material); mature products lower uncertainty (saturated demand/supply, reduced defects/process rejection); frequent breakdowns (machines, absenteeism), unpredictable/low yields, poor quality initially, limited capacity.
- Scenarios: salt/steel/sugar commodity — predictable supply & demand → **efficient**; automobile — sometimes certain supply/uncertain demand or vice versa; new communication device — highly uncertain supply & demand.

### 34.7 Supply Chain Capabilities and the Cost of Responsiveness
- Focus: bridge the **gap between current and desired capabilities** (technology, process improvement, supplier relationship, sourcing, manufacturing, distribution, warehousing, storage, transportation, end-mile).
- Responsiveness capabilities: handle wide variety (200 models of the same Vitara/Brezza), shorter lead time, large variety, highly innovative products (Philips), high service level (installation, demonstration, delivery per schedule), handle supply uncertainty.
- **Highest responsiveness** = extra inventory, short lead time, highest quality, largest variety, processes even minimum orders, highest service level → **cost high**.
- Vice versa: willing to wait → consolidate orders (wait 30 min more, consolidate 5 pizza orders) → **lower cost**.
- Efficient: steel mill, sugar mill (commodity markets); somewhat efficient: apparel (trends known for 6 months/1 year); somewhat responsive: automotive (many models); highly responsive: **Zara, Dell, 7-Eleven Japanese store** (daily inventory change; customer central to the strategy).

## Lecture 35 — Achieving Supply Chain Strategic Fit (Contd.)

### 35.1 Third Step — Alignment of Responsiveness and Implied Uncertainty
- High uncertainty → you must be **responsive**; matured supply/demand → go for an **efficient** supply chain.
- **McMaster:** MRO product variety is huge (keys of many sizes); rapid delivery + variety → choose a **responsive** supply chain, though it cannot be only responsive.
- **Aligning roles across the supply chain:** different stages may require varying responsiveness/efficiency. Being responsive at the retailer (high availability, extra inventory) gives a positive chance to reduce manufacturing cost (bulk production, minimum changeover); you can be efficient when acquiring resources/raw materials. The **retailer observing maximum uncertainty** should get the **maximum margin**; manufacturer and raw-material suppliers should be efficient.

### 35.2 Strategic Fit Zone — Certain Demand
- Certain demand + certain supply → a **very efficient** supply chain (**efficiency zone**): steel, sugar, commodity market, standardized jeans (only color/trend variations). Apparel tops/girls' stock (many designs) → responsive.
- **Zara** mostly produces for females/girls with weekly fashion/inventory change → responsiveness important, minimum inventory. **Walmart** (same apparel industry) → **efficient**, cost efficiency, standardized products. **Amazon** divides patterns into categories (regular jeans → efficient; girls' tops → responsive).
- The **strategic zone shifts** depending on where the product lies (certainty vs uncertainty).

### 35.3 Examples of Misalignment
- All functions should target the competitive strategy; a responsive retailer should not refuse a single order.
- Efficient = Walmart (bulk); responsive = Dell (starts manufacturing when the order/inputs arrive).
- There is **no right supply chain strategy independent of the competitive strategy**. **BMW** promoting fuel efficiency would not be believed (BMW is known for speed, comfort, luxury, safety; **Volvo** for safety). BMW store is in Calcutta, not Kharagpur; demand → the customer comes specially for a test drive; **Harley-Davidson** keeps a centralized store for free test rides; customized products cannot be stocked in the store.
- Jeans industry: supplier absorbs no uncertainty → efficient; manufacturing handles the trend; the **retailer observes maximum uncertainty** (seasonal sales **70%/50%/20+25% off**) → maximum margin. Girls' top: manufacturer must change the trend/manufacturing process quickly; the retailer observes least uncertainty → efficient.
- Some stakeholders are efficient, some responsive.

### 35.4 Comparison — Efficient vs Responsive
- **Primary goal:** cost reduction vs quick response.
- **Product design:** easy/minimum cost vs **modularity** (LED TV — replace sound panel, picture/control board, storage, processor).
- **Pricing:** not concerned with price vs concerned.
- **Manufacturing:** efficient/minimize cost vs capacity flexibility.
- **Inventory:** minimum vs provide inventory (manage obsolete inventory via the secondary market).
- **Lead time:** standard (5/7/10 days) vs same-day.
- **Supplier selection:** charge, then quality, capability, market access vs negotiation starts with how quickly changeover/design change happens.
- **Multiple products/customer segments:** Maruti **200 models**, base ~₹10 lakh to top **₹20–23 lakh**; base-model buyers seek efficiency/fuel efficiency; BMW carries 2–3 people while lower-segment cars are full (seat capacity 5, trying 7–8). Maruti came with **two supply chains — traditional and NEXA** (luxury/premium), like **ICICI Bank** separate branches for investment/insurance.
- **Product life cycle:** introduction (uncertain demand & supply, high margin/price to recover R&D, defects → responsive); growth/maturity (saturated supply/demand, competition → lower price/margin, standardized process → efficient).
- **Competitive changes:** globalization and more competition (Amazon same-day → Myntra/Flipkart/eBay must ensure it); the next e-commerce trend = same-day delivery for **90–95%** of products.

### 35.5 Tailored Supply Chain
- Multiple customers/demands/patterns/variety → **no one-size-fits-all pure strategy**.
- **Maruti Alto** through the traditional store vs **Baleno** (status) vs **NEXA**.
- **Dell:** earlier responsiveness (online orders, configure systems); realized the lead time was huge → adopted a traditional chain with a retailer in the local market; the retailer fulfills immediate demand for regular products; specialized/high-configuration takes 1–2 days lead time with inventory at the retailer.
- **Levi's:** standardized-size jeans (efficient) and customized jeans (responsive); standard products get a lower price.
- Design: efficient when uncertainty is absent; tailor toward responsiveness when uncertainty is present.
- Strategies: maximum efficiency; different manufacturing processes/transportation modes (drone/air fast; ship sustainable; ship/road/train cost-efficient); centralized vs distributed inventory (fast-moving low-implied-uncertainty items in decentralized locations; **Blue Nile** diamonds centralized); pharmaceutical industry new drug (product availability is a big question; promoted through medical representatives; doctors prescribe but the drug is hard to find) → introduction stage challenge → responsive.

### 35.6 Expanding the Strategic Scope
- Not limited to one functional department/operation/stakeholder; must be across **all functions, stages, and stakeholders**; shared objective.
- Scope levels:
  - **Intra-operation** (minimized local view within one function — e.g., manufacturing: process, handling, inventory, changeover, packaging; targeting efficiency in one operation but not the others).
  - **Intra-functional** (align all operations within one functional unit/department toward efficiency/responsiveness).
  - **Inter-functional** (all functions within one unit/stakeholder aligned).
  - **Inter-company** (all organizations in the supply chain aligned — maximizing **supply chain surplus**).
- Example: distributor packaging/inventory; align competitive, product development, supply chain, marketing, R&D, HR, accounting, purchase, and warehousing strategies.
- A **mixture** is possible (responsiveness 9/10 while cost efficiency 5/10, or cost 8 and responsiveness 7).
- Key drivers: align markets; global growth/expansion (set up distribution/fulfillment centers globally); supplier relationship/collaboration; process redesign; track KPIs.

### 35.7 Challenges to Strategic Fit
- **Variety + shrinking life cycle.**
- **Globalization + increasing uncertainty** (overseas suppliers/markets bring disruption risk) → build flexibility (**Honda** manufactures sedans, SUVs and hatchbacks on the same production line).
- **Fragmentation / increased outsourcing** (losing ownership and control): motivate the retailer, or assure buyback/reprocessing/secondary market if inventory is not consumed.
- **Change in technology** (shorter life cycle; new product introduction uncertainty).
- **Environmental sustainability** measures (**Myntra** packaging change; **Starbucks**).
- **Walmart case (all 6 drivers):** facilities & inventory **efficient** (centralized distribution centers → minimum inventory cost, facility/operation cost down); transportation **responsive** (own fleets, extra cost recovered from savings); **cross docking** (no inventory, consolidate orders, ship pin-code-wise); sourcing **efficient** (feed large orders, negotiate price); information **responsive** (huge information network); pricing = **everyday low pricing** (same price each visit → demand fixed, supply fixed → efficient). Result: huge variety, volume, minimum price, fair quality; **reduced out-of-stock occurrences by 16%**.
- **Dell case:** before **2007** a direct-sale model (online orders, design, direct shipping, close to customer, real-time information) but problems of distance and build-to-order lead time; **JIT** minimizes cost/inventory, minimum cash, supplier collaboration. After 2007 it moved to **retail distribution partners** near the customer, product diversification (laptops, headphones, electronics), supply chain optimization (added the retailer), strategic partners, sustainability/environmental initiatives (**WEEE** — waste from electrical and electronic equipment), and a customer-centric approach with more responsiveness + efficiency.
- Major source: **Chopra & Meindl — Supply Chain Management: Strategy, Planning and Operation.**

## Lecture 36 — Make or Buy Decision

### 36.1 Roadmap (Seven Perspectives)
- **Strategic alliance → make-or-buy decision → strategic sourcing → supplier selection → supplier relationship management → continuous monitoring → purchasing** (repetitive POs raised based on **vendor rating**).
- The make/buy step decides whether an operation (sub-assembly, warehousing, transportation) is done in-house or outsourced.

### 36.2 Strategic Alliance
- A **legal agreement between two or more companies** to collaborate in an agreed area, forming a **third entity/new reason**.
- **Hero Honda example:** Hero was in the cycle business (non-fuel two-wheeler); Honda produced/sold bikes abroad; Hero used Honda's technology, Honda used Hero's supply chain; they created a new entity for the **fuel two-wheeler** market.
- Benefits: access to a new market, **shared intellectual property** (technology/resources), **mutually beneficial/win-win** partnership.

### 36.3 Types of Strategic Alliance
- **Equity strategic alliance:** resource commitment + ownership diluted (acquiring equity) — e.g., a car manufacturer investing in a battery company (EV trend), equipping local vendors producing **nickel, lithium-ion, lead-acid batteries**.
- **Non-equity strategic alliance:** minimum resource commitment; a temporary project; no dilution of ownership (e.g., two software development companies combining expertise for one project).
- **Joint venture:** highest resource commitment; dedicated resources; e.g., **Maruti Suzuki**; two pharmaceutical companies for R&D, manufacturing and distribution of a new drug.
- Deciding factors: project complexity, risk mitigation, control vs independence, communication/trust.

### 36.4 Vertical vs Horizontal; Domestic vs International
- **Vertical strategic alliance:** upstream/downstream — acquiring suppliers, or sourcing value-adding activities at different stages (raw material, sub-assemblies, warehousing, transportation/3PL).
- **Horizontal:** acquiring same-stage players (pharma acquiring pharma; transportation agency acquiring transportation agency); collaborate in marketing, distribution, or joint production.
  - **Amazon/Flipkart** have their own distribution networks but not all pin codes (far/rural) → collaborate with **Indian Postal Services** (access to rural; delivery 10–15 days).
- **Domestic:** expand an unexplored area (local brand famous in one region going to another); **pool resources** (10,000 units + 15,000 units = 25,000 → economies of scale); access complementary resources (manufacturing expert + R&D expert).
- **Apple:** good at R&D/design/software; not producing — outsources manufacturing to **China** (low cost), now looking at **India** as a big strategic partner.
- **International:** access overseas markets and cheap/quality supplies; forward and reverse supply chain scope; **avoid import barriers** (Delhi electrical products billed from a UP/Haryana state office; Apple producing in China and importing to India).
- International synergies: an internationally known brand gives comfort, reliability, quality, safety.

### 36.5 Purpose/Benefits of Outsourcing
- **Enhance competitiveness:** **Nike** does not manufacture; **Philips** outsources to co-manufacturers (with quality, production, and material-planning engineers at the co-manufacturing sites).
- **Improve efficiency & cost effectiveness:** a small player's per-unit cost is higher; outsource to a third vendor serving 2–3 players → economies of scale; restaurant + **Swiggy/Zomato/UberEats** (50 orders → pay margin for 50; 500 orders → pay for 500).
- **Access to new market and customer.**
- **Foster innovation and learning** (Philips "Innovation and you").
- **Mitigating risk and uncertainty:** Kharagpur campus has ~**20,000 residents** during classes vs ~**2,000** during vacation; restaurants share risk with a delivery partner.
- **Enhance flexibility and adaptability:** responsive to 2,000 people; avoids the fixed-asset cost of 500 bikers.

### 36.6 Make or Buy — Break-even
- Graph: quantity on x-axis, profit/sales revenue on y-axis; **fixed asset investment + variable cost = total cost**; **loss below Q*** (break-even), profit above.
- **Outsource if order quantity < Q***; **produce in-house if > Q***. For that supplier, the quantity is big enough (producing for other players) → minimize total cost, shipping cost, inventory cost.
- Not the only criterion: even if profitable, outsource if you are not good at packaging/transportation assets/software/vehicle routing.
- Advantages of making high + risk tied to buying high → **make in-house**; both low → **buy**; moderate → moderate outsourcing.
- Strong control over product/services → **make in-house** (**Zara**: fashionable products in **Europe** — high price/control; standard products outsourced to cheaper **Asian** locations).
- Standardized products (commodity t-shirt) → outsourcing (**Walmart**).
- Implementing a successful make-or-buy: analyze cost and expertise; evaluate quality and control needs; consider the capacity of stakeholders and your own; scale up/down for demand fluctuations; run a **pilot survey/case study**.

### 36.7 Benefits and Limitations of Outsourcing
- **Benefits:** cost saving; **specialized expertise** (a hospital outsourcing healthcare-waste management — a different industry with WHO/environmental-agency regulations); focus on core competencies; scalability and flexibility; improved efficiency/productivity; shared risk; enhanced customer satisfaction (doctors focus on patients).
- **Limitations:** **loss of control** (quality, timeliness, overall performance — e.g., waste pickup promised within 24 hours but sometimes 30–35 hours); communication and coordination challenge (partners must change raw material; language/cultural barriers; different industry); dependence for quality/delivery (a disruption stops the production line); sharing secrets/confidential information; **hidden/indirect costs** (negotiation, contractual documents, disputes); negative impact on the internal workforce (dissatisfaction/dissonance).

### 36.8 Cases
- **Apple:** product design/software in the **US**, manufacturing in **China** (cheap), selling in the **US**.
- **Nike:** designing/branding; lacks capacity to commercialize → outsources manufacturing, cutting cost while delivering a high-quality comfort shoe.
- **Google:** a search engine; free products (search, nearby hospitals, maps); makes money by **selling apps and advertisements** (targeted ads using search data).

## Lecture 37 — Strategic Sourcing

### 37.1 Strategic Sourcing — Basics
- After deciding to outsource, **strategic sourcing = identification, evaluation and selection of suppliers** (long-term investment of resources; vendors/sources/stakeholders cannot be changed overnight).
- Do not delete struggling suppliers (quality/price/delivery); keep a **local vendor** alongside an **overseas vendor** (dependency if overseas supplies are interrupted; cost savings via JIT with the local vendor).
- Purpose: strong relationship, improve quality, delivery performance, reduce cost, mitigate risk.
- Key elements: supplier selection/evaluation, **supplier relationship management**, **supplier segmentation**, **global sourcing**.
- Factors: product and process specifications, available suppliers in the market, customer service; feasibility of sourcing locally or globally.
- Includes: development of the sourcing strategy, **negotiating contracts** (delivery, quality, price, dispute handling, payment terms/installments), establishing partner relationships; ensure the vendor reaches the minimum **Q***/break-even.

### 37.2 Characteristics of Strategic Sourcing
- **Disciplined, repetitive buying process** (5/10/15 lots per month depending on the product/value; high-value inventory cannot be kept).
- **Total cost factor** = cost of acquiring material (not only raw material) + quality + service delivery + after-sales + the person tracking 5 vendors.
- **Consolidated purchasing power** (leverage high volumes) vs inventory holding cost trade-off.
- **Tight supplier relationship** (arms-length vs shoulder-length): depends on the product; vendors set up facilities nearby for JIT (ensure break-even by feeding large orders).
- **Improved teamwork and purchasing skills:** all departments (logistics, operation, marketing, accounting, purchasing, store) to capture all terms/conditions — one person may miss them.
- **Realigning business processes:** transparent information sharing minimizes order-placing/tracking cost, cycle time, inventory cost; continuous improvement with KPIs.
- Continuous improvement example: **Philips** asks vendors to generate quality reports for all components supplied; IQC checks on receipt, in-process, and at OQC.

### 37.3 Supplier Selection and Management
- Importance of a supplier: unit cost, reducing the total cost of acquisition, meeting customer requirements, relying on few suppliers, depending on the supplier for designing; categorize by **quantity** and **quality**.
- Quality-based category example — **AC**: cooling unit, PCB, remote are very important; the outer plastic casing can compromise quality (once hung, not touched/dropped).
- Supplier selection criteria: quality, delivery, performance history, warranties, claim policies, production facility, capacity, technical/human potential, scale up/down, financial position, industrial relations.
- After selection, some parameters become obsolete (history, dealings with other vendors, financial position); track **quality, price, delivery performance** (three major, maximum share).
- Supplier evaluation: continuous; not only select/reject the lot — improving vendor quality is also the manufacturer's **shared responsibility**.
- **Supplier certification:** **ISO 9001** (quality), **ISO 14001** (environment), **ISO 45001** (work hygiene/safety); if certified, the decision is easy.

### 37.4 Supplier Relationship Management (SRM)
- After vendor selection (say 4 vendors for a component), manage relationships; vendors differ in quality/price/delivery/contract terms (some JIT, some ask you to hold inventory, some very good on quality).
- Collaboration → mutually beneficial relationship → trust, open communication, maximizing both profits, sharing/mitigating risk, access to the other organization's resources (technology, skills).
- SRM steps: **define SRM** (align strategy — a risk-taking manufacturer vs a safe-environment supplier); **identify key suppliers** aligned with the strategy; **assess capabilities** (capacity, turnover, market expansion, manpower/technical skills, renovation frequency); **establish governance structure** (roles, responsibilities, decision-making; the retailer absorbing maximum uncertainty gets a higher margin); **supplier collaboration initiatives** (help a small supplier implement supplier-management/inventory/WMS software); **foster open communication**; **implement a performance matrix** (KPIs per lot per supplier); **development programs** (**L&T, Havells, ABB** organize seminars for retailers to educate them about new products).
- SRM objectives: leverage supplier capability (**Apple + UPS/DHL**); cost reduction (**Apple** China → India; **Philips** co-manufacturers); security of supply (multiple nearby/overseas vendors); become the **customer of choice**.
- SRM challenges: too focused on cost (ignoring quality/delivery/variety/innovation — product life cycle fell from 5 years to 2; **70–80% of smartphone users change phones every 2–3 years**); no alliance/mismatched risk profile; no open communication; ensuring a win-win (**Walmart** feeds large orders; **Alibaba** = 5% of China's GDP).
- **Supplier segmentation:** two parameters — quantity and price/value (50,000 items @ ₹5,000 vs 200 pieces @ ₹50,000). Automobile: engine highly priced/valuable; screws huge quantity, low value.
- **Kraljic matrix — four categories:** **strategic** suppliers (high value, high risk), **leverage** suppliers (high profit, low supply risk), **bottleneck** suppliers (low profit, high supply risk), **non-critical** suppliers (low profit, low supply risk).
- Criteria: how much you spend, risk taken by the supplier, criticality of the component, performance (quality, price revision, delivery), industrial relations, market position, geographic location (nearby → reduce lead time, JIT, less inventory; overseas → quality/price but no JIT/zero-inventory).
- **ABC analysis for suppliers:** 10–20% suppliers → ~**70%** value (engines, tyres, chassis, steel body); ~30% moderate suppliers (20%); 40–50% suppliers → ~10% volume (screws).
- Tailored SRM: **strategic** → develop competitive advantage; **leverage** → efficiency; **bottleneck** → maintain safety stock; **non-critical** → efficient transaction management.
- Maruti examples: **SX4** not very successful but partners still supply; Vitara Brezza, Grand Vitara, Swift, Desire; highest-sold Alto and Maruti 800.

### 37.5 Global Sourcing
- Reasons: enhanced features/quality, reduced price, access to the **latest technology**, corporate social responsibility.
- Risks: political disruption, disaster, dispute, **Russia-Ukraine war**; infrastructure/local context.
- **Colorado manufacturer case (machine products):** machine products are high-quantity and require special operations (used in home, garage, vehicles); fluctuating fortunes until 1990, then demand surged → expansion; **build-to-order** → high lead time; market share eroded by competitors' shorter lead time/lower cost; outdated material source planning → **$1 million excess inventory** went obsolete; average lead time **12 weeks** (~double competitors). New president (expert in lean manufacturing/supply management) developed a **3–5 year strategy** to globally implement lean, minimize total cost and lead time (lacked in-house strategic sourcing). **Cross-functional team** (purchasing, marketing, sales, R&D, manufacturing); objective to reduce lead time by **80% (to ~2 weeks)**, competitors were at 5–6 weeks; identified **critical parts** → local players, non-critical parts → maintain inventory; assessed and selected qualified suppliers; consolidated suppliers and fed them large orders (promise: reduce lead time, maintain quality, minimize price). Implemented **Kanban quantities** (bins per component requirement which vendors replenish). Results: **lead time 6–8 weeks → 2 days**; **on-time delivery 50% → 95%**; quality improved; **price stability 30%**; **inventory dropped 50%**.

## Lecture 38 — Vendor Rating

### 38.1 Vendor Rating — Purpose
- **Vendor rating = a formal evaluation system for all vendors/suppliers** providing components; it applies to **repetitive purchasing decisions** once external partners are fixed.
- For a component, you may have fixed **4/5/6 suppliers** depending on the situation (few vs many vs one); continuously monitor their performance against predefined criteria.
- Major criteria: **price, quality, delivery schedule, services** (query handling, complaint handling, promptness); other criteria: industrial relations, financial position, innovation/renovation.
- Purpose: assign a rating/score; prioritize; **allocate the maximum order to the highest-rated** vendor; provide feedback for continuous improvement/benchmarking; **minimize subjectivity** in judgment; select vendors for a new model (e.g., model B).

### 38.2 IQC Process (Screw Example)
- Raw material → **IQC (incoming quality control)**: a lot of **10,000 units**, sample **250 units**; check dimensions (length ± tolerance, diameter, thread diameter) and qualitative measures (extra material on the thread → tool wear, damaging the hole; visual/aesthetic shape, color, flavor).
- Decisions: lot OK → store → production floor → finished goods; lot not OK → send back to the vendor; **third option = segregation** (urgent need; segregate, move OK to store, rejected back to the raw-material supplier; segregation cost of **5–10 people**, **debit note** raised to the vendor).
- After using 500 screws, **10–15 missing**/cap absent/threads missing/shape deviated → **zero-hour rejection** (material rejected before processing) → send back + debit note.
- Quality factors: meeting all specifications (screw diameter, thread diameter, cap diameter, screw length); visual inspection; minimize rework/repair.
- Competitive pricing: keep both the **lowest- and highest-priced** vendor (cannot eliminate any; develop vendors); price stability/accuracy; stable pricing; accurate billing; prior notice of price changes.
- Delivery: variability (a vendor who says "5 days ± 3 days") → extra inventory (extra cost).
- Services: complaint resolution speed, communication, resupply.
- Reliability, innovation (readiness to accept feedback/technical improvements), relationship (communication, transparency, collaboration).

### 38.3 Need for Vendor Rating
- Evaluate performance for informed decisions (delivery, price, quality, services); the decision should not be biased in the purchasing department; quantitative recording; allocate order percentages (e.g., **70% to the best, 30% to the rest**).
- Identify strengths/weaknesses; product life of 4/5/10 years → technological advancements shorten it → continuous/repetitive design change → supplier collaboration; **supplier development** is the manufacturing unit's responsibility (train, share best practices; implement inventory/WMS/ERP at the supplier end).
- Risk mitigation (safety stock for delivery variability; alternatives for quality/cost optimization); reducing rework; performance benchmarks; continuous improvement in SRM; transparent decisions.
- **Ola example:** Ola owns no taxis; its stakeholders are local taxi unions/owners/drivers; transparent behavior (comfort, cleanliness, optimized route, charging options) and **rating each ride**; feedback determines the next ride allocation (nearby best-rated driver); drivers request good ratings.

### 38.4 Vendor Rating Steps
- **Define the evaluation criteria clearly** (varies by industry — price for repetitive/routine items; quality for high-status products like **Rolex/BMW**; safety for airlines; the same screw matters differently in a desk, chair, car, or aeroplane).
- Evaluate all criteria; **assign weights** (shirt example: cost, quality, brand, comfort, availability; one person gives **30% comfort, 20% quality, 15% cost, 10% brand**; another gives **70% cost, 10% quality, 5% brand**).
- **Gather data** (IQC, production-floor zero-hour rejection, accounting/purchase department order acceptance/processing, payment, issue settlement).
- **Evaluate performance:** score 1–10; multiply score by weightage (cost 20% → ×0.20; quality 15% → ×0.15); overall rating = sum per vendor (**V1, V2, V3**).
- **Review/validate per lot** and give continuous feedback to the vendor (transparent, e.g., Ola shares feedback with drivers).
- **Use ratings for decision making** (Ola allocates rides based on ranking + proximity).

### 38.5 Techniques
- **Categorical plan:** subjective method; parameters quality, delivery, price; sub-parameters rated **good/satisfactory/poor** (subjective — strict vs lenient marking).
- **Weighted plan:** score × weightage, summed (vendor 1: quality 8/10, delivery 7, price 5; weightages quality 40%, delivery 35%, rest 25%).
- **Cost ratio plan:** cost incurred to acquire services — delivery cost **₹5,000** on a total lot of **₹1 lakh** → **10%** (benchmark 7% → improve delivery/logistics); inventory cost **20%** vs benchmark **12–13%** → reduce extra stock.
- **Decision matrix:** pairwise comparison of criteria (criterion 1 > criterion 2 → weight 1, else 0).
- **Service cost ratio:** intangible services (customer-care setup cost).
- **IBM quality rating system:** desired cost of inspection vs actual; actual inspection is higher; goal = no inspection (error-free; **quality is free**).
- **Quality rating system:** lots received vs rejected → **lot quality index**; L1 accepted, L2 rejected by sampling but segregated/run, L3 rejected/reworked at the supplier, L4 rejected/returned (transportation, storage, inspection, transformation, maintenance cost; paying for new but receiving repaired → cost priority higher, performance reduced).

### 38.6 Vendor Rating Format (Industry Example)
- **Vendor rating by co-maker (manufacturer)** for a vendor number; date of issue, vendor/co-vendor name, part name/code (10 screw types → different codes).
- **Quality description sheet:** screw diameter, thread diameter, length; visual color/flavor/dimension.
- Parameters:
  - **Quality:** 25 lots → 0% rejected → 5/5; 2 lots rejected → 1.
  - **Quality complaint note:** none → 5.
  - **CAPA** (corrective and preventive action) report submitted and no repetition in the next 10 lots → 10/10 (not fixed; some products 20, some 5).
  - **Detailed inspection report** shared → 5, else 0.
  - **Vendor process maturity/setup** (IQC, PQC, OQC) → 5.
  - **Vendor aligned to** the Philips program → 5.
  - **Rejection on line** (zero-hour rejection): **<0.1% → 5**, **<2% → 3**.
  - **Price performance:** lowered by 5% → 10; <2% → 5; increased → 0.
  - **Delivery:** highest priority (**40 marks** for on-time delivery); responsiveness (CAPA report speed; PO confirmation within 3 days → 3 marks).
- **Overall rating:** **<50 poor, 50–80 average, >80 good.**

### 38.7 Advantages and Tools
- Minimize subjectivity; feedback from all areas (purchase, accounting, quality, store, delivery); better communication; overall control; specific action; continuously review standards.
- The **vendor is the first pillar** of the supply chain — manage it effectively/efficiently and supply chain performance starts there.
- Multi-criteria decision-making techniques: **AHP (analytical hierarchy process), ANP (analytical network process), TOPSIS, DEMATEL** — find the current position, the gap, and fill it.

## Lecture 39 — Supply Chain Coordination

### 39.1 Coordination — Basics
- Three major players: **supplier (backward supply chain), manufacturer (conversion), distributor (forward supply chain)**; plus the retailer.
- Coordination **within one organization** (functional units — marketing, production, quality, R&D, ISO, HR, purchase) and **throughout the supply chain** (suppliers, manufacturers, distributors, retailers).
- Goal: **optimize overall performance** — improve efficiency, reduce cost, enhance responsiveness, ensure timely delivery.
- If the demand function is known, **share the information electronically** throughout the supply chain (supplier, manufacturer, warehouse, inventory, transportation) without modification/intervention → optimize transportation (know units to transport and truck capacity; consolidate orders or use full capacity).
- Involves **collaboration, information sharing, and synchronization**.
- Key elements: demand forecasting, inventory management, production planning, transportation, logistics, communication system.

### 39.2 Coordination with Suppliers (Backward)
- **Collaborative forecasting:** manufacturer predicts demand (market representatives give facts/figures) → shares the production schedule with suppliers; e.g., of **100 items**, by rating V1 (highest) gets **45 units**, another gets **25**, V3 (moderate) gets the rest.
- Real-time sharing of sales trends/market conditions without modification.
- **Vendor-managed inventory:** the vendor manages inventory up to the manufacturer (in the manufacturing unit or a nearby storage house; JIT; manufacturer allows the vendor to use the facility and pays as consumed).
- **Quality assurance:** share the quality description sheet/matrix (screw length/diameter/thread diameter, preference limits) → minimize returns/rejections.
- **Continuous improvement:** record parameters; if a process deviates, inform the vendor → **CAPA**.
- **Communication and collaboration:** align suppliers with the manufacturing strategy (JIT → supplier must maintain JIT; provide training/help/technology/inventory software); regular meetings; **integrated IT system** (share production planning, track orders/inventory; vendor attached to the same platform).

### 39.3 Coordination with Distributors (Forward)
- **Order fulfillment:** respond to customer demand, pick/pack/distribute, last-mile delivery; collaborate with the manufacturer on units, features, customer service level demanded, and inventory replenishment.
- **Distribution planning:** optimize routes and schedules, reduce transportation cost.
- **Inventory management/stock replenishment:** e.g., **Walmart, Vishal Mega Mart, Spencer's** — shelf inventory + warehouse safety stock. **Point-of-sale data** at billing; as items deplete (500 → 496), share with transportation, warehousing, manufacturer, co-manufacturer, distributor, and raw-material suppliers → better planning and safety stock (less storage).
- **Customer service/reliable delivery:** the retailer is the main stakeholder interacting with the end customer and has the best information about demand (even why a customer rejects your product/rejects it for a competitor) → extract via a fast communication network.
- **Return management:** efficient returns/exchanges; e-commerce — about **50% of customers** check return eligibility before placing the order.
- **Data sharing and analytics:** share the production schedule with raw-material suppliers; the distributor provides insights on customer demand/market trends; decide how many models to run (e.g., **3 of 5 models**).
- **Performance matrix:** delivery accuracy, on-time delivery, on-time return pickup, correct billing information, customer satisfaction.
- **Supplier-distributor coordination:** purchase orders (the distributor's needs go to the supplier, and point-of-sale information also to the raw-material supplier to avoid the bullwhip — order 5, produce 5 not 15); production plan; delivery/transportation (ship only 5 and consolidate to utilize capacity).

### 39.4 Bullwhip Effect
- **Lack of coordination:** no pure communication; information is not transparent → inefficiencies and sub-optimal outcomes.
- **Bullwhip example:** retailer orders **10** → distributor orders **15** (safety stock) → manufacturer produces **25** → raw-material supplier receives **30**; demand is only **10** → 20 extra inventory.
- **Causes:** demand forecasting inaccuracies; **order batching** (produce 30 in one go to minimize per-unit cost/changeover, but higher inventory/handling cost — a trade-off); **price fluctuations** (petrol/diesel midnight price revision — price rise → huge lines/hoarding; price drop → no one buys, then a rush after → a spike though actual demand is saturated; crude-oil market forces → daily price changes).
- **Negative impact:** increased cost, excess inventory, stock-out.
- **Obstacles to coordination:**
  - **Incentive obstacles** (resistance to new technology).
  - **Information-processing obstacles** (not sharing correct information; manipulating; not wanting vendors to know different prices/policies).
  - **Operational obstacles** (production bottlenecks, capacity constraints; bottleneck example: **5s, 6s, 10s, 7s, 6s** → the 10s stage processes two while the next processes one → pile-up; assign two persons to the 10s stage; transportation delays; quality-control problems — OQC holds up 50,000 packed items).
  - **Pricing obstacles** (different pricing policies, lack of transparency, keeping negotiation power).
  - **Behavioral obstacles** (resistance to change; lack of trust — today's supplier may tomorrow acquire the manufacturer or expand).
- Bullwhip: information is amplified at every stage; reduce the number of players (1–2) to reduce the inflation, but industries needing a long structure should implement IT infrastructure/technology for transparent quick information sharing; caused by order batching, price fluctuation, and lack of information sharing.

### 39.5 Impact of Lack of Coordination
- **Manufacturing cost** increases: producing in bulk and standardizing the process minimizes per-unit production cost, but frequently changing over from one model to another increases cost.
- **Inventory cost:** everyone maintains inventory (retailer, distributor/wholesaler, carrying-forward agent, manufacturer, co-manufacturers, raw-material suppliers), often across ~**20 storage houses/warehouses** — a non-value-adding activity/wastage.
- **Replenishment lead time** increases because many players exist and information is not quickly shared.
- **Transportation cost** increases: sometimes transporting **10 units** when the vehicle can carry **200** → capacity not fully utilized.
- **Shipping and receiving cost** increases.
- **Level of product availability** suffers: with no clear communication, sometimes extra inventory and sometimes stock-out.
- **Profitability** decreases because the overall cost increases → margins decrease.

### 39.6 Managerial Actions to Improve Coordination
- **Aligning goals and incentives:** possible only when all stakeholders see a **win-win**; if the network must be responsive (variety, quality, highest service level through distributor/retailer/online), all stages must be aligned (raw-material suppliers provide variety, retailer maintains variety, warehouse maintains variety, production runs all possible variety) — but if the retailer shares the risk without sharing the incentives, ask why the retailer should be responsive; share incentives/risk meticulously throughout the chain.
- **Pricing for coordination:** avoid lot-size-based quantity discounts; use a **two-part tariff** (some fixed cost, some variable cost) and volume-based incentives/discounts; contracts such as **buy-back, revenue sharing and quantity flexibility** optimize product availability and supply chain profit.
- **Altering sales-force incentives:** the **sell-in** approach (selling to the retailer) is not the ultimate objective — you should **sell-through** to the end user; if you sell to the retailer but the retailer cannot sell further, it is a problem.
- **Improving information visibility and accuracy:** point-of-sale data — as soon as the product code is scanned, that information should be shared quickly.
- **Collaborative forecasting and planning:** all stakeholders participate and see how transportation, warehousing and raw-material providers must behave and design their functions accordingly.
- **Designing single-stage control of replenishment:** retailer→wholesaler→distributor→manufacturer→co-manufacturer→raw-material supplier should not cascade; **one replenishment order** shared throughout the chain without intervention (software: vendor-managed inventory, warehouse management inventory).
- **Improving operational performance:** reduce lead time (with sure information, process the order and reduce delivery time); reduce lot size (smaller lots reduce fluctuation between stages but raise manufacturing cost while lowering inventory cost) → decreasing information distortion; lower fixed ordering, transportation and receiving cost facilitates cost reduction.
- **Rationing:** stabilize demand during artificial spikes (petrol price-hike midnight → everyone refills to full capacity; rationing schemes so demand is spread rather than a rush followed by 10 dead days).
- **Pricing strategy to stabilize orders:** Walmart's **everyday low pricing**; festival/seasonal offers at Amazon/Flipkart increase orders by **60%** → extra resources (machines, manpower, facility) for a 1–2-month season are wasted; stabilize demand uniformly so full capacity is used efficiently.
- **Building strategic partnership and trust:** trust and information sharing are essential for coordination; avoid duplicating efforts; settle conflicts (delivery, quality, pricing) through a common mechanism.

### 39.7 Supply Chain Macro Processes and Case Studies
- The **supply chain macro processes** (revisited): **customer relationship management** (forward chain — selling products/services, negotiating, after-sales service, order management, handling customer queries), **internal supply chain management** (within the manufacturing unit — planning production, scheduling/sequencing activities, utilizing capacity, planning demand and supply), and **supplier relationship management** (all activities between supplier and manufacturer — quality, cost, price, variety, changeovers, models, disputes, delivery, sharing design specifications).
- **Amazon case:** leveraged strong IT infrastructure and strong CRM; success = ability to **customize product offerings to individual customers** (tailored supply chain). **One-click ordering** (Industry 4.0): for routine fixed products (e.g., a fixed tea brand/size/flavor/quantity), just push a button → order placed and payment auto-done; for customized products, feed specifications and Amazon provides them; handling routine orders this way makes Amazon efficient. Rare customer-service calls must get the best positive experience (call-back button, mention availability time; heavy call-center traffic handled via call-back). Uses SAP, ERP, Oracle, Salesforce and Microsoft, but **develops innovative platforms in-house** — that is the competitive edge.
- **Hindustan Oil Limited case:** financially strong, customer-centric, focused on quality, delivery and R&D (latest lubricants); success attributed to a strategic approach based on customer satisfaction, commitment to quality and sustainability, and investment in R&D; an efficient supply chain ensuring timely delivery, variety and quality to a large customer base; diverse products/services (refining, marketing, distribution of petroleum products); growing revenue; sustainability measures to reduce the carbon footprint (petroleum products have a high carbon footprint).
- **Conclusion:** coordination among all stakeholders (forward with distributors, reverse with suppliers) so that any information received at any point is shared throughout; strong healthy relationships built on **trust, transparency and shared goals**, with incentives shared so each stakeholder's objective is optimized; collaborative planning, forecasting and replenishment (CPFR) manages efficiency and effectiveness throughout the chain.

## Lecture 40 — Supply Chain Performance: Balanced Scorecard Approach

### 40.1 Supply Chain Performance — Effectiveness and Efficiency
- Target: **effectiveness** (deliver the right package of product/services) and **efficiency** (do it cost-effectively); align all activities/functional areas across all stakeholders from the start to the end point.
- Basic structure: raw-material suppliers → manufacturers/co-manufacturers → distribution network (the distribution agency can also be considered a customer) → customer, with returns processed.
- **Cost function components:** raw-material acquisition cost (negotiate quality/variety; vendor rating → get maximum from the best vendor); transportation cost (routing, vehicle utilization, mode); storage/warehousing cost (inventory, handling, extra packaging, kitting); manufacturing cost (bulk production → economies of scale lower per-unit cost but raise inventory cost; ensure market consumption); distribution/end-mile cost (EVs, bicycles to minimize cost).
- Key measures: on-time delivery, stock availability, order accuracy, production; e.g., quick delivery from a fulfillment center is a **warehousing** measure (record, pick, pack, deliver).

### 40.2 Quantitative and Qualitative Measures
- **Qualitative:** cannot be quantified — feeling about the product, delivery experience, satisfactory/less-satisfactory feedback, post-purchase dissonance, after-sales service.
- **Quantitative:** on-time delivery (five minutes late or early), quality defects (complaints), **flexibility** (changeover from model A to B in 10 vs 5 minutes → manufacturing flexibility; flexibility also applies to warehousing/transportation — a small family cake vs a birthday cake for a hundred people).
- Quantitative measures split into **financial** and **non-financial**:
  - **Financial (direct):** return on investment, profit, inventory savings/inventory cost, purchase-order cost/raw-material acquisition cost.
  - **Non-financial:** product quality, on-time delivery — they indirectly drive financial performance (enhanced customer experience → loyalty → more frequent purchases → profit and market share increase).
- Two basic purposes of defining KPIs: (1) measure the current performance level, (2) benchmark against the industry leader and bridge the gap; they show where you are now and where you want to be in 5/10 years.

### 40.3 Financial Performance Measures
- **Raw-material cost / total cost of ownership:** not only the raw-material price but storage, transportation and handling of raw material until it enters production.
- **Revenue from goods sold.**
- **Activity-based cost:** inventory-management cost; logistics cost (distribution, transportation, warehousing). In **India logistics cost ≈ 14–15%** vs global average **8–10%** and developed nations **6–7%** — spending 6–7% extra directly reduces profit; reduce via alternative routes/modes, warehouse updation/automation and inventory minimization.
- **Inventory holding (carrying) cost**, damage during handling, **transportation cost** by mode (air/ship/road; **ship is the cheapest and most sustainable**), **cost of expired/perishable goods** (raw material expiring in the warehouse or finished product expiring in the fulfillment center), **penalty for incorrect filling of orders** (segregate/send back → transportation cost, delay, production-line stoppage, idle manpower), **credit for incorrect fill** (vendor bears the cost), **cost of goods returned by the customer** (defect, quality, functionality, manufacturing, packaging or delivery issues), **credit for goods returned to the supplier.**

### 40.4 Non-Financial Performance Measures
- **Cycle time:** the time required for any activity (lecture recording — 45–46 minutes recorded, edited to ~40 minutes final).
- **Supply chain lead time:** transform raw material into finished goods and ship to the end customer; **order-to-delivery** (in-house delivery from warehouse/fulfillment center to the location).
- **Customer service level:** qualitative (variety needed, level of innovation, delivery experience, expectations) and quantitative (lead time the customer can wait).
- **Order fill rate:** how quickly orders are filled from existing stock; **stock-out rate:** if 100 orders are placed and 95 are filled on time → fill rate 95%, stock-out 5%; lost sales → the customer shifts to someone else who maintained inventory.
- **Back-order level:** orders pending because of insufficient inventory, cleared when inventory arrives. Automobile example — **Maruti/Hyundai/Toyota** waiting periods of 3/6/12 months (partly a marketing strategy to signal high demand, partly because a model can have **>200 variants** → minimum 200 cars of the same make in inventory; **Grand Vitara** example).
- **Probability of on-time delivery:** what proportion of demand is met on time.
- **Inventory levels:** raw material, WIP, finished goods, spare parts, rejected material and **returns** (a broken component can be replaced — the product is not fully rejected); balance so there is no stock-out and no excess.
- **Resource utilization (five M's — man, money, machine, material, methods):** IIT Kharagpur lecture theatres idle for 2–2.5 months (mid-May to end-July) → run online courses/NPTEL and industry training programs; a low-demand model B line runs only 7–8 hours vs model A 24×7 → combine model B with a closely related model C/D to minimize changeover; warehousing capacity 100 ton but storing only 70–75 ton; logistics — a truck booked Kharagpur→Calcutta returns empty; **financial resources/inventory** — ₹10 crore inventory earning nothing vs 4–5% if deposited in a bank.

### 40.5 Qualitative Performance Measures
- **Customer satisfaction:** Swiggy/Zomato — food quality, taste, hygiene, packaging, whether the courier wore gloves, smooth payment, cancellation/refund.
- **Customer complaints** (delayed delivery, etc.) and **customer response time** (how quickly a query is answered).
- **Product quality:** Zomato ratings — the highest-rated restaurant is reflected first; e-commerce gives the general rating of suppliers.
- **Delivery experience** and **order accuracy** (ordered = received; cooking instructions such as "less spicy" followed; correct order delivered on time).

### 40.6 The Balanced Scorecard — Framework
- A tool / comprehensive framework to record all performance measures (financial and non-financial) and **align business activities to the vision and strategy** (e.g., a responsive strategy → a responsive supply chain throughout).
- Ensures **internal and external communication** (internal within the organization; external with warehousing, distribution, transportation partners and suppliers).
- Developed by **Robert and David**; includes financial and non-financial measures across **four perspectives: financial, customer, internal business process, and learning and growth**.
- Center/heart of the model = **vision and strategy** (compete on cost, responsiveness, quick delivery, etc.); for each strategy define: **objective → measure → target → initiative**.
- **Customer perspective:** how we should appear to customers / how customers evaluate us. Example: minimize delivery time → measure = **lead time** (from placing the order to receiving it); target staged (5–6 days → 4–5 days → 2–3 days → single-day → same-day); initiatives (courier picks within **4 hours**, delivers to the nearest hub in **6 hours**, hub-to-hub/customer within **24 hours**; Delhi→Kolkata 24 hours, Kolkata→Kharagpur 5–6 hours, final delivery ~2 hours).
- **Learning and growth perspective:** unskilled / semi-skilled / skilled workers; without learning, growth or a visible career path people get bored and leave; objective = move unskilled → semi-skilled → skilled (skilling/reskilling is never-ending); measures = skill level, technical efficiency, managerial efficiency, sorting people by functional unit (marketing, purchase, manufacturing, quality, R&D); initiatives = training and development programs.
- **Internal business process perspective:** objective = reduce the **fall of rejection rate (FOR)**; measure = FOR (2 defects of 100 products → 2%); target staged (a new model from 12–15% → <10% → 8–10% → 5–7%); this requires investment in the process/technology/manpower, so evaluate whether the investment actually reduces FOR. Other internal aspects: **process automation** (remove faulty manual operations), training at critical stages where most defects occur, removing **process bottlenecks** (a 5 s stage vs a 10 s stage → uniformity across ~500 stages of the production line), and aligning the number of activities per person.
- **Financial perspective:** how stakeholders view us financially — cost of production, logistics cost (India **14% → target 10%**); since **70% of freight goes by road** (cost, delay, accidents, weather), shift to **ship** (≈₹1.5/km vs road ₹4–5/km) and manage the resources made obsolete; cash flow, liquidity ratio (high inventory ties up cash), ROI (investing **10%** in IoT but getting **2%** return → ensure returns), financial results monitored monthly/weekly, and long-term investment (**Jio** — free services in year 1, capture ~**60%** share, then price).
- How the four perspectives link: customers judge brands (**Sony** TV/sound, **Hitachi/Lloyd** AC, **Uber** globally); stakeholders (this year **10%** profit → 5% dividend + 5% reinvested vs all 10% now); business processes create value for the customer.
- **Productivity example:** the same input yields more output, or less input yields the same output; monitor capacity utilization, trial production and spare-part production weekly/monthly.
- **IIT Kharagpur example:** run industry training programs during the 2–2.5-month summer and December breaks to use idle high-end infrastructure, generate revenue and strengthen industry links (placements, training).

### 40.7 Benefits, Alignment and Criticisms
- **Continuous improvement** tools: **kaizen, six sigma, lean manufacturing**; monitor daily/weekly.
- **Eveready India Limited example:** before applying for **ISO 9001** (quality) and **ISO 14001** (environment), the company trained internal employees (one each from quality, production and store) for ~10 days and conducted an exam → they became certified **internal ISO auditors**; maintained records for 6 months–1 year; then applied for external certification with confidence.
- **Benefits:** align performance measures and strategy at all levels and stakeholders; the best way of communication; a **single thread** attaching all stakeholders; compare with best practices/benchmarks in KPIs; attach the organization's mission statement and strategic plan to all four perspectives.
- **Adoption:** ~**60% of Fortune 500 companies** use the balanced scorecard.
- **Criticisms:** lack of a well-defined strategy (the framework assumes one clear strategy, but the environment changes → strategy changes → alignment problem); too much focus on **lagging measures** (missing opportunities from existing strengths); using **generic metrics** not applicable to all players (a 15% player cannot be directly compared with a 5% player); managers manipulating measures to earn short-term rewards.
- **Southwest Airlines case:** used the balanced scorecard for **ground-crew performance** to lower cost through fast **ground turnaround**; **Indigo** — most players keep **180–200 employees per plane**, Indigo manages with **90–100**; train ground staff based on these parameters.
- Limitation: directly connecting R&D (prototypes may not be commercialized for 10 years but may yield value for 20 years) or manufacturing (multiple changeovers needed to provide variety) with financial measures is not possible; **maintenance services** (a 30-minute downtime of the main machine stops production) cannot be linked to profit — hence non-financial measures from all departments are essential; a **dashboard** lets top management see which department is doing well and which is lagging.
- Conclusion: satisfy customer needs more profitably and enhance the customer experience by measuring both financial and non-financial measures across all functional units.
