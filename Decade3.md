## Lecture 21 — Fundamentals of Supply Chain Management (SCM)

### 21.1 The Soap/Detergent Example (Backward Integration)
- Customer wants **detergent or soap** → goes to **Big Bazaar** → gets delivery through the **outbound logistics/distribution network** (distribution centers/fulfillment centers) coming from a manufacturer, e.g. **P&G**.
- Now trace the **backward supply chain** for the raw materials — plastic, packaging, chemicals:
  - **Packaging** is itself a supply chain: packaging comes from a **paper manufacturer**, which gets raw material from the **timber industry**.
  - The timber industry uses a **harvesting machine** (to cut trees and process them), which uses **engines, tyres, steel components** → many more supply chains.
  - The **tyre supply chain** has two major raw materials: **rubber** and **steel wire** — each from its own supply chain. The chain keeps going.
- This is the **system approach** of supply chain management. In **logistics** we focused only on the **distribution, transportation, warehousing, and packaging** part between these players.
- **Key difference:** the complete supply chain structure = all players and activities; logistics = the material movement/storage/ packaging between them.

### 21.2 Definitions
- **P&G example:** P&G designed **six different baskets** and provides many products under them, served across many countries; the key point is **how value is added to the end delivery**.
- **Sunil Chopra and Peter Meindl:** supply chain consists of **all parties involved, directly or indirectly** — manufacturers, suppliers, transporters, warehouses, retailers, and even **customers**.
- **Daeal & Chen:** a group of **interconnected participant companies** that add value to a stream of **transformed inputs** from their source of origin to end products/services demanded by the **designated end customer**.
- **Customer is a stakeholder** — the supply chain ends with the end customer who uses the product; it is a network that **matches demand with supply**.

### 21.3 Evolution of the Concept
- Initially only **logistics** was considered; it was made a separate department and optimized for **15–20 years**; then the realization came that this is not enough → a **comprehensive view** was needed to handle all supply chain activities.
- **Example:** designing and marketing a product in your own lab (R&D) but not wanting to go into manufacturing/sourcing/distribution → needed a **complete supply chain solution** (e.g., **Hitachi** packages: sourcing, packaging, warehousing, manufacturing — many functional areas can be outsourced).
- Concept moved from manufacturing to the **services industry**. But no services industry is complete without the product industry:
  - **Educational services** (this lecture) need pen, screen, camera, lighting, recording room.
  - **Healthcare** — doctor's diagnosis is service, but treatment needs **medicines, vaccines, injections** (products).
  - **Consultancy** is near-pure service, but implementing advice may lead to purchasing/building.
- **Backward supply chain** = supplying raw material from suppliers to other suppliers to the manufacturing unit. **Forward supply chain** = distributing from the OEM to the customer (outbound).

### 21.4 The Three Flows (All Bidirectional)
1. **Information** — manufacturer → customer (education about product/services, advertisements, billboards, electronic/print media, manuals, installation by engineer); customer → manufacturer (feedback/ratings — used to redesign and rethink the delivery system). Information includes **product design specifications, pricing information, sourcing information**.
2. **Product** — finished goods and semi-finished goods flow forward (supplier→manufacturer, sub-manufacturer→manufacturer); **reverse flow** for recycling, reuse, refurbishment, warranty/guarantee returns, and packaging (manufacturer's responsibility to collect).
3. **Funds** — earlier only reverse (customer → manufacturer); now with **one-click returns** refunds must flow manufacturer → customer, so funds flow bidirectionally too.

### 21.5 Cost Function of the Supply Chain
- **Material (raw material) cost** → **inbound logistics cost** → **manufacturing cost** → **outbound logistics cost** → **handling cost** (warehouse) → **distribution cost** → **last-mile delivery cost** → **inventory cost** (raw material, semi-finished, finished goods).
- **Extra costs:** packaging cost, **returns** (end-of-life or under warranty), **sustainability** measures (initially add cost, long term reduce carbon footprint and cost), **information cost** (designing the platform for sharing information).
- You cannot simply remove an activity (packaging, storage, inventory); you **manage the level** to reduce cost — **Walmart** keeps inventory at **centralized store distribution centers** rather than at 10 different locations, reducing inventory cost.

### 21.6 Objectives of SCM
- **Timely delivery** (maintain inventory near the customer; keep delivery promises, e.g. within **one day**).
- **Effective inventory management** — two things only: **not stock out** and **not keep excess inventory**.
- **Flexibility and responsiveness** — if customer asks **5 units** you ship 5; **500** → scale to 500; **5000** → 5000; deliver models A, B, C, D quickly.
- **Efficient and cost effective** — lower inventory, inbound/outbound logistics, warehousing, handling, distribution cost.
- **One system/one family of stakeholders** — strategic decisions (cost effectiveness vs responsiveness) shared with all stakeholders.
- **Improve logistics management** — optimize delivery time, cost, amount; be careful with same-day delivery (need enough orders to enjoy economies of scale; running a private fleet for 1–2 orders is not cost effective).
- **Increase overall customer value** — reduce non-value-adding activities, add value-adding activities.
- **Spread the network** (from 10 pin codes to 12/20/30), **increase demand fulfillment**, **quality** (right product, right quality, right time), and **improved integrated technology**.

### 21.7 Supply Chain Decisions (Three Levels)
1. **Strategic (top-level management)** — long-term commitment of resources (10/15/20 years):
   - In-house vs. **outsource** (the supplier is part of your chain for ~20 years), **location** of suppliers/warehouses/distribution centers/manufacturing centers (factory near markets to cut outbound cost), **product to be manufactured**, **mode of transportation**, **information system setup**.
2. **Planning (middle level)** — medium term (quarter to 1 year):
   - Which **market supplied by which factory** (market 1-3 → factory 1; market 4-5 → factory 2) considering capacity, demand, raw-material availability, cost, distance; **plan inventory** at raw/semi/finished level; **subcontracting** (e.g., packaging); **timing and size** of the market proportion.
3. **Operational (lower level)** — day-to-day routine:
   - Handle day orders, allocate orders, set **order due date**, generate **pick list** (today X units of model A, Y of model B, Z of model C), set delivery schedules, place **replenishment orders**.

### 21.8 Types of Supply Chain Models
- **Continuous flow model** — continuous flow of product with little variation; compete only on **cost** (e.g., **steel industry** — sheets of a given thickness are almost the same across brands).
- **Agile model** — unpredictable demand that must be prioritized (one car used as **normal taxi** vs as **ambulance** — ambulance demand can come anytime).
- **Fast model** — **perishable** products; packaging must be consumed today or at most tomorrow (e.g., **milk products**).
- **Flexible model** — demand affected by **seasons**; scale up in peak season, scale down otherwise (keeping extra inventory leads to selling at **50–75% off**, an extra cost).
- **Efficient model** — uniform products (steel, **sugar**, **salt** industries, profits very less; only packaging/quality differ) → must be cost efficient.
- **Custom model** — **luxury brands** (BMW, Mercedes, Rolex) — specialized/customized services (choose interior, boost engine, more boot space).

### 21.9 Three Concepts of SCM
- **Configuration** — overall architecture of the supply chain (forward/backward; e.g. **Dell** direct supply chain with no intermediary → needs **vertical integration**; alternatively many stakeholders → more efficient but less control).
- **Relationship** — **arms-length** vs. **close relationship** with stakeholders (sharing the "secret recipe"; for quick renovation, suppliers must also renovate raw material quickly).
- **Coordination** — coordination across functional areas/stakeholders; without it (information lag, inaccurate demand prediction) inventory, manufacturing, and order fulfillment all get hampered.
- **Challenges/opportunities:** adoption of technology, collaboration with partners, and **focus on sustainability** (regulators stress sustainable practices; customers demand technologically strong services).

## Lecture 22 — SC Operations: Manufacturing versus Service SC

### 22.1 Supply Chain Operations — Position
- Many researchers say SCM is part of operations management and others say the opposite; the professor's view: the complete supply chain has many sub-functions, and **operation management is part of supply chain management**.
- Sub-functional areas: generating raw material, **transportation**, converting raw material into finished goods, **distribution**, **warehousing**, **retail operations**.

### 22.2 Components of Supply Chain Operations
- **Supplier sourcing** — **sourcing ≠ purchasing**: sourcing means setting up the supply chain, looking at long-term stakeholders; once the long-term relationship is established, regular supply is ensured through **repetitive purchase orders** (purchasing).
- **Supplier–purchaser collaboration** — as long as the same product/model runs, you need the same supplier; keep the supplier informed of design/parameter/raw-material changes (e.g., earlier copper, now aluminium).
- **Supplier information management** — number of suppliers (S1, S2, S3), orders per supplier, pricing, quality, durability, delivery frequency/responsiveness.
- **Purchase order management** — record the order, process it (locating where the product/inventory is), pack, ship, and final last-mile distribution.
- **Inventory management** — inventory of raw material, semi-finished goods, finished goods, and **rejected material** (zero-hour rejection or market returns under warranty); resell/refurbish/recycle operations.
- **Warehouse and transportation management** — temporary storage or **cross docking**; different warehouse operations.
- **Supply chain planning** — three management levels (top/medium/lower) as in Lecture 21.
- **Shipping** — shipping partners/agents take the product from one distribution hub to another near the customer; last-mile delivery through them.

### 22.3 Main Functions of Operation Management
- **Planning** — ensure smooth flow of resources.
- **Scheduling** — what type of product/services, where (in-house/outsource), how much, when, and in what order to produce.
  - **Maruti Suzuki Grand Vitara example:** the product basket has many models/variants — petrol base/middle/top, diesel, hybrid, CNG, different colors and engine capacities; you must schedule how many units of each to produce today based on retail-chain information.
- **Control** — monitor progress and make changes; set **standards** by identifying **KPIs**, find the **industry benchmark**, identify the **gap**, and plan **phase-wise** improvement (phase 1 covers this much, phase 2 this much, phase 3 this much).

### 22.4 Supply Chain in Operation Management
- At a **manufacturing facility** raw material is assembled/treated/fixed → final good; this happens only with the smooth supply of raw material through the supply chain.
- If supply chains are efficient: quality is not deteriorated in transit; waste is minimized; inventory is not excessive; quick response to changing customer preferences (info shared with **R&D**, new prototype, source supplies per new prototype).

### 22.5 Product vs. Services — Basic Differences
- **Product is tangible; services are intangible** (some service firms add tangible experience — hotel decoration, amenities like swimming pool/spa/gym).
- **Perishability** — a product can be produced now and consumed later; a service cannot be produced now and delivered later.
- **Heterogeneity** — a product is standardized (targeting standardization to minimize cost); services are heterogeneous (a barber's **500 haircuts** from morning to evening are all different; **customer participation** is continuous).
- **Inseparability** — in services, production and final delivery cannot be separated (production only starts when the customer arrives); in products, production in Delhi and consumption in Calcutta can be separated.

### 22.6 Manufacturing vs. Services Supply Chain
- **Manufacturing (product) supply chain:** highly **standardized** (e.g., a marker pen); production/assembly/packaging/distribution; variability in the production process is not expected; you aim for efficiency, minimum rejection, quality; suppliers → manufacturer (quality checks, segregation) → retailer → customer; rejected items go back to the manufacturer for remanufacturing.
- **Services supply chain:** no tangibility — just delivering the experience. Examples: **healthcare, lawyer (legal advice), property dealer** (maps, location, documentation), **transportation** (flying — no physical product). Even "pure" services use products (law books, records).
  - **Restaurant** — you consume both service and product (meal).
  - **Barber shop example:** during a haircut you may use a particular **shampoo, oil, head massager machine** — ensured through the product supply chain; but as a customer you carry nothing physical — only the **experience** (including how the waiting-queue time was handled). Production starts only when the customer reaches, so **every service is unique**.

### 22.7 SCOR Model Applied to Services
- The basic model (SCOR, by the Supply Chain Management Council) has five stages: **plan, source, make, deliver, return**.
- In **services**, **make and deliver are not two different stages** — you make while you deliver (customer is already in the shop taking a head massage/haircut); services **cannot be inventorized** (no inventory management — instead there is **queue management**, opening temporary service channels).
- In products, all five stages are possible.

### 22.8 Service Industry Examples
- **Healthcare/medical** — doctor's advice/diagnosis, treatments, prescribed medicines (pharmaceutical product industry → **hybrid** industry).
- **Hospitality and tourism** — hotels, restaurants, travel agents (tickets; consume products during the journey).
- **Financial services** — insurance, investment plans, savings schemes.
- **Educational and training** — near-pure service (uses camera, screen, pen, mic, lighting, back-end NPTEL team — but you carry only the experience). You evaluate the professor's delivery, not the back-end team.
- **Professional services** — lawyer, doctor, property consultant.
- **Information technology** — earlier antivirus came on **CDs** (hardware) then installed; now you buy a **key online**, download the software (no hardware).
- **Transportation services** — shipping agents carry physical products but do no value-adding activity.
- **Entertainment** — movie; you take only the experience.
- **Retail** — not a product manufacturer; organizes products, ensures inventory on the shelf (e.g., **Big Bazaar's own brand**; Vishal Mega Mart sells branded products).
- **Property management** — relates both product and services.

### 22.9 Comparison Table (Manufacturing vs. Services)
| Aspect | Manufacturing/Product SC | Services SC |
|---|---|---|
| Supply chain input | Supplier material and forecast information | Customer information |
| Value-adding | Physical production (converting raw material) | Receiving the service (value-adding time); engage the waiting customer (magazines, screens) |
| Demand uncertainty | Managed via inventory (produce and keep) | Managed via **queue** (cannot produce before customer arrives; idle then sudden rush) |
| Supply chain output | Standardized | Heterogeneous (customer is part of the service) |
| Nature of demand | Predictable | Not predictable (can come anytime) |
| Required response | Standardized (smooth flow via inventory) | Customer wants immediate service on reaching |
| Number of SKUs | Limited (one or two retail outlets in a city) | Many (many local/brand garages)
| Product portfolio | Largely homogeneous | Always heterogeneous |
| Delivery network | Depends on nature of product | Multiple networks necessary (e.g., Maruti Suzuki under **Nexa** vs. traditional chain) |
| Inventory management | Manages inventory of supporting products | Queue management; limited inventory turns (1–4 a year) |
| Reverse logistics | Important | Not important generally (but after-sales needs warranty repairs) |
| Production system | Push (produce then push) | Pull (record customer info then serve) |
| Supplier responsiveness | Not critical | Very critical (e.g., special shampoo needed immediately) |
| Customer relationship | Maintained but not that deep | Deep relationship (airlines, hotels) |
| Labor intensive | Low | High (direct customer interaction) |
| Customer involvement | Nowhere | Very high |
| Quality | Easy (standardized product/process design) | Hard (variance is huge) |
| Inventory vs. queue | Inventory exists | Inventory does not exist; queue exists |
- **Manufacturing examples:** automotive, food processing, electronics, heavy engineering. **Services examples:** hotel industry, third-party logistics services, heat treatment, hardware retail.
- **Magazine in a lift example:** in multi-story buildings, **mirrors** are installed in elevators to keep you engaged (fixing your shirt/hair) so the waiting period is forgotten — improving the experience.

### 22.10 Amazon Case Study
- Amazon (based in **Washington**) earlier **filled orders from distributors** (collect orders, route through distributor — time taking). It then **set up its own warehouses** inside the US and other countries, building up inventories so orders could ship faster, and added **courier partners**.
- Major step: **Kindle** — converted physical books into **digital books** (offering more than **4.6 lakh digital books**), so physical delivery is not required; added **audio and video content** to increase the experience; started **music** online.
- Then acquired **Zappos** (online shoe retailer) and **diapers.com**, diversifying into product categories — showing how product and services can be managed together (digitalizing physical products saves transportation cost, e.g., antivirus key vs. CD).

## Lecture 23 — Principle Streams of Supply Chain

### 23.1 The Three Streams
- **First stream** — suppliers to manufacturer (procurement/sourcing/supply).
- **Second stream** — within the manufacturing facility (operations/material/production).
- **Third stream** — manufacturer to end customer (distribution/warehousing/transportation).

### 23.2 First Stream — Sourcing Management
- **Sourcing is the first step** because it develops your stakeholders/supply chain (long-term suppliers), then **procurement** (repetitive purchase orders with sourced partners), then **supply** (ensuring smooth supply).
- **Steps:** identify, assess, and enlist suppliers; then ensure **cost-efficient acquisition** through **tendering procedures** (listing sources, floating tenders, terms related to pricing, delivery, quality).
- Sourcing is **strategic** — a long-term effect on competency: if you change partners frequently, today's partner becomes a competitor with your "recipe."
- **Types of sourcing:**
  - **Outsourcing** — e.g., packaging or printing (not your expertise). Sometimes the outsourced company **sets up a facility inside your plant** (you charge them for facilities).
  - **Backward integration** — acquire/start your own raw-material supply units (a tyre maker taking ore direct to make steel wire).
  - **Forward integration** — set up your own forward supply chain (e.g., **Dell** selling directly to the customer).
  - **In-house (insourcing)** — e.g., within a **Philips** home-appliance maker, plant 1 is the **molding** plant (plastic parts) and plant 2 assembles the products; plastic components come from within.
  - **Near sourcing** — for **JIT**: ask raw-material suppliers to set up near your facility (share large enough orders so both parties profit). **TVS example:** fragile glass/lights are made near the production house to avoid scratches in long-distance transport.
  - **Low cost country sourcing** — high-cost countries vs. cheap locations (**Bangladesh, Bhutan, Nepal**, even **Walmart** garment players); **China** not low-cost now but has low entry charges for foreign players.
  - **Global sourcing** — the whole world is one marketplace (access to new skills/resources; **China** best known for **lighting solutions** — even a single **LED**; earlier known for quantity, now quality).
  - **Prime subcontracting** — outsourcing part sub-assemblies (e.g., the **PCB** circuit board of a **washing machine** — a small but critical component).
  - **Captive services operation** — partially or wholly acquire; more control over quality/security but consider economies of scale.
  - **Professional services** — lawyer, specialists (you can't do it yourself; some seek permission to fight their own cases with limitations).
  - **Manufacturing** — e.g., **Philips** has no manufacturing unit but controls **co-manufacturers** who produce for it.
  - **Vertical integration** — backward (acquire suppliers) and forward (acquire distribution/transportation). **Horizontal integration** — acquiring a competitor (Philips acquiring **Havels**), but in the supply chain context vertical integration is our concern.
  - **Few or more suppliers** — a single supplier means large orders (negotiation power) but a disaster stops your whole line; too many suppliers spread quantity. The professor supports **2 or 3 suppliers** (feed large orders, negotiate cost/quality, and shift if one fails).
  - **Joint venture** — **Maruti Suzuki** (leverage Maruti's Indian supply chain and Suzuki's technology).
  - **Virtual enterprise** — online companies (e.g., **McKinsey** surveys/reports helpful when designing healthcare insurance products/facilities).

### 23.3 First Stream — Procurement Management
- **Definition:** the process responsible for overseeing and administering all activities involved in acquiring the material, products, goods, and services required to ensure smooth, efficient, effective operation within the production facility.
- **Fundamentals:**
  - **Purchasing portfolio** — what products, quantities, frequency, number/size of lots, expected quality (better negotiation; **Walmart** feeds large orders to limited suppliers).
  - **Organize purchases** — e.g., one month needs **three deliveries** (load 1, 2, 3) of defined sizes; when the PO is initiated, when it reaches the supplier, quantity and component variation.
  - **Control inventory** — finished/raw/semi-finished; avoid too much semi-finished (e.g., **BS3 petrol and BS4 diesel engines** completely phased out in **Delhi** caused a rush to sell two/four-wheelers at **50% less**).
  - **Include procurement in product development** — procurement knows which material is beneficial (performance, quality, quantity, price), how many suppliers exist, and whether JIT can be done.
  - **Risk management and performance indicators** — avoid stock-out; maintain a minimum safe inventory/safety stock so the production store always smoothly feeds the line.
  - **Identify customer needs** — through R&D; think out of the box beyond competitors.
  - **Manage and prioritize vendor relationships** for long-term relationships.
  - **Collect spend data** — spending analysis software/apps (data from Paytm/UPI/PayPal) shows where you spend most (grocery, travel, fuel, clothing); in industry, if you repeatedly feed large orders to one player, use that **negotiation power**.
- **Steps of procurement:** identify the source and plan how much to purchase → choose/select suppliers (e.g., **60% to one, 20%+20%** to others; **vendor rating** decides) → **negotiate the contract** (quantity, quality, payment terms — usually **90 days** after receipt to check incoming quality, inspect, invoice, pass through accounts, then pay) → **place the first order** (documenting every term/condition) → **accelerate the order** (a second supplier should accelerate if one fails; if forecasting failed, don't stop) → **receipt and inspection** (if OK, store and move to the production line) → **invoice and payment** (on time to manage relationships).
- **Construction company example:** supplies needed are cement, steel, electrical wiring, electrical equipment; smooth supply ensures on-time project completion. Price is not the only criterion (the tender with the minimum quote may not be best) — **project delay** is a major concern.
- **Procurement vs. Sourcing:** sourcing is the first stage of **developing the supply chain** (focus: **who** makes the supplies); procurement involves procuring goods/services from already-running supply chains (focus: **what** supplies), ensuring a steady flow of inputs.

### 23.4 First Stream — Supply Management
- Ensures the **smooth, continuous supply** of goods/products. Requires **proper integration with suppliers** (technological) so information is quickly shared (a demand rise or anticipated demand drop is communicated; the supplier should not overproduce).
- It is a function of **pre-production logistics and inventory management**; targets "**should not stock out** and **should not have excess stock**."
- **Role:** identifying, negotiating, and procuring a service/good essential to ongoing operations; from the listed sources pick the best supplier, but **retain a few suppliers** (2/3/4) — "few" is subjective (tyres from **MRF** or **Bridgestone**; **fuel pump/filter** from 2–3 suppliers like **Bosch**).
- Formulate a **strategy for developing and maintaining relationships**; use technology/procedures to integrate better; consider the **theories of supply and demand** to fix price and ensure smooth supply and demand generation.

## Lecture 24 — Principle Streams of Supply Chain (Contd.)

### 24.1 Second Stream — Operation Management vs. Production Management
- **Production management** — only converting raw material into finished goods (all activities required to finish the good).
- **Operation management** — can happen anywhere (HR processes, customer relationship management, packaging, distribution); defined as a **sub-part of the supply chain** where many functional units perform operations. It is an area of business management fundamental to running a successful enterprise.

### 24.2 Responsibilities of Business Operation Management
- **Design the process** — stages to convert raw material (5–10 stages for a simple product; up to **100–1000 stages** for an automobile/home appliance including final packaging). Also process design within quality (IQC incoming check sheets, in-process critical-stage parameters) and HR (joining → induction → placement).
- **Balance resources, revenues, and cost** — customer expectations → gather resources → acquire resources (cost) → sell to the market → generate revenue.
- **Continuously evolve product/services design** — e.g., **Philips** advertises "**innovation and you**" (better promises, product features, process design, longevity).
- **Overall operational strategy** — expansion/new production line; aligning operation strategy across two organizations when acquiring another unit.
- **Streamline and optimize production** — convert raw material with minimum cost (improved productivity/efficiency, reduced waste).

### 24.3 Processes and Functional Units
- Processes: production, quality, storing raw material, packaging, designing, HRM — each with a set of activities (quality, maintenance, skills, capacity).
- **Capacity** — growth strategy means more markets/products → enhance capacity; if demand is roaring, go for **double/triple shifts**; ensure capacity utilization (warehouse utilization, full container utilization).
- Activities are linked: if **IQC** doesn't ensure raw-material quality, production can't be ensured; if **HRM** doesn't hire/place, who ensures quality?
- **Process of operation management:** **planning** (what/when — information comes from the market/customer, competitors, other industries → processed within R&D/in-house unit → new product/renovation → new raw materials/components → share with suppliers), **scheduling** (when to perform each task), **execution** (consumes resources and carries maximum cost).
- **5 M's (basic resources):** **man, money, machine, material, methods** (plus technology, skills, manpower).

### 24.4 Types of Operations
- **Manufacturing** — design prototypes → create → commercialize → deliver; needs the 5 M's plus capacity, production floor, quality tools, documentation.
- **Services** — customer is part of the delivery process; heterogeneous (every service unique). Examples: retail (arranging P&G inventory on the shelf, replenishing, locating the store near the customer), **hospitality** (front desk, housekeeping, **5S** Japanese approach: **Seiri, Seiton, Seiso, Seiketsu, Shitsuke**), logistics (no value-adding — just dropping).
- **Functional units under the complete SC:** raw-material acquisition, placing/executing vendor orders, inventory management, storage house, manufacturing/production floor, distribution.

### 24.5 Strategy of Operation Management
- **Reduce cost** — the first three (quality, productivity, cost) are interrelated: less rejection → high process capability → less rejection → higher productivity → lower cost.
  - **Rejection-rate example:** if rejection falls from **5% to 2%**, you deliver **97** products instead of 95 from every 100; productivity improves and cost falls.
- **Optimally utilize data** — collect market survey/trends data → figure the demand function → acquire raw material → convert → move to market.
- **Set goals and forecasting.**
- **Enhance collaboration** — for efficiency, minimum cost is the USP; don't add extra packaging wrongly (but don't destroy the product). Example: BMW/Mercedes/F1 cars — an efficient-but-low-pickup engine would defeat their purpose; the **baseline/common thread** of the operation strategy must be shared.
- **Human resource management** — a long-term asset; retaining resources improves the overall supply chain and internal collaboration.

### 24.6 Material Management
- Ensures smooth supply of the material used in the manufacturing unit while maintaining **optimum stock**. There must be a **safety stock level** — when inventory reaches it, the purchase/store department places a PO to replenish; it should never touch **zero** or the whole production line sits idle.
- **Trade-off:** time, cost, quality — how much quantity to buy. You feed only **100 units** to your supplier; **Walmart** feeds **10,000 units** (inventory cost but huge negotiation power on cost, quality, and timing).
- **Main objectives:** lower acquisition price (Walmart negotiating), high inventory turnover (frequent conversion to sales), continuous supply of material, consistent quality, favorable supplier relationships.
- **Components:** make-or-buy decisions, product improvement, forecast, **MRP** (ERP software for quantity and timing of raw material), purchase planning.
- **Ordering cost vs. holding cost** → **Economic Order Quantity (EOQ)**: holding cost increases with quantity, ordering cost decreases; EOQ is where total cost is minimum (holding and ordering cost at equilibrium).
- **Inventory control:** three types of inventory; material supply management; timely delivery of quality material; **quality control** (incoming quality control must ensure defect-free raw material).
- **Significance:** cost reduction (minimize waste, avoid extra inventory, still ensure smooth supply), improved quality (cost–quality–productivity linked), timely delivery, variety of raw material, inventory optimization (JIT).
- **Best practices:** comprehensive material management plan shared across the network; inventory management best practices; strong relationships (pay on time, help groom the vendor's product basket); continuously monitor/analyze data; **ERP** software.
- **Challenges:** complex supply chain (e.g., **automobile** — glass, rubber, tyres, engines, metal, leather, wood; **more than 1000 vendors**; with 500–1000 components and 3 vendors each → **~3000 vendors**); cost control (negotiation depends on purchase volume); quality control (provide specifications to suppliers).
- **Profitability improvement:** reduce cost, logistics cost, inventory carrying cost, waste cost, manufacturing cost; **reduce working capital** (avoid idle inventory lying in stock).

### 24.7 Production Management
- The **production floor** converts/transforms raw material into finished goods using the 5/6 M's; the **market** is added as the 6th M because it provides information on when/how much to produce and what quality/features to add.
- **Types of manufacturing processes:**
  - **Continuous** — steel, sugar, salt (sure of demand and supply, day and night, no variation).
  - **Semi-continuous** — changeovers in between (e.g., **Maruti Suzuki** — petrol 10 variants + diesel 10 variants + 5 colors → 100+ models on 3 production lines with changes in between).
  - **Intermittent** — **job shop** (restaurant — different orders/recipes/equipment) or **batch** (pharmaceutical — ingredients change but machinery/recipe steps stay the same).
  - **Project** — one product, not in quantity (road construction, real estate, ship building).
- **Objectives:** right quantity of goods/services to meet demand; maintain the right quality standard throughout; deliver at the right time as per customer expectation/deadlines; ensure overall profitability.
- **Functions of the production manager:** product and resource selection; **PPC (production planning and control)** for schedules; **machine maintenance and replacement** (quick response to breakdowns that would shut the line).
- **Strategies:** continuous improvement, **JIT**, **total productive maintenance (TPM)**, supply chain management.
- **Furniture manufacturer example:** type of furniture, sources required, checked with market demand, meeting standards, quality/durability.

### 24.8 Third Stream (Quick Review)
- Logistics management components: **transportation, warehousing, inventory management, fulfillment and distribution**.
- **Objective:** right product, right time, right quantity, right quality, right customer, at right location.
- **Benefits:** high profitability, productivity/efficiency, enhanced customer experience; inventory planning reduces warehousing cost; transportation planning and optimal routes reduce transportation cost.
- **Components:** warehouse management (picking, packing, shipping; **barcode scanning, RFID, automated warehouses with robots**), transportation management software (best route by road condition and distance), **distribution management** (recording orders, sale analysis, stock management, order fulfillment, price circulation and control).
- **Challenges:** shortage of skilled manpower (to handle automated warehouses); inadequate capital / hesitancy to invest in new technology.
- Interdependence: if the first stream ensures smooth functioning, the second stream processes on time with quality, then the customer experience can be enhanced through the distribution network.

## Lecture 25 — Objectives of Supply Chain Networks

### 25.1 Objectives of Supply Chain Management
- Information comes from the **customer end** (delivery expectations — when, quantity, quality, variety, overall delivery experience, return policies, features) → processed → physical prototype designed → vendors tapped for raw material.
- **Sources of cost and revenue:** revenue comes only from the **end customer**; customer expectations define the cost function (features, quality, variety).
- **Maximize value generated** — the difference between the **cost paid by the customer** and the **experience incurred**. The customer evaluates not only the product but the **delivery service** (a misbehaving delivery person, being asked to come downstairs, etc.).
- **Improvement of cost of quality** — **quality is free** means the cost of ensuring quality is lower than the cost of not ensuring it (appraisal cost; warranty/guarantee fixes cost more).
- **Quick replenishment** — not only raw-material inventory from the supplier but also how quickly the retail shop can replenish stock; possible only with a responsive supply chain.
- **Speedy delivery / shorten time to order** — regular items (next-day is fine) vs. emergencies (medicine in half an hour/one hour → **emergency supply chain**).
- **Efficiency and efficacy** — efficacy = how effectively you produce; efficiency = minimum cost of delivering.
- **Delivery optimization** — consolidate orders from multiple customers (e.g., promising **30-minute pizza** delivery, consolidate 2–3 nearby orders); find the best route and utilize proper container capacity.
- **Awareness** of supply chain efficiency and dynamics (how things operate outside).
- **Inventory optimization** — balance inflow/outflow of inventory (raw material and in the distribution chain) to balance demand and supply. *Inventory exists because there is a gap between supply and demand*; minimize the gap with an integrated information system.
- **Flexibility** — how quickly you adopt changes (process, product quality, new features) coming from the market.
- **Business characteristics** — macro (large-quantity shipments → efficiency/effectiveness) vs. micro (few-minutes delivery → emergency supply chain).
- **Cost reduction** — proper utilization in warehousing, raw-material supply, inventory, transportation.
- **Better decision making** — with timely/accurate information (growth strategy → expand network, capacity).
- **Better distribution** — channel inventory to the customer using market information.

### 25.2 The Supply Chain Network
- **Interconnection of organizations:** raw-material suppliers, transportation agencies, manufacturing/co-manufacturing units, distribution partners, customer.
- **Activities:** raw-material production (converting raw material into finished), shipping, storage, distribution, warehousing, transportation, last-mile delivery.
- **Resources:** **goods, information, and finance** flow through the distribution network.
- **Key components:** fix the number of each stakeholder (distributors, transportation agencies, warehouses/third-party warehousing, raw-material vendors).
- **Complexity:** simple (steel — ore from one supplier, steel rods to the end retailer) vs. complex/critical (automobile; **hospital** — tests, medicines, equipment, emergency services, shifting a patient to another hospital needing ICU/blood group/medicine/doctor expertise).
- **Scale:** fixed quantity (steel rods → precise stock management) vs. volatile (hospital ambulances — 5 hours no demand then 5 ambulances needed in 5 minutes).
- **Designing any network:** optimize processes, enhance collaboration, leverage technology, improve visibility (hospital patient movement from lab to ICU), minimize cost, reduce lead time, mitigate risk, enhance customer satisfaction.

### 25.3 Network Design Decisions
- **Facility role** — e.g., warehouse storing finished goods near the customer (immediate fulfillment but higher facility cost in the city center) vs. away from the city (cheaper).
- **Facility location and capacity allocation** — current and future expansion feasibility.
- **Market allocation** — factory 1 supplies 3 markets, factory 2 supplies 2 markets, etc., considering distance/transportation cost and the ability to shift to an alternate supply center if one breaks down.

### 25.4 Factors Influencing Network Design
- **Strategic factors** — long-term investment; once you set up a warehouse/vendor location you cannot change frequently.
  - **Cost leadership example:** opening a retail showroom inside the heart of the city is not cost-efficient; 2 km away gives cheaper location and operational expenses.
  - **Foxconn/Flextronics** located maximum facilities in low-cost **China** (but response time higher, away from market); **Zara** sets up manufacturing/stores in high-cost **Portugal/Spain** to be near customers and engage them quickly.
  - **Cement example:** for a standard product, where the retail shop is located (costly or cheap) hardly matters as long as cost is minimized.
- **Technological factors** — **semiconductor** manufacturing needs very high initial investment → few, very-high-capacity facilities (but higher transportation cost); **Coca-Cola bottling plant** needs low initial investment → set up small bottling plants near markets to minimize transportation distance.
- **Macroeconomic factors** — taxes, tariffs, exchange rates, shipping cost; **Special Economic Zones** (rebates); India simplified its tax structure to invite foreign investment.
- **Infrastructural factors** — road connectivity, electricity, water, airport, train (cheap for heavy logistics); **Shanghai** is costly but many players still set up there for infrastructure.
- **Political factors** — stable government promotes investment; computed via the **global political risk index** (India ranked **30th**, behind factors like natural disasters and frequent agitations).
- **Competitive factors** — clusters (furniture market, goldsmith street) give easy raw-material access and customer awareness vs. **organized one-stop malls** (buy 1–2 months of grocery and clothing in one visit).
- **Customer response time and local presence** — convenience stores (quick access to milk products) vs. supermarkets/malls 5–10 km away.
- **Logistics and facility cost** — city-center transport is difficult (timing restrictions); **transportation cost decreases as the number of facilities increases**:
  - Reason 1: full container capacity (10 → 100 → 1000 units) lowers cost until the container is full, then a second container shoots cost up.
  - Reason 2: a facility near the end market reduces the **outbound** distance (inbound logistics cost < outbound logistics cost).

### 25.5 Framework of Network Design (Three Phases)
1. **Supply chain strategy** — per global competition (growth/expansion, stability, acquisition); align the supply chain strategy with the competitive strategy (fighting **Walmart** → cost efficient → wait for economies of scale, consolidate orders, don't run a vehicle for one order).
2. **Regional facility configuration** — regional factors: tax structure, regional demand, political scenario, exchange rate, demand risk, cost factors, competitive environment, production technology.
3. **Desirable sites** — pick 4–5 candidate locations (e.g., in Calcutta), evaluate each with cost, technology, local demand, political stability, exchange rate, and infrastructure parameters; pick the best (e.g., third location).

### 25.6 Zara Case Study
- **Spanish largest apparel manufacturer** — more than **4700 retail outlets** in **76 countries**; deals with inconsistent, uncertain demand yet claims to be **highly responsive**.
- Apparel industry **design-to-sales cycle** is normally **6 months**; Zara reduced it to **4–6 weeks**. Its strategy: **renew store inventory as soon as possible** — **75% of the merchandise display** changes every **3–4 weeks** (new inventory, new designs).
- Because the cycle is so short, Zara is **never out of season/fashion**, matching changing customer preferences, so it sells all products at **full price** (avoiding competitors' 50–75% seasonal sales).
- It produces only **40% of finished goods** initially (not too much, to avoid extra inventory and seasonal sales).
- **In-house + outsourced manufacturing (unique strategy):** **40% of manufacturing is owned by Inditex** (which owns the Zara brand), producing in costly **European** locations; the rest is produced in **cheaper Asian** locations. When demand is certain, outsource to Asia; when trends are uncertain, manufacture in-house to control new products and come up with new designs frequently.
- **Responsiveness and postponement** — postponing the manufacturing decision avoids piling up big inventories; accurate forecasting reduces the need for seasonal sales. Zara spends heavily on its **information network** to closely monitor customers.
- **Delivery time:** within **Europe 24 hours**; **48 hours** for stores in **America and Asia**.
- **Case questions:** What advantage does Zara gain by a responsive supply chain? (apparel is a trend industry — no one wants to wear the same shirt as someone else). Why has Inditex chosen both in-house and outsourced manufacturing? (control new designs in-house, cheap Asian locations for certain demand). Why does Zara source uncertain-demand products from **local** manufacturers? (near the market — any fluctuation can be amended; if far away you keep producing and must go for seasonal offers). What advantage from replenishing stores multiple times? (no high inventories → frequent shipments keep inventory updated). Is Zara's responsive replenishment infrastructure better for **online or offline**? (the professor's opinion: online allows more promises and easier consolidation of segregated orders; offline takes more effort/time to collect and synchronize).

## Lecture 26 — Supply Chain Uncertainty and Processes

### 26.1 Introduction — Uncertainty
- The supply chain manager faces uncertainties from the **customer end** (demand) or the **supply end**.
- Examples of not being 100% sure: the **exact time** of delivery (though location can be specified), the **quality** of goods after transport/handling, and disruptions like the **COVID-19 pandemic** (shutdown, factories closed, workers moved to villages).
- The primary fight in SCM is to **meet demand with supply** when either is uncertain.
- First solution in mind: keep extra **safe inventory** at the retail outlet — but how much? If you predict a cyclone tomorrow but there is none for 10 months, the inventory may become **obsolete** and be sold at reduced end-season prices.

### 26.2 Four Scenarios (Supply Uncertainty × Demand Uncertainty)
| Supply \ Demand | Low demand uncertainty | High demand uncertainty |
|---|---|---|
| **Low supply uncertainty** | **Efficient** supply chain — continuous supply/demand products (**sugar, salt, steel, cement**); compete on **pricing** | **Responsive** supply chain — e.g., **new mobile phones** (same components, new features/apps); respond quickly to demand from anywhere |
| **High supply uncertainty** | **Risk hedging** — e.g., **semiconductors** (automobile demand saturated but **chip shortage** left 50 lakh units unsold with Maruti/Hyundai/Toyota); pool resources across players | **Agile** supply chain — biggest challenge (e.g., **healthcare** emergency, **disaster relief** — locate affected people, ensure minimum supplies for 8–10 days, then rescue) |

### 26.3 Demand Uncertainty
- **Demand uncertainty** = unpredictability/variability from the customer side.
- Reasons: **market trends** (customer focus shifts to alternatives), **consumer behavior** (stocking groceries for 2–3 months vs. the usual 8–15 days), **economic conditions** (recession reduces purchasing power — COVID hit automobile demand), **external events**.
- **Implied demand uncertainty** differs from demand uncertainty — it refers to the **special promise/emergency** portion of demand:
  - **Taxi vs. ambulance:** a taxi may have no ride all day then many in 12 hours (manage/hedge by pooling resources with other players); an **ambulance** is the highest uncertainty — you call it and cannot wait even 5 minutes (unlike waiting 30 minutes for a taxi).
  - In implied demand uncertainty, **order frequency is not sure**, **lead time is very short**, and **service levels expected vary**.
  - **Emergency orders** vs. **long lead time:** a **Zomato** order can be 30–60 minutes (bad weather delays acceptable), but emergency/disaster management must be very quick.
  - As **service level increases, implied demand uncertainty increases** (like anything). Selling through **many channels** (airlines selling through own website, MakeMyTrip, Goibibo, ixigo, IRCTC) also increases implied demand uncertainty.

### 26.4 Supply Uncertainty
- Factors: **capability of the supply chain** and **life cycle position**.
- **Capability:** production process, **yield quality** (how quickly the process responds to change — shifting model 1 → model 2), quality (efficient conversion; **standardization** early reduces rejection). Standardize the process as early as possible.
- **Life cycle position** (introduction → growth → maturity → decline):
  - **Introduction:** very uncertain demand **and** supply (still standardizing raw materials/process; unsure how many vendors to develop) → must be **responsive** (cost higher, low productivity, higher pricing to recover R&D cost).
  - **Growth:** competitors enter → lower cost/price; recover R&D cost plus margin by now.
  - **Maturity:** processes standardized, demand/supply saturated (15–17% customer share, ±1–2% fluctuation) → be **cost efficient** and promise quality.
  - **Implied demand uncertainty** also ripples into supply (e.g., three equally important ambulance calls at once — whom to address first).

### 26.5 Mitigating Supply Chain Uncertainty
- **Diversify suppliers** — multiple suppliers in different geographic locations (a disaster in one region interrupts supply); keep one or two **local vendors** alongside **overseas** suppliers (cheaper cost/quality/technology but long supply chain).
- **Enhance visibility** — real-time tracking of inventory and activities at each stage; uncertainty exists because we deal with **partial, vague, uncertain information**.
- **Build flexibility** — store inventory and use **agile manufacturing practices**.
- **Foster collaboration** — suppliers must be ready to supply 5 then 50 units and change over to a second model (communication, trust, collaboration throughout the network).
- **Implement risk management practices** — assess risk, **scenario planning** (routes a/b/c/d, outcomes, probabilities).
- **Invest in resilience** — backup inventory, backup location, backup manufacturing.
- **Monitor and adapt** — regularly monitor performance and external factors.
- **Conclusion:** improve visibility and communication across the supply chain (shared data) → leads to **agility** (proactive response to uncertainty/risk).

### 26.6 Uncertainty vs. Risk
- **Risk** can be **estimated** (route A → 15% risk, route B → 25% risk; if risk is 25% maybe returns are 35%, if 15% maybe returns 20%).
- **Uncertainty** — the probability cannot be found out (e.g., depth/interruption of a disaster; cyclone prediction up to some extent but how much it disrupts the supply chain is another story).

### 26.7 Types of Uncertainty and Risk
- **Logistics-related** — physical flow interruption (delays, transport network, storage issues, insufficient inventory, carrier strength, flight operations) affecting efficiency/effectiveness.
- **Information-related** — lack in information delivery disturbs the whole network; without a proper demand function for the next 15 days, manufacturing/procurement/inventory planning fails; needs information **accuracy, availability, timely processing**.
- **Customer-related** — footfall, conversion (50–60%), unpredictable consumer behavior, **reputation risk** (negative brand image), **receivable risk** (credit), forecasting errors, demand delays (customers roaming without buying, or buying unexpectedly).
- **Environment-related** — political, economic, social, technological, environmental, global factors (e.g., **Russia-Ukraine war** → crude oil prices up → transportation hit → shipping cost up; change of government → market sentiment).

### 26.8 Mitigating Measures (Demand/Info Uncertainty)
- **Regular plan generation** — plans are not permanent; generate frequent plans, re-evaluate with the changing market, **automate** warehouses/inventory/stock/manufacturing (if done, **inventory cost can reduce by 37%**).
- **Consistency in planning** — stay true to your strategy (if you claim to be responsive, don't suddenly compete on cost — **Rolex** suddenly advertising cheap watches would lose belief).
- **Integration of additional data sources** — beyond marketing/distribution/sales channels, track patterns via **social media** ratings/reviews; align resources with demand and change production plans.
- **Enhanced visibility across the supply chain** — combat the **bullwhip effect** where information gets amplified over stages:
  - Customer places **50** → retailer places **75** with manufacturer → raw-material supplier gets **110** components.
  - Reduce this with **Electronic Data Interchange (EDI)** to share exact information (if demand is 50, order material for 50).
- **Holistic view and performance-tracking KPIs** aligned across all stakeholders (not just one manufacturer/distributor/warehouse); adopt a **system approach**.

### 26.9 Supply Chain Macro Processes
1. **Supplier Relationship Management (SRM)** — sourcing/negotiating suppliers on quantity/quality/price; buying; **design collaboration** (sharing your "recipe" so they manufacture components); supply.
2. **Internal Supply Chain Management (ISCM)** — raw material → finished goods within the manufacturing unit; strategic planning, demand planning (how many units of B/D today, when to shift from A to B), internal order fulfillment.
3. **Customer Relationship Management (CRM)** — with the final customer: market, identify demand/expectations, design the product/process, pricing (what the customer is willing to pay), selling, after-sales services (installation, returns/damages, spare parts over 20 years).
- If any macro process is misaligned (wrong market demand → wrong strategic planning → wrong component), the whole chain suffers — align all three macro processes.

## Lecture 27 — Supply Chain Operational Views

### 27.1 SCOR Model (Supply Chain Council)
- The **SCOR model** (given by the Supply Chain Council) is the basic building block to build distribution systems quickly and with performance; it includes major processes across all stakeholders.
- **Five major processes:** **Plan, Source, Make, Deliver, Return.**
  - **Plan** — planning, scheduling, and coordinating resources to meet customer demand efficiently (demand size → plan → schedule process timing).
  - **Source** — sourcing supplies/materials, negotiating **quality, quantity, price** (and delivery — orders, lot size).
  - **Make** — manufacturing, assembly, or producing the product (raw material → finished; finished may be raw material for the next organization).
  - **Deliver** — the distribution network: recording customer orders, processing through warehouses, keeping inventory, ensuring final delivery.
  - **Return** — managing products the customer did not like (resell quickly, repair, recycle, or dispose of end-used products).
- The five steps apply at **suppliers, manufacturer, distribution network, and customer** (a supplier may use only Deliver and Return; a second-stage supplier does value-adding, plans its supply replicated from the manufacturer's plan, sources, makes, delivers).

### 27.2 SCOR Elements and Performance
- **Four elements/pieces:** **Performance, Process, Practices, People.**
  - **Performance** — reliability (deliver correct quality/quantity to the market), responsiveness/agility with minimum cost, efficient asset management (full warehouse/container capacity, high manufacturing productivity).
  - **Process** — the value-adding activities; standardize plan/source/make (sourcing done once, then repetitive purchasing); methods/techniques/tools to optimize and reduce cost.
  - **Practices** — optimize activities and improve service levels (minimum delivery time).
  - **People** — collaborate as a team across suppliers, manufacturers, co-manufacturers, distribution chain, and consumers; upgrade the technology/skills of raw-material suppliers (your responsibility) so they provide quick, flexible, maximum-quality supplies at lower cost.
- **SCOR performance matrix (four categories):**
  1. **Supply chain reliability** — products delivered in correct quantity and right condition (quality not degraded during shifting/handling/storage).
  2. **Responsiveness** — speed of responding to a customer query (not only delivering with speed but service with speed; if installation fails, how quickly the best engineer visits and addresses queries).
  3. **Supply chain cost** — minimized by maintaining efficiency across the network from start to end.
  4. **Asset management** — managing **working capital** (minimize inventory) and fixed assets (warehouse capacity, extra resources).
- **Advantages:** applicable through the entire supply chain; use measures for **benchmarking** practices; develop metrics and align activities; common repository/standardized process (repetitive: plan, source, make, deliver, return daily).

### 27.3 Limitations of SCOR
- **Cannot be applied to the services industry** — **make, deliver, and return** are not possible for services (an airline/hotel can't be returned after consumption; a pen under warranty can); making and delivering cannot be two stages (services are made and delivered at the same time — barber head massage; customer is part of the chain); **sourcing** half-services before reaching the customer is impossible.
- **Complexity** — applying all five stages for all players is difficult to align; supply chains are already complex (many retailers, online platforms — **Amazon, eBay, Flipkart, Walmart**, local shops, one-stop markets, own websites).
- **One-size-fits-all difficult** — requirements differ by product; not all stages apply to all markets.
- **Lack of flexibility** — fixed around five areas; unexpected disasters are not taken care of.
- **Limited scope** — services industry and **sustainability/social responsibility** are not addressed.
- **Information accuracy** — timely, processed, accurate data is required; the source and accuracy of information is a big challenge.

### 27.4 SCOR in E-commerce
- There is no concept of a local market now — the **digital supply chain** on online retail platforms (you may never meet your customer).
- Implement SCOR to integrate suppliers, customers, and contract partners; when information is shared quickly and all stakeholders decide on the same information, they are more aligned; data is easily analyzed electronically (timely, processed, accurate data is the basic requirement).
- **Challenges:** ensuring fast delivery, low cost, easy payment methods, efficient customer support, after-sale services, and payment-method integration into SCOR.

### 27.5 Process and Cyclic View
- The supply chain is viewed as a **series of cycles**, each between the interaction of two stakeholders:
  1. **Customer order cycle** — between **customer and retailer**.
  2. **Replenishment cycle** — between **retailer and distributor**.
  3. **Manufacturing cycle** — between **distributor and manufacturer**.
  4. **Procurement cycle** — between **manufacturer and raw-material supplier**.
- **Series of events (any cycle):** marketer markets the product → buyer places the order → marketer records the order → shipping of the order → buyer receives the order → return the order if not ok.
- **Members:** customer, retailer, distributor, manufacturer, supplier.
  - **Customer** — approaches the retail outlet (online app, organized/unorganized retail); gives preferences; retailer records and supplies the order; finally **change of ownership and entitlement** (retailer → customer).
  - **Retailer** — purchases from the distributor; manages the flow of goods/services (movement and storage of raw material/WIP/finished goods); processes/assembles and hands over to the customer.
  - **Distributor** — aggregates demand from local markets and communicates with the manufacturer; services distribution relates to where service centers are located; key activities: logistics, transportation, warehousing, storage, inventory management.
  - **Manufacturer** — sources raw material from suppliers and converts it into the final product; the manufacturing process begins with the creation of the material from which the design is made.
  - **Supplier** — the first extreme end; designs its distribution network and manufactures to supply raw material to the manufacturer.

### 27.6 The Four Cycles — Events
- **Customer order cycle:**
  1. **Customer arrival** — the goal is to **convert the customer arrival into an order** (setting up the retail center).
  2. **Customer order entry** — record the order (goal: quick entry); communicate to the rest of the supply chain so manufacturers/raw-material suppliers get an intimation of how quickly the product is being consumed.
  3. **Customer order fulfillment** — the order is filled and sent; inventory is replenished from the distributor.
  4. **Customer order receiving** — the customer receives the order, receives ownership, receipt records updated, and payment is completed.
- **Replenishment cycle** (retailer–distributor):
  1. **Retail order triggered** — customer purchase triggers the retailer to place an order with the distributor.
  2. **Retail order entry** — orders from different retail shops combined/recorded; record how quickly inventory is depleting.
  3. **Order fulfillment (retail)** — distributor fulfills orders from (say) **50 retail shops**.
  4. **Retail order receiving** — all 50 retailers receive the order.
- **Manufacturing cycle** (distributor–manufacturer):
  1. Order arrives from the distributor.
  2. **Production scheduling** — schedule different models/products/varieties (how much of each; based on the order, place orders with vendors).
  3. **Manufacturing and shipping** — production done, then packaging and shipping to the distributor/warehouses.
  4. **Receiving** — inventory consumed is replenished.
- **Procurement cycle** (manufacturer–raw-material supplier):
  1. Order based on the production schedule reaches the supplier.
  2. Supplier schedules its own production with its raw material and provides it.
  3. Manufacturing/packaging, then shipping the raw material to the manufacturer.
  4. Manufacturing unit replenishes its inventory/updated stocks to meet future demand.

### 27.7 Push-Pull View
- **Pull view** — reacting to a customer order (customer visits, places the order, then you prepare/deliver); a **reactive process** (production starts after the customer order arrives).
- **Push view** — keep producing and depleting inventory (based on speculation/forecasting that this will be the demand).
- **Setting the push-pull boundary is very important.**
  - **Grocery retail shop** — the three upstream cycles are already done (procured, manufactured, replenished); the **customer order cycle** is filled at the boundary → boundary set at the customer order cycle.
  - **Building a house** — the complete supply chain is **pull**: you cannot purchase sand, steel, bricks, wood, electrical appliances, cement until the contract/tender is finalized; then procure, build step-by-step, install, and replenish as resources deplete.
  - **Dell** — push up to the manufacturer (all raw material components/procurement already in inventory), pull from manufacturing onward (record the customer's configuration requirement, assemble within 2–3 days, replenish with the retailer) — extends the pull supply chain up to the manufacturer.
- **Why relax the boundary:** a pure pull supply chain is very difficult (uncertain when demand comes); including some stages under push maintains some inventory so orders can be processed immediately.
- There is no unique approach — depending on analysis, decide how far to go with push vs. pull.
- **Conclusion:** SCOR is the basic model for product manufacturing (plan/source/make/deliver/return) including all stakeholders; planning at the distribution center cannot be done in isolation; several cycles happen between players; the push-pull boundary must be set carefully (some reactive supply chains are completely pull, like construction).

## Lecture 28 — Supply Chain Models and Modelling Systems

### 28.1 Introduction to Supply Chain Modeling
- **Supply chain modeling** — designing and analyzing a supply chain to identify potential improvements; a kind of **design of experiment** approach (like learning to cook — add an ingredient, taste, adjust).
- **Offline model (static)** — takes care of the present scenario, records current data, and predicts the output.
- **Online/dynamic model** — continuously feeds data; reflects changes in the environment (Monte Carlo simulation — different scenarios and the probability of each output).
- **Local vs. global** supply chains involve many stakeholders, processes, products (raw material, finished good, packaging after return, end-of-life); organizations: raw-material suppliers, manufacturers, co-manufacturers, distributors, warehousing partners, end-mile delivery partners.
- **Consequence of miscalculation:** more risk, uncertainty, interruptions to smooth supplies, and higher cost (not standardizing the process → extra cost).
- **Two ends of the continuum:** **cost efficient** vs. **responsive**; most organizations find a mixture depending on the product (a cost-efficient supply chain for one product, an emergency/healthcare supply chain kept toward the responsive side).

### 28.2 Eight Components to Model
1. **Procurement** — multiple vs. single vendors; how many components outsourced; lots per month; **lot size** (large lots give negotiation power — **Walmart** feeds very large orders; uniform batches let suppliers avoid quick changeovers).
2. **Manufacturing** — locations (multiple/centralized); initial investment cost; number of production lines (changeovers; running a line 10 hours continuously affects productivity; setup and rejection during changeover).
3. **Warehouse** — near the customer (reduce outbound logistics distance, quick response) vs. centralized facility (minimize inventory cost but higher transportation cost; can't fully use economies of scale/container capacity).
4. **Transportation** — compare **inbound vs. outbound** logistics cost; **outbound is always higher** → keep the warehouse near the end market (furniture from Amazon/Flipkart is disassembled and reassembled; a container carries **8–10 cars** vs. much more raw material, so locate the factory near the end market).
5. **Transportation modes** — road, rail, air, ship; ship is least cost (and sustainable), road slower, air fastest but costlier; depends on infrastructure availability.
6. **Inventory** — raw material, semi-finished, finished goods, and **rejected** (hoping to repair/resell in the secondary market or recycle/remanufacture); near-customer warehouses → multiple warehouses → higher inventory cost; online platforms moving to a **zero-inventory model** (channel the product directly from seller to buyer).
7. **Information technology** — quick, relevant information; transparency (where material is, quantity, units damaged, consumed in production/end market); the type of system ensures quick information sharing.
8. **Customer service** — expectations of quality, quantity, frequency, variety, innovation, delivery (doorstep vs. pickup vs. feel-and-purchase), returns, and end-of-life recycling/reuse.
- **Distribution** — last-mile delivery and consolidating multiple orders (Amazon sometimes makes multiple doorstep visits instead of consolidating; but some products are needed early, like breakfast grocery items).

### 28.3 Significance and Types of Models
- **Significance:** minimize cost while maximizing service; efficiently ensure the flow of **material, information, and finance** (planning and controlling the flow of these resources).
- After developing the matrix for the eight components, put in constraints (capacity limitations, customer requirements, supply chain capability) and model.
- **Experimentation/scenario analysis** — simulate different scenarios and trade off cost efficiency vs. responsiveness.
- **Model types:** **linear programming** (linear relationship between independent variables), **simulation**, **queuing model** (queue length, idle waiting time, processing time), **statistical models** (stochastic, non-probabilistic/deterministic), and operation research models — **transportation, assignment, simplex method, CPM/PERT**.
- **Select the right model** depending on requirements.

### 28.4 Supply Chain Model Types
- **Continuous model** — continuous flow of product, information, and finance; used with fixed demand/supply and predictable forecasting (commodities, grocery, FMCG, **steel, sugar, salt** — demand fixed by population). Objective: **efficiency**; characteristics: **JIT**, one-piece flow, reduced inventory/inventory cost, waste eliminated. Disadvantage: **less flexible** (an unexpected major disruption interrupts supply/demand). **Amazon** focuses on continuous sourcing at minimum cost; standard shipping (fulfillment center far) vs. **same-day delivery** (fulfillment center near → adds responsiveness, higher inventory cost but lower outbound logistics; combine with **crowd-sourcing** local courier partners).
- **Fast model** — speed is the main parameter; quick delivery/timely response; **Zara** is the best example (industry changes trends every 6–7 weeks but Zara every other week); best for products with a **very limited life cycle** (entertainment — a movie/web series must be distributed everywhere at launch; **seasonal products** for 4–5 months).
- **Efficient supply chain model** — extends the continuous flow model; minimize cost; priority on **efficient inventory management** and getting the most out of workers/equipment (minimum inputs, maximum output). Challenges: raw material/commodity costs, capacity constraints (24×7 running can still cap at **50 lakh units**), labor shortage/delays, highly competitive sectors.
  - **Zara sourcing by lead time:** short lead time → source locally (**Spain, Portugal, Morocco**); high-fashion items → ship immediately; reasonable lead time → nearby offshoring; higher lead time → low-cost country (**China, India**); ample time/standardized items → source anywhere in the world (best quality/cost/technology).
- **Agile model** — quickly adapt to changes in the market (**Nike** amends products on customer recommendations); works when communication/collaboration is strong. Required for uncertain demand with everyday renovation/innovation. Four characteristics: **align the process, virtual integration** with stakeholders, a **network**, and **market sensitivity**. Possible only with **jointly managing suppliers** (supplier manages inventory; change product/process design). **Dell** is the custom-configured example (combines agile + continuous flow — buys raw material components from Asian countries, assembles per the customer's configuration).
- **Flexible model** — varying customer demands; turn it on/off; components: **part segmentation, precise algorithms, flexible planning**; **Hewlett Packard** is the best example (senses market conditions and customer preferences frequently).
- **Virtual supply chain model** — the future model; uses **cloud computing, data analysis, AI, IoT**; purpose: greater visibility and communication (efficient, quick, agile, flexible); **Procter & Gamble** shares information quickly and manages minimum inventories.

### 28.5 Best Practices for Supply Chain Modeling
- Understand business needs (different products need different supply chains — steel → continuous/efficient; healthcare emergency → agile/flexible; disaster management → quick/speedy; entertainment → speedy).
- Choose the appropriate modeling approach.
- Validate with accurate data (not one-time — validate the established model repeatedly) and test scenarios (what-if).
- Involve stakeholders (are they ready for the flexible/agile model?).
- Regularly update and refine the model; **document assumptions** (e.g., linear programming's linear-relationship assumption — if forgotten, the model becomes a disaster).
- Train and educate people; track the best model against the defined performance measures.

### 28.6 Challenges in Supply Chain Modeling
- **Complex relationships** — different stakeholders/activities are hard to configure in one model.
- **Data** — models work only with data; need accurate, timely information.
- **Multiple objectives** — one organization competes on responsiveness, another on cost efficiency.
- **Dynamic environment** — everyday changes require model changes.
- **Integration issues** — multiple supply chains/stakeholders cause conflicts (cost efficiency vs. responsiveness vs. centralized inventory).
- **Resource constraints** — limited time, money, machine, method, material.
- **Scenario complexity** — multi-clone networks and **omni-channel** distribution (air tickets through Goibibo, MakeMyTrip, ixigo, IRCTC, IndiGo, Vistara).
- **Risk management / change management** — any change in the PESTLE (political, ethical, legal, technological, social, environmental) dimension disrupts the supply chain; how quickly/efficiently you manage change matters.

### 28.7 Future — Supply Chain Control Towers
- A combination of **machine learning, AI, IoT, and cloud-based solutions**; keeps track of every parameter (warehousing, transportation, inventory, per-unit shipment/manufacturing cost, warehouse capital, capacity utilization).
- It gives you the data; you feed it into your model to get the output (cost, lead time, expected end-time delivery) and choose the best-fit model for your product.

### 28.8 Case Studies
- **Intel** (largest computer-chip manufacturer): chips cost ~**$100** with ~**$5.5 per chip** transportation/supply chain cost; new low-cost **Atom chip** costs ~**$20** but still ~$5.5 supply chain cost. They first scaled down **packaging** (high value-to-weight ratio) and worked on **distribution cost** (could not reduce further), so they focused on **inventory**. Problem: a **9-week order cycle** meant 9–10 weeks of inventory. They went to **make-to-order**, cut the chip assembly/test window from a **5-day to a 2-day weekly schedule**, analyzed sales and operation planning frequently, and asked **vendors to hold inventory** — reducing **$4 per unit** on the $20 Atom chip (with challenges: small production orders → higher production cost, lost economies of scale in transportation).
- **Pharmaceutical industry:** frequent acquisitions/divestments; the distribution network was inefficient/fragmented. Using a **mixed integer linear programming** model (manufacturing sites, distribution centers, demand patterns, aggregated product groups, global and local business levels), they achieved **10% overall cost reduction** and **95% next-day delivery**. Challenges: getting data from different stakeholders, frequent recommendations, human resistance to change, and making trade-offs.
- **Conclusion:** modeling simulates scenarios/outcomes/probabilities and environmental factors, but you must **feed regular data** — obtained via **supply chain control tower** solutions (the virtual world for all physical items: record inventory depletion, quickly replenish damaged products, share customer complaints with the manufacturing process).

## Lecture 29 — Supply Chain Drivers

### 29.1 The Six Drivers
- **Six supply chain drivers:** **facility, inventory, transportation** (logistics drivers) and **information, sourcing, pricing** (cross-functional drivers).
- Whatever you do with any driver affects the two ends: **cost** or **responsiveness**.
- **Transportation example:** shipping by **air** adds responsiveness (quick delivery) but very high cost.
- **Chopra and Meindl classification:** facility, inventory, and transportation are **logistical drivers** (logistic activity — shipping, storing, producing); sourcing, pricing, and information are **cross-functional drivers** (touch different functional units within a company; broader scope).
- **Logistics vs. cross-functional:** logistics drivers work with different stakeholders (facilities, warehouses, transportation) and define efficient/timely delivery; cross-functional drivers encompass all aspects from procurement to the customer end and have more significant impact on **responsiveness and agility**; logistics drivers focus on **efficiency and cost optimization**.

### 29.2 Driver 1 — Facility
- Facilities: **manufacturing facilities** (raw-material suppliers also have manufacturing units) and **warehousing facilities** (temporary storage, **cross docking**, raw-material stores).
- **Role in competitive strategy** (cost-responsiveness continuum): a **centralized facility** reduces responsiveness (longer lead time) but is **cost efficient** (inventory maintained at one point instead of three locations needing facilities/manpower/resources); **facilities near the end market** are more responsive but increase inventory cost.
  - **Toyota and Honda** are more responsive by opening **local manufacturing facilities near markets**: benefits — avoid **currency fluctuation** (if manufacturing in India, don't export from India), avoid **trade barriers**, increase responsiveness (change for the Indian market easily while other models keep running). Honda manufactures **SUVs, sedans, and normal cars in the same manufacturing unit** → high facility utilization.
- **Components of facility decision:**
  - **Role — capacity and flexibility:** too much excess inventory → obsolescence/damage; too little → stock-out. Flexibility = quickly amending the product/component/process design. **Product-focused** (all fan-related assembly, soldering, PCB, packaging in-house) vs. **functional-focused** (a molding plant supplying plastic parts to many fan makers — Havells, Philips).
  - **Cross docking vs. storage:** a small stoppage/consolidation/segregation center (pin buys, location buys, different containers).
  - **Location:** near the customer; labor cost (Europe high, developing nations cheap), operational cost, infrastructure, tax implications.
  - **Capacity:** excess capacity → responsive but costlier; low capacity → efficient but not responsive (stock-out risk).
- **Facility-related metrics:**
  - **Capacity** — max units/day (two shifts, three shifts), expansion feasibility.
  - **Utilization** — e.g., a **10-ton** truck carrying 8/9/10 tons (full utilization lowers per-unit cost); delays increase with utilization (making 100 items to minimize cost causes waiting/delays).
  - **Processing time, setup time (change-over), downtime (breakdown/absenteeism), idle time.**
  - **Production cost per unit** — total cost (material, manpower, equipment depreciation, facility) divided by total units.
  - **Quality losses** — produced 100 but only 95 OK (5 rejected for missing components, safety, etc.).
  - **Theoretical cycle time** — pure perfect-flow time (e.g., **30 seconds** packet-to-packet); **actual average flow time** includes delays; **flow time efficiency** = theoretical/actual (max 1; aim for 0.9/0.95).
  - **Product variety** — number of models (automobile >100 models; color, engine capacity, airbags, petrol/diesel).
  - **Volume contribution of the top 20% (80-20 rule)** — identify the **20% of SKUs** adding **80% of volume**. **SKU = stock keeping unit** (usually **8 alphanumeric digits**; soap changes SKU if size, packaging, flavor, price, or color changes); used to track inventory depletion/replenishment.
  - **Average production batch size** — larger batches reduce transportation cost but increase inventory storage cost.

### 29.3 Driver 2 — Inventory
- **Why keep inventory:** to manage the balance between supply and demand (uncertain production/consumption), exploiting **economies of scale** (bulk production reduces production cost; bulk transport reduces transportation/distribution cost — combining **10 pizzas** from McD to IIT Kharagpur campus).
- **Enhancing responsiveness** — extra inventory meets demand quickly; minimum inventory means recording the order (**Dell** customized product takes 1–2 days vs. buying a standardized product immediately).
- **Impact on cost/profitability** — handling cost, idle inventory (no money until consumed), lower profit margin.
- **Material flow time** — time from entry to exit; **Little's Law: I = D × T** (I = inventory, T = flow time, D = throughput rate — the rate at which sales to end customers happen).
- **Customer demand and throughput rate** — align inventory levels with customer demand.
- **Role in competitive strategy:**
  - Locating inventory: large quantity of finished goods near the customer → high responsiveness/high cost; centralized inventory → lower cost (**Amazon** has different hubs — an order from Kharagpur comes via the Calcutta hub, then to other hubs).
  - **Amazon book example:** **best-selling books** placed at regional warehouses (quick delivery, high responsiveness); **slow-moving** books stocked centrally (lower inventory cost); **very slow-moving** books ordered only when an order is received (replenish from publisher/distributor).
- **Components of inventory decisions:**
  - **Product availability (fill rate)** — how many times demand is met from existing inventory; trade-off with stock-out (reduce inventory 5% → how much stock-out; are you ready for 0.5% stock-out or keep 5% extra?).
  - **Cycle inventory** — inventory to meet the usual demand cycle between two orders (depends on average regional demand, order quantity, lead time).
  - **Safety inventory** — minimum level maintained for unpredictable demand.
  - **Seasonal inventory** — for a particular season (summer/winter).
- **Inventory performance measures/practices:**
  - **Cash conversion cycle** (cash-to-cash) — how quickly you consume inventory and receive receivables.
  - **Average inventory** = (starting + ending inventory)/2; can be expressed in **units, days of stock** (10 days stock), or **financial value** (₹10 crore inventory/day).
  - **Seasonal inventory.**
  - **Inventory turnover** = cost of goods sold / average inventory (higher = efficient stock rotation).
  - **Days of inventory outstanding** = average inventory / (cost of goods sold per day); higher = longer time to sell (not generating enough demand → stop producing excess).
  - **Average replenishment batch size** — units placed per order (larger batch helps negotiate price).
  - **Average safety inventory** — units or days.
  - **Fill rate** — fraction of demand met from existing inventory (visited 100 times, met 98 → **98% fill rate, 2% stock-out**).
  - **Obsolete inventory.**
- **Overall trade-off:** inventory near the customer → more responsive; inventory away → more lead time but saves cost.

### 29.4 Driver 3 — Transportation
- **Faster mode** reduces lead time, increases cost, and increases responsiveness (train cheaper than air; **ship** is the least-cost option but infrastructure-dependent and cannot do end-mile delivery).
- **Influence on inventory and facility location:** to utilize full container capacity you need large warehouses to hold that size of inventory; frequent full-container shipping affects facility-location decisions.
- **Faster transportation** can reduce inventory levels and enhance responsiveness — **Dell flies components from Asia** for customized products (higher cost compensated by not keeping extra inventory).
- **Role in competitive strategy:**
  - Balancing responsiveness and efficiency — competing on speed → air (fastest); competing on efficiency → cycle/electric vehicle (lower cost).
  - **Strategic adaptation** — high-value items need responsiveness but can't be stored near the customer because value is high: **Blue Nile** (online diamond store) ensures **overnight delivery using FedEx**, centralizing diamond inventory (reduces inventory cost and **security** cost — manage security at one point instead of five).
- **Components of transportation decision:**
  - Design of the transportation network (mode, location, route; **routing algorithm** for the optimal route, minimize cost).
  - Multiple supply/demand points — consolidate orders or go for single delivery (30-minute pizza delivery).
  - Choice of transportation mode — considering **speed, shipment size, cost, and flexibility**.
- **Transportation-related metrics:**
  - **Average inbound transportation cost** — always **higher** than outbound (that's why the manufacturing/assembly facility should be near the market); usually expressed as a **percentage of cost of goods sold**.
  - **Average incoming shipment size** — small vs. large lots (helps define inventory practices and negotiation).
  - **Average inbound transportation cost per shipment** — total cost / shipment size (10,000 units → ₹10,000 → ₹1/unit; 11,000 units → ₹0.91/unit).
  - **Average outbound transportation cost** — final product from the manufacturing unit to the customer; measured as a **percentage of sales**; it is higher than inbound.
  - **Average outbound shipment size.**
  - **Average outbound transportation cost per shipment** — percentage in total sales.
  - **Fraction transported by mode** — e.g., 5% ship, 20% train, 50% road, rest air (the major portion by air should be streamlined to a more cost-efficient mode).
- **Overall trade-off:** economies of scale/consolidation → efficiency; individually processing orders/fast delivery (air, drone) → responsiveness.

## Lecture 30 — Supply Chain Drivers (Contd.)

### 30.1 Driver 4 — Information (Cross-Functional)
- Affects **all other drivers**: facility location (if a market gives bulk orders, locate near it), logistics activities (ship quantity, container capacity), sourcing (vendor info: capacity, capabilities, skills, quality, quantity, coverage, industrial relations), pricing.
- **Information and pricing:** excess inventory → seasonal offers/concessional rates to consume it; stock-out/supply interruption/sudden demand rise → raise prices for **rationing**.
- **Responsiveness vs. efficiency:** responsiveness = quickly responding to customer requests (variety, quality, price, quantity) via quick information sharing; efficiency = **warehouse capacity maximization** (exact inventory, stock rotation rate), transportation (units per shipment).
- **Role of information in the supply chain:**
  - **Asset utilization** — equipment utilization, warehouse utilization, optimum vehicle route (real-time data → real-time decisions).
  - **Coordination of supply chain flows** — material, finance, and information flows; requires a coordinated/integrated supply chain.
  - **Increasing responsiveness** — transparent supply chain with visibility of outside fluctuations (competitor strategy changes, extra services).
  - **Reducing cost** — best route (traffic, road, weather), full container utilization, minimizing idle inventory holding cost, avoiding stock-outs, enabling **JIT**.
  - **7-Eleven/Walmart** invested in information channels to ensure product availability (no stock-out), tap demand-defining parameters; **cross docking** (consolidating large container orders and segregating **pin-code wise**) is purely a function of the information tool.
  - **Optimizing sourcing decisions** — pick the best vendor by price, quality, and flexibility (ready to ship 50 or 500 units).
- **Role in competitive strategy:**
  - **Customer-centric approach** — record customer requirements (product, features, quality, variety, color, after-sales) and provide tailored services.
  - **Investment in information technology** — online players/**Amazon/Flipkart**/Walmart/7-Eleven need a strong IT network for **visibility of all transactions** (where the product is, reached hub, out for delivery, received, return processed, credit back to customer); use analytical tools for more information-based decisions.
  - **Coordinating for a common goal** — communicate the low-cost product category to raw-material suppliers (be efficient in batch size, material, technology). Responsiveness example: **Swiggy** claims to deliver even a **single piece of sweet**, but if the courier partner refuses (quantity too small), coordination fails.
  - **Optimizing information sharing** — too much information creates complexity; spending **5% extra** on information and getting only **1% margin** → make the trade-off.
- **Case study — Sun Sweet Growers (California):** deals with the **dry fruit** market (seasonal — harvested **September–October**, demand peaks at **Christmas**). It implemented **SOP (sales and operation planning)** with sales/marketing teams and **early-warning capability systems** to flag fluctuations (hike or drop) beyond routine seasonality. Results: production overruns reduced from **30% to 15%** (less inventory processing cost and fewer seasonal offers at 30–50% lower price); forecasting improved **15% to 20%**.

### 30.2 Components of Information Decision
- **Push vs. pull** — push (forecast and push inventory) vs. pull (record the customer order then process); both need an information system; set the **push-pull boundary** (e.g., push up to procurement/manufacturing, then pull in replenishment/customer order cycle).
- **Coordination and information sharing** — lack of coordination leads to inefficiencies and **loss in supply chain surplus** (no correct information sharing → suppliers produce excess inventory).
- **Sales and operation planning** — connect sales with operations; implement only what the market requires.
- **Enabling technologies** — **ERP, MRP, advanced analytics, cloud-based platforms**; automated warehouses (robots manage stock rotation, no inventory becomes obsolete; ERP identifies idle resources that can be outsourced or rented).

### 30.3 Information-Related Metrics
- **Forecast reason/horizon** — the forecast period must exceed the lead time (if you forecast demand 50 for tomorrow but the lead time is **3 days**, the forecast serves no purpose; the horizon should be 3–5 or 5–7 days).
- **Frequency of update** — how frequently the forecast is updated (flag changes, take corrective action — forecast 50 but revise to 30–35).
- **Forecast error** — the difference between predicted and actual demand; measured using qualitative and quantitative techniques.
- **Seasonal factors** — seasonal demand spikes (dry foods, **AC/fan/cooler** in summer).
- **Variance from the plan** — difference between planned production/inventory and actual (breakdown, raw-material interruption, absenteeism).
- **Ratio of demand variability to order variability** — the **bullwhip effect** (demand 50 → retailer orders 75 → manufacturer 100 → raw-material supplier 110); if the ratio is **less than 1**, you are not predicting accurately and the bullwhip exists.
- **Trade-off** — information helps both efficiency and responsiveness; risk can be better handled with prior information.
- **Complexity and cost** — complex market structure/product/customers/channels create complex supply chains.
- **Marginal value** — whether spending 5% extra on information returns 10% (or only 1%).
- **Crucial to assess minimum necessary information** — collect at least the point-of-sale/point-of-billing data; always trade off complexity vs. value.

### 30.4 Driver 5 — Sourcing
- **First decision:** in-house vs. **outsource** (to a third party); usually efficiency → outsource, responsiveness → in-house (not absolute).
  - **Swiggy example:** delivering a single piece of sweet requires your **own delivery network** (a third-party partner demanding a minimum order kills responsiveness).
  - **Efficiency:** with only 50–100 orders, you cannot acquire resources; outsource to a courier partner (pay per order — 50 today, 500 tomorrow → efficient).
  - **Responsiveness via a courier:** **DHL** as a partner gives quick service and tracking → outsourcing can add to responsiveness.
- **Low-cost country:** **IKEA** manufactures furniture in low-cost sources; **Dell** sources from **China** (cost) while maintaining responsiveness via **in-house production** and direct customer interaction.
- **Globalization** gives opportunities to customers and manufacturers.
- **Role in competitive strategy:** outsourcing to expert courier partners adds responsiveness; use **break-even analysis** to decide outsource vs. in-house (fixed + variable cost vs. revenue/profit; produce in-house only if demand exceeds quantity **Q**).
  - **Zara** — in-house (Europe) and outsourced (Turkey, Bangladesh, Pakistan, Asian countries) — outsourcing for higher lead time, cost minimization, and standardized products, in-house for quick pattern/fashion changes.
  - **Cisco** — efficiency supply chain for **low-end** products (home-network routers packed/bulk-ordered in China → shipped to the US) and responsiveness for **high-value high-end** products (outsourced to contract manufacturers in the US, near the end market).
- **Components of sourcing decision:** in-house vs. outsource (break-even analysis); supplier selection (capabilities, skills, cost/price, quality, experience, quantity, lots, delivery schedules); procurement (how many lots per month/week, load quantity, product segments).
- **Sourcing-related metrics:**
  - **Days payable outstanding** — time from delivery to getting paid.
  - **Average purchase price** — across lots of different quantities (did you negotiate a better price for large lots?).
  - **Supply quality** — sampling checks on incoming raw material/finished goods.
  - **Supply lead time** — a long lead time means the supplier is not responsive (may be working on efficiency, and vice versa).
  - **Fraction of time on-time delivery** — e.g., of 10 orders, how many were delivered on time (within 24 hours if promised).
  - **Range of purchase price** — relationship between quantity and price (higher quantity → reduced price?).
  - **Supplier reliability** — variability in lead time, quality, and quantity (ordered 50,000, supplied only 30,000 due to capacity/breakdown issues).
- **Overall trade-off** — maximize total **supply chain surplus**; sourcing reduces cost; the basic purpose is low-cost supplies and expert services.

### 30.5 Driver 6 — Pricing
- Affects all other things: lowering price → demand increases → suppliers supply more, manufacturing processes more, the distribution channel feels the burden (**big billion days**/seasonal offers → **30–35%** demand hike); when struggling for raw material, raise price so customers buy less (rationing); **panic buying** (customers buy in quantity though actual demand isn't there — they won't return for 4 months).
- **Role in determining revenue** — pricing strategy (marginal, psychological, competitive pricing) defines revenue; the tool to match demand and supply (excess demand → increase price; excess supply → decrease price so people buy in bulk).
- **Impact on demand** — lower price → higher demand.
- **Strategic positioning** — low price fetches customers on pricing (though customers may suspect compromised quality); **high-status products** don't compete on cost; routine **FMCG** items compete on pricing.
- **Segmentation and targeting** — **P&G** offers multiple products in detergent/soap (very low cost, medium, top market), with compromises on product quality.
- **Balancing efficiency and responsiveness** — efficiency/lower price requires cost effectiveness; responsiveness/innovation (latest services) can charge high.
- **Customer differentiation** — **Amazon** case: 2 books at **$30** — **standard shipping** (3–5 days, ~**$5**), **2-day shipping** (~**$13–14**), **1-day shipping** (~**$22+**), **routine delivery** (15 days, free shipping). Extra/quick services cost more but put a high degree of uncertainty on Amazon's supply chain (anytime a same-day order can come).
- **Components of pricing decision:**
  - **Economies of scale** — lower cost → lower price.
  - **Everyday low prices vs. high-low pricing** — everyday low pricing (Walmart) suits a saturated market; high-low pricing creates sudden demand spikes.
  - **Fixed price vs. menu price** — **Amazon** charges a fixed price for standard delivery; menu pricing for same-day/2-day options.
- **Pricing-related metrics:**
  - **Profit margin** — gross margin (only cost of production) vs. net margin (after operational cost).
  - **Scope** — SKU, product line, division, entire; find the **20% of customers/products** contributing **80% of profit** (80-20 rule). **Maruti Suzuki** — of 30 models, the top 20% contribute 80% of profit (some run at a loss); **credit-card industry** — customers who always pay on time and never take loans are a **cost** to the company (extra card/service cost, no revenue).
  - **Days sales outstanding** — time from delivery to getting the money back.
  - **Incremental fixed cost per order** and **incremental variable cost per unit** — extra fixed cost (machine/infrastructure) vs. variable cost (10 cars → 50 tyres, 20 cars → 100 tyres).
  - **Average sale price** — same product sold at MRP or at 50% lower for excess inventory.
  - **Average order size** — helps design economies of scale.
  - **Range of sale price.**
  - **Range of periodic sales** — highest/lowest sales quantity day/week/month wise (relationship between price change and sales).
- **Trade-off:** everyday low pricing → stable patterns; pricing fluctuations → demand fluctuations → extra pressure on the distribution network.

### 30.6 Conclusion
- Handle all **six drivers** — three **logistics** drivers (facility, transportation, inventory) and three **cross-functional** drivers (sourcing, information, pricing).
- Varying these drivers on a 1–10 scale impacts responsiveness/efficiency depending on the market and customer requirements; manipulate the drivers to get the desired performance.
- **Strategic fit** (discussed in future sessions) — develop the strategy and fix the drivers based on the chosen strategy.
